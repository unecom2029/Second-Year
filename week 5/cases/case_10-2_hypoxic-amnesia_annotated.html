Case Study 10-2

A 55-year-old man with profound memory loss following cardiovascular surgery

Chief Complaint

A profound memory loss following cardiovascular surgery1

History of Chief Complaint

This is a 55-year-old, male government worker retired because of medical disability. During coronary surgery he suffered several extended periods of anoxia and has experienced severe neurologic consequences. He is presenting for a checkup, 4 years postsurgery.

Past Medical History

Five years ago, this patient was diagnosed with coronary artery occlusion, and bypass surgery was performed. During recovery, an atrial tear resulted in a significant loss of blood2. The patient experienced a 15-minute period of hypoxia3. The tear was repaired; however, during this second trip to the operating room, the patient's EEG was flat and his pupils were fixed4. The following day, further bleeding required a third trip to the operating room; again his pupils were fixed and the EEG was diminished in amplitude5.

Over the next 2 days the patient gradually regained consciousness. He had reduced strength and paresthesia in the left arm6. He also demonstrated severe memory loss and confusion with respect to time and place7. Prior to the surgical event, he had no history of neurologic signs or symptoms.

General Physical Examination

This was a well-nourished, well-hydrated, obese male who was alert and cooperative and in no acute distress. Carotid auscultation revealed a soft bruit without radiation on the right8. Heartbeat was regular; a grade III harsh systolic murmur, auscultated best at the second intercostal space on the right, with an S4 gallop was present9. Peripheral pulses were intact. Lungs were clear to auscultation and percussion. The abdomen was soft, without masses, tenderness, rigidity, or rebound. A +2 peripheral, dependent edema was noted in the lower extremities bilaterally10. Skin was sallow in appearance, with poor texture and turgor. A well-healed cicatrix of the anterior chest wall extended from the second intercostal space to the diaphragmatic area; a healed 2-cm cicatrix was noted in the left antecubital fossa; and a healed 1-cm cicatrix was located in the left subclavicular area.

Neurologic Examination

Mental Status. The patient was disoriented with respect to time and place and could not describe the reason for his past hospital confinement or recall the history of his illness. However, his speech, naming, reading aloud, and comprehension were all normal for his age12. He rapidly forgot information recently expressed to him; he could not recognize any words presented to him 5 minutes previously13. He could not recall the names of staff or physicians in either the office or the hospital. Prior to his illness, he had had a strong interest in American history and politics. During the examination he could recall most of the president's names and supply some details concerning their era, but he could not identify the current president14 or describe any recent historical events. He frequently relied on his family members to provide any recent descriptions of his life. He did not recall why he was ill or any of the events occurring around the time of his cardiac surgery. No other significant personality changes or cognitive deficits were detectable15 in the patient.

Cranial Nerves. His visual fields were full to confrontation; fundoscopic examination revealed AV nicking and silver wire changes11 without exudates, hemorrhages, or papilledema. His hearing was slightly diminished in both ears16. A full range of facial expression was present, and the jaw-jerk reflex was normal. Palate and uvula elevated symmetrically, and the tongue protruded on the midline.

Motor Systems. Although the patient had motion in all extremities, strength was diminished on the left side, with slightly elevated deep tendon reflexes and a Babinski sign18. Strength in the left upper extremity was +3/5 and +4/5 in the left lower extremity17.

Sensation. Vibratory sense, proprioception, and discriminative touch were diminished but not absent in the left and normal in the right extremities19. Paresthesias (tingling sensations) were found involving the patient's left hand and forearm20.

Synthesis

Clinical Reasoning Walkthrough

01 · Localization

Two circuits, not one

This case has a dual localization. The amnesia is bilateral and circuit-wide — a lesion of the limbic (hippocampal–diencephalic) memory circuit — since language, personality, and general cognition are preserved and there's no left-vs-right asymmetry described in the memory deficit itself.

The left-sided weakness, hyperreflexia, Babinski sign, and sensory loss are a separate, lateralized upper motor neuron finding, localizing to the right cerebral hemisphere (corticospinal tract and dorsal column-medial lemniscus projections), not to a peripheral nerve or segmental level.

02 · Distribution

Multifocal, selectively vulnerable

Not diffuse — cranial nerves, language, and right-sided motor/sensory function are spared. Not focal — two separate territories are involved (bilateral hippocampal CA1 plus a right-hemisphere region).

This patchy, "select structures only" pattern is the signature of global hypoperfusion: the tissue injured is whatever has the highest metabolic demand or the thinnest collateral supply, not whatever sits in one vessel's territory.

03 · Time Course

Acute insult, chronic stable deficit

Two discrete intraoperative events 5 years ago (documented flat EEG/fixed pupils, then diminished-amplitude EEG) produced an acute injury, followed by a 2-day gradual return of consciousness.

The exam 4 years later is unchanged from what would be expected at recovery — this is a fixed, static deficit, not a progressive one, which argues strongly against a neurodegenerative process and supports a one-time ischemic/anoxic mechanism.

04 · Differential Diagnosis

Working differential

DiagnosisFitsArgues against
Most likely
Global hypoxic-ischemic injury: bilateral hippocampal (CA1) damage + right ACA–MCA watershed infarct		 Two documented intraoperative hypotensive/hypoxic events; classic anterograde amnesia with a temporally graded retrograde component; arm-predominant left weakness with UMN signs matches a watershed distribution; stable 4-year course. Requires accepting two co-occurring lesions from one systemic event rather than a single unifying vascular territory — but this is exactly what global hypoperfusion produces.
Bilateral paramedian thalamic infarction (e.g., artery of Percheron) Can produce a severe amnestic syndrome with preserved language. Classically causes decreased arousal/vertical gaze palsy at onset, which isn't described; doesn't explain the lateralized left-sided UMN signs.
Cardioembolic right MCA territory stroke Would explain the left hemiparesis and hyperreflexia/Babinski. Doesn't account for the bilateral, temporally graded memory loss, which is the dominant and defining feature here.
Chronic hypertensive small-vessel (lacunar) disease AV nicking/silver-wire changes confirm real, longstanding hypertension as a contributing risk factor. Lacunes don't explain an abrupt, perioperative-onset amnestic syndrome this severe; this is a contributing comorbidity, not the primary mechanism.
Neurodegenerative dementia (e.g., early Alzheimer's disease) Anterograde amnesia is a hallmark early feature. Onset is sharply time-locked to a specific perioperative event, not insidious; course is static over 4 years rather than progressive.
05 · Diagnostic Workup

Next steps

1
MRI brain with DWI/FLAIR
Look for chronic T2/FLAIR signal change in the bilateral hippocampi (CA1 subfield) and in the right ACA–MCA borderzone; also excludes a mass or hemorrhage as an alternative explanation.
2
Formal neuropsychological / memory battery
Quantify the anterograde deficit and map the retrograde gradient (e.g., Wechsler Memory Scale) to confirm the Ribot-type pattern already suggested by the presidents question.
3
Echocardiogram ± carotid duplex
Characterize the valvular disease behind the murmur/S4 gallop and screen for a thrombus or embolic source; carotid duplex quantifies the stenosis behind the soft right-sided bruit.
4
Outpatient EEG
Assess for residual diffuse slowing or epileptiform activity, given that both intraoperative EEGs were markedly abnormal (flat, then low-amplitude).
5
Ophthalmologic exam
Formally grade the hypertensive retinopathy (AV nicking, silver wire) to stage chronic vascular risk burden and guide blood pressure targets.
06 · Prognosis

Fixed deficit, ongoing risk

Four years out, the anterograde amnesia is almost certainly permanent — bilateral CA1 loss doesn't regenerate. Some further shrinkage of the retrograde gradient toward normal is possible but unlikely to resolve fully. The motor/sensory deficit has already plateaued.

The patient's ongoing cardiac disease (murmur, S4, edema) and hypertension mean he remains at real risk for a second hypoperfusion event, which is arguably the more urgent forward-looking concern.

07 · Management

Checklist

  • Cardiology follow-up to optimize the valvular disease and heart failure signs driving hemodynamic instability
  • Blood pressure control to slow further hypertensive small-vessel injury
  • Carotid duplex surveillance given the bruit, to determine if stenosis warrants intervention
  • Neuropsychological rehabilitation — external memory aids and structured routines, since the underlying tissue loss isn't reversible
  • PT/OT for the residual left arm/leg weakness and ADL adaptation
  • Safety and caregiver planning, given his reliance on family for recent-event recall
Underlying Neuroanatomy

Concept Deep-Dive

localization

The Hippocampal–Diencephalic Memory Circuit (Papez Circuit)

This patient's lesion sits at the first node. Losing the hippocampus itself breaks the whole loop's ability to encode new memories, even though every downstream station (fornix, mammillary bodies, thalamus, cingulate) remains structurally intact — which is why his deficit is purely mnemonic rather than a broader limbic/behavioral syndrome.

pattern

CA1 ("Sommer Sector"): Selectively Vulnerable to Anoxia

SubfieldVulnerability to anoxia/ischemia
CA1 (Sommer sector)Highest — first and most severely damaged; also the site of neurofibrillary tangle accumulation in Alzheimer's disease
CA2Relatively resistant
CA3More resistant than CA1
Dentate gyrus (CA4 border)Relatively resistant

A brief, global hypoxic episode doesn't damage the hippocampus uniformly — it targets CA1 specifically. That's exactly the kind of selective, patchy injury a 15-minute hypoxic period plus two intraoperative hypotensive events would be expected to produce, well before it damages more resistant cortex.

pattern

Anterograde Amnesia + Temporally-Graded Retrograde Loss

This is Ribot's Law in action: older memories are more consolidated and less hippocampus-dependent for retrieval, so they survive; anything recent — including everything formed after the injury — is lost. His ability to name past presidents but not the current one is the single cleanest bedside sign of this gradient.

localization

Global Hypoperfusion & Watershed (Borderzone) Infarction

During systemic hypotension, the territories furthest from all three major arteries — the borderzones — receive the least perfusion pressure. The anterior (ACA–MCA) watershed maps onto the shoulder/proximal-arm motor cortex, which is exactly why this patient's weakness is arm-predominant (3/5) more than leg (4/5), rather than the equal arm/leg weakness of an internal capsule lesion.

workup / vascular

Vascular Supply of the Hippocampus & Amygdala

Both the anterior choroidal artery and the posterior cerebral artery send small penetrating branches into the hippocampus and amygdala — these are relatively distal, thin-caliber vessels with limited collateral reserve. That makes the medial temporal lobe a second point of vulnerability during global hypoperfusion, on top of CA1's intrinsic metabolic fragility.

term

AV Nicking & Silver-Wire Arterioles

AV nicking = a thickened, hypertensive arteriole compressing the vein it crosses. Silver-wire arterioles = arteriolar wall thickening that increases the light reflex to a metallic sheen. Neither finding is acute — both are markers of the patient's chronic hypertension, a standing risk factor separate from (but compounding) the acute perioperative injury that caused his amnesia.
