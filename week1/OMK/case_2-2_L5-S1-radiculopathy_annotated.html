Chief Complaint

This is a 26-year-old student who is complaining of sharp, burning pain in his right leg with weakness and difficulty walking.

History of Chief Complaint

The young man had enjoyed his usual good health until last Sunday afternoon when he was playing intramural football. During a vigorous play he experienced the sharp burning pain down the inside of his right leg. He describes the pain as feeling like burning gasoline being poured down the inside of his leg and foot. He denies hearing or feeling any popping or cracking associated with the onset of the pain. He stopped playing football and noticed a mild weakness in the leg as well. Although he reports that the burning pain has subsided somewhat since then, the weakness has persisted and he feels that it may even be getting worse.

Family History

Both mother and father are alive and well. He has a sister who is 23 and is well also.

Allergies

NKA

Review of Systems

Due to previous good health and the specificity of the Chief Complaint, the ROS will be focused on the back and extremities. He denies tics or adventitious movements but admits to weakness in his right lower extremity. He denies painful or swollen joints but admits to a dull pain in the center of his lower lumbar spine. He admits that it is painful to stand on the right leg and attempts to unweight the extremity when ever possible. He complains that the pain is made worse by sitting, walking or standing for long periods of time and by coughing or sneezing.

Physical Examination

This is an alert and cooperative 26-year-old well nourished male. He is standing and leaning to the left and elevating the right leg. He admits to feeling uncomfortable at this time. Blood pressure is 140/95, heart rate is 75 b/m, respirations are 17 and temperature is 98.6°F. The remainder of the exam will focus on his back and extremities. Extremities are four in number and without clubbing or cyanosis. Full range of motion is intact in the left lower extremity but the patient has restricted movement of the right especially in the sagittal plane. There is no tenderness to percussion on the spinous processes of L1 through L5. Straight leg raising on the right leg is painful at 20 degrees, and right sided pain is reproduced with left leg rising at 45 degrees. He walks with an antalgic gait, favoring the right leg.

Neurologic Exam

Mental Status. He is awake and oriented to person, place and time. Fund of knowledge and memory appears intact. Speech is clear.

Cranial Nerves. All cranial nerves 1–12 are intact.

Motor Exam. Strength, tendon reflexes and range of motion in the upper extremities and the left lower extremity were intact. In the right lower extremity, strength in flexion and extension was 5/5 at the knee with a patellar reflex of 2/4. Strength for plantar flexion was 1/5 and for eversion was 2/5 in the right foot, normal in the left ankle. The Achilles tendon reflex was 1/4 to trace in the right. Dorsiflexion of the great toe was 4/5 and plantarflexion of the right ankle was 1/5. He could walk on his heels but not on his toes. Minor's sign was present upon rising from a seated position and the patient complained of pain in the right leg at this time.

Sensory Exam. All systems were intact throughout the head and body with the exception of the lower extremity on the right. Reduced sensation to touch and pin-prick was present on the posterio-lateral aspect of the calf, lateral aspect of the ankle and lateral aspect of the foot including the fifth digit. Patient complained of an achy feeling in the lateral calf muscle extending to the ankle. His gait was markedly antalgic, favoring the right side and he could not walk on his toes due to his right weakness.

Clinical Reasoning Walkthrough

Condensed from the small-group discussion + the OpenEvidence breakdown of the 11 standard localization questions. Read top to bottom: where it is → what it is → how you'd prove it → what happens next.

01 · Localization

Segmental, not suprasegmental

Intact mentation, cranial nerves, and a normal exam everywhere except the right S1/L5 distribution rule out cortex, brainstem, and diffuse CNS disease. Severe focal weakness (1/5 plantarflexion) with hyporeflexia (Achilles 1/4–trace) and no UMN signs (no hyperreflexia, spasticity, Babinski) is a segmental, lower motor neuron pattern — a nerve root.

02 · Distribution

Focal, unilateral, single root level

Every finding — weakness, sensory loss, reflex change, pain — sits in one distribution (predominantly S1, minor L5) on one side. The left leg and both upper extremities are entirely normal. One structural lesion, one level — not diffuse, not multifocal.

03 · Time course

Acute onset, watchfully "stable"

Sudden onset during vigorous football — a discrete event, not a gradual one. The burning pain has eased, but the weakness has persisted and may be worsening. Small-group settled on "stable" as the temporal category (it's not fluctuating), but a severe motor deficit that hasn't clearly started improving after an acute injury is exactly the detail that keeps this from being routine watchful waiting.

04 · Differential diagnosis

05 · Diagnostic workup

How you'd actually prove it

1
MRI lumbar spine, without contrast. Gold standard.
Obtain urgently rather than the usual 4–6 week watch-and-wait — progressive motor weakness is a recognized indication for early imaging
2
Screen for cauda equina before assuming routine radiculopathy.
Post-void residual bladder volume, saddle sensation, anal tone
3
EMG/NCS if diagnosis stays ambiguous after MRI.
Denervation changes may not appear for 2–3 weeks post-injury — timing the study matters
06 · Prognosis

Good overall — this deficit is the outlier

Lumbar radiculopathy is generally favorable: about a third resolve by 2 weeks and three-quarters by 3 months conservatively, and 90–95% of EMG-confirmed cases do well with aggressive conservative care. This patient's severe deficit (1/5 plantarflexion) is the concerning exception — unaddressed, ongoing compression risks permanent S1 weakness, gastroc/soleus atrophy, chronic neuropathic pain, and progression to cauda equina if the disc herniates further centrally.

07 · Management

Urgent workup, then stepwise care

  • Urgent MRI + spine/neurosurg consult (deficit severity)
  • Cauda equina red-flag screen at every visit
  • NSAIDs first-line; short oral steroid course sometimes used
  • Activity modification — avoid prolonged sitting, Valsalva
  • PT once tolerated: stabilization, lumbar extensor stretching, core work
  • Epidural steroid injection if conservative care stalls
  • Microdiscectomy strongly considered given progressive, severe weakness — surgery speeds pain relief/functional recovery vs. conservative care alone
08 · vs. Case 2-1

Same complaint category, opposite temporal profile

Both cases: young men, unilateral-to-bilateral weakness, diminished DTRs, a segmental/LMN localization. Small-group's own contrast: Case 2-1 was insidious, chronically progressive, bilateral, and hereditary — Case 2-2 is acute, post-traumatic, focal/unilateral, and structural. Same exam vocabulary (weakness, hyporeflexia, sensory loss), completely different diseases — the time course and distribution are doing almost all of the differentiating work.

Concept Deep-Dives

The underlying neuroanatomy this case is built on — pulled from small-group, worth knowing cold.

Provocative testing

Straight leg raise & crossed SLR — what the tension test proves

Raising the leg tensions the sciatic nerve and its roots via the dura; if a root is already inflamed by a herniated disc, that added tension reproduces the radicular pain. Ipsilateral SLR is sensitive (~80–90%) but not very specific. Crossed SLR — raising the unaffected leg reproduces pain on the affected side — is much less sensitive but roughly 90% specific for disc herniation: it takes a lot of dural tension transmitted across the midline to bother an already-irritated root, so a positive crossed test is hard to explain any other way.

"Can't walk on your toes" localizes to S1. "Can't walk on your heels" localizes to L5. This patient can heel walk but not toe walk — S1 is doing almost all of the work here, with only mild L5 overlap, consistent with an L5–S1 disc affecting the traversing S1 root more than the exiting L5 root.

Reference · dermatome map
Pathophysiology

Three ways a nerve root gets compressed

1 · Disc herniation

The nucleus pulposus bulges/extrudes posterolaterally — right where the DRG and spinal nerve sit — usually from disc dehydration/degeneration over time, occasionally from an acute load like this patient's football injury.

2 · Facet joint arthropathy

Osteoarthritic overgrowth/osteophyte at the facet joint narrows the foramen the root exits through — a slower, degenerative mechanism, more common with age.

3 · Ligamentum flavum hypertrophy

The ligamentum flavum is unusually elastic and stays taut in the upright, tensioned position. As disc height drops, it goes slack — like a rubber band losing tension — and buckles inward into the space right behind the DRG/spinal nerve, causing stenosis.

MRI · normal vs. bulging vs. herniated disc
Vertebra anatomy reference
All three, in one cross-section

All three funnel down to the same clinical picture (radiculopathy), which is why imaging — not just the exam — is what tells you which mechanism is actually responsible. This cross-section shows all three at once: herniated disc anteriorly, bone spurs at the facet joint posterolaterally, and thickened ligamentum flavum posteriorly — a reminder that these often coexist rather than acting alone, especially in older or degenerative spines.

Level of injury

Root vs. plexus vs. peripheral nerve

Pattern
RootDermatomal sensory loss; myotomal weakness (partial — most muscles get more than one root)
PlexusBroader, multi-root pattern; the plexus mixes roots specifically so no single muscle depends on only one
Peripheral nerveTotal loss of everything that one nerve supplies — sensory and motor together, sharply bounded by that nerve's own territory

Small-group's own example: damage C5–C6 alone and you lose a little shoulder abduction/elbow flexion — partial, because other roots contribute too. Damage the median nerve and you lose everything it supplies, totally — because a peripheral nerve carries only what it carries, no mixing. This case's pattern (one dermatome, one myotome, sparing everything else) is root-level, not a peripheral nerve lesion.

Reflex physiology

Two spinal reflexes — and which one a tendon tap actually tests

The Ia stretch reflex — this is the one a tendon tap actually tests (e.g. patellar, Achilles).

The Golgi tendon organ (Ib) reflex — autogenic inhibition. Included as the contrast case.

What this diagram shows (Ib, Golgi tendon organ): rising tension in a tendon fires a Type 1b afferent, which synapses on an inhibitory interneuron (silencing the agonist's α-motor neuron) and an excitatory interneuron (facilitating the antagonist) — a protective, disynaptic circuit that prevents a muscle from contracting against dangerously high tension.

What a patellar/Achilles reflex actually tests (Ia, muscle spindle): a tendon tap stretches the muscle spindle, firing a Type Ia afferent that synapses directly, monosynaptically, onto the α-motor neuron of that same muscle — no interneuron in between for the excitatory limb. That's why it's fast, and why it's the reflex tested at the knee (patellar, L3/L4) and ankle (Achilles, S1) in this case.

Either way, the principle that matters clinically is the same: interrupt the arc anywhere along the root or peripheral nerve and the reflex is diminished or gone — which is exactly why a 1/4-to-trace Achilles reflex localizes just as precisely as the strength deficit does. (The opposite pattern — hyperreflexia and clonus — shows up when descending cortical input is lost instead: the UMN picture this patient does not have.)

Clinical gestalt

Minor's sign and the pain-avoidance picture

Three separate findings in this case are the same behavior in three settings: leaning left / elevating the right leg while standing (unweighting at rest), an antalgic gait favoring the right leg (unweighting while moving), and a positive Minor's sign — pushing up off the chair/thighs with the arms instead of using the legs (unweighting while rising). All three are the body minimizing load and tension on an already-irritated S1 root. Recognizing them as one pattern — rather than three separate findings to memorize — is what makes the exam read quickly at the bedside.
