LO 1 — Diagnostic Classification

Framework

Shared Diagnostic Framework

Anorexia Nervosa, Bulimia Nervosa, and Binge Eating Disorder are all built from combinations of the same three symptom clusters. Learning these three first makes every individual diagnosis below a matter of "which combination, at what BMI, for how long."

1

Binge Eating Behaviors

Eating a larger amount of food than most people would in a 2-hour period, plus a subjective feeling of loss of control. Culturally-normalized overeating (e.g., a holiday meal) does not count.

2

Calorie Deficit Behaviors

Fasting, restricting intake, or excessive exercise used to create an energy deficit — outside of what's culturally, religiously, or athletically normalized.

3

Purging Behaviors

Self-induced vomiting, laxative/enema misuse, diuretic misuse, or other medications used specifically to offset intake.

🔑 What Counts as "Abnormal"?

Each cluster is only pathological when it falls outside societal, religious, or performance-related norms. An Olympic-level athlete or bodybuilder eating or restricting to match their training load is not, by itself, evidence of an eating disorder — context matters as much as the behavior itself.

Substances Commonly Misused for Purging

StimulantsAmphetamines (e.g., Ritalin, Adderall), pseudoephedrine, caffeine — weight loss is a known side effect of increased energy expenditure.
Thyroid medicationsLevothyroxine taken by someone with a normal thyroid induces an artificial hyperthyroid state, which burns extra energy.
Laxatives / EnemasProducts such as Miralax or fleet enemas.
DiureticsLasix is the most commonly tested example.
EmeticsIpecac syrup — historically used to induce vomiting; sale is now restricted and it is rarely abused today.
Weight-loss-indicated drugs used without indicationLipase inhibitors, serotonin modulators, naltrexone/bupropion, GLP-1 receptor agonists — purging-type behavior when used by someone who does not medically qualify for weight-loss treatment.
Running Case — Introduced Without Vitals

A 24-year-old female presents to her primary care provider asking for medications to help with weight loss. She states she has been dieting and feels as though she is significantly overweight. She states that when stressed, she eats larger servings and will sometimes eat a half-gallon of ice cream. She has tried exercising more, diet pills, thyroid supplements, and laxatives, and has found none helpful.

Q: This case is deliberately left vague. What single piece of missing information would most help differentiate the diagnosis, and why?

▸ Click to reveal answer
Answer

Vital signs, specifically BMI. BMI is one of the main criteria separating Anorexia (low BMI) from Bulimia/Binge Eating Disorder (normal-to-high BMI). Without it, the ice cream episode could point to binge eating, and the diet pills/laxatives to purging — but there's no way yet to know whether this is a restriction-dominant or binge-dominant picture. We'll revisit this same case with vitals added in the Anorexia and Bulimia sections below.

Restriction-Dominant Eating Disorder

Anorexia Nervosa

Epidemiology ⭐ HY

  • F:M ratio 10:1
  • Bimodal peak onset: 12–14 and again around 18
  • High-risk groups: athletes, dancers, models, actors — anyone under heavy early scrutiny of appearance
  • Higher prevalence in higher socioeconomic groups (unusual among psychiatric disorders)
  • Highest mortality of all psychiatric disorders — from medical complications, comorbid diagnoses, and suicide

Risk Factors

  • Caucasian females
  • Eating difficulties in early childhood
  • Early praise for weight loss
  • Maternal depression
  • Family history of an eating disorder
  • "Type A" personality traits
🔑 Three-Point Diagnosis

1. Restriction of energy intake relative to what's normal for age, sex, and physical health.
2. Fear of weight gain — to the point of continued weight loss or maintenance of a low body weight, with no comfort reached at a lower weight.
3. Distortion of body image — the person believes they are overweight despite an objectively low BMI. This distortion is also what most impacts treatment engagement and self-esteem.

Q — Case Revisited: Same 24-year-old female. Vitals now added: BP 110/60, Pulse 60, BMI 15.5. She still reports feeling significantly overweight, occasional half-gallon ice cream episodes, and use of diet pills/thyroid supplements/laxatives. What's the diagnosis?

▸ Click to reveal answer
Answer

Anorexia Nervosa, Binge-Eating/Purging Type, Severe. A BMI of 15.5 falls in the severe range (15–15.99). The ice cream episode suggests a binge-eating pattern, and the diet pills/thyroid supplements/laxatives suggest purging-type compensatory behavior — both layered on top of a body-image distortion (feeling overweight at a BMI well below normal). The low BMI is the single biggest clue steering this toward anorexia rather than bulimia.

Presentation & Comorbid Diagnoses

  • Low BMI, social withdrawal
  • Online "pro-ana" community involvement
  • Rituals around food
  • Depression, irritability, low energy (the brain and heart are the body's two highest energy consumers — both suffer first in caloric restriction)
  • Suicidal ideation
  • Persistent inability to feel satisfied with weight loss achieved
  • Depression
  • Anxiety
  • OCD / OCPD
  • Substance use disorders
  • Personality disorders

Physical Effects by System

Brain

Headaches, seizures, enlarged ventricles

Dental

Caries, tooth enamel erosion (from purging)

Endocrine

Low testosterone/estrogen, amenorrhea, hypothyroidism, hypercortisolemia

Heart

Bradycardia, arrhythmia, QTc prolongation, hypotension, mitral valve prolapse

Dermatologic

Lanugo (fine body hair), Russell's sign (knuckle calluses from induced vomiting)

GI

Melanosis coli, delayed motility, SMA syndrome

MSK

Osteoporosis / osteopenia

⚠️ Purging Method Changes the Acid-Base Picture

Vomiting removes stomach acid → metabolic alkalosis, with decreased chloride and potassium. Laxative abuse pulls fluid/electrolytes from the gut → metabolic acidosis, with decreased sodium and increased chloride. This is a common second/third-level exam distinction.

🧠 Memory Hook

Baseline metabolic derangements: hypokalemia, hypocalcemia, hypophosphatemia, hypomagnesemia, plus thiamine (B1), folate (B9), B12, and iron deficiencies. A significant thiamine/folate deficiency in severe anorexia can present as a Wernicke-Korsakoff-type picture (confusion, ataxia, memory disturbance) — not just an alcohol-use-disorder finding.

Assessment

CategoryIncludes
Labs / StudiesCMP, CBC, lipid panel, thyroid function tests, urine drug screen (stimulant misuse), EEG, EKG, bone density scan
Physical ExamVital signs, cardiac auscultation, echocardiogram, Russell's sign, lanugo, dental exam for caries

Treatment

Weight restoration, generally around 1 lb/week, comes first — but reintroducing food too aggressively risks refeeding syndrome.

⚠️ Refeeding Syndrome

Reintroducing food/electrolytes/vitamins after prolonged restriction can trigger dramatic intracellular fluid and electrolyte swings. Watch for acute gastric dilation, CHF, arrhythmia, and rhabdomyolysis. Monitor labs closely and replete electrolytes proactively.

Once medically stabilized, treat comorbid diagnoses (depression, anxiety, etc.) and pursue therapy — largely CBT, though the lecturer notes this is an oversimplification of the real therapeutic approach.

Binge-Then-Compensate Eating Disorder

Bulimia Nervosa

Epidemiology

  • F:M ratio 10:1 (same as anorexia)
  • Onset typically later than anorexia — often not diagnosed before 18, sometimes not until the 20s
  • High mortality, but lower than anorexia
  • Risk factors: history of physical/sexual abuse, family connection to eating disorders

Diagnostic Criteria

  • Recurring episodes of binge eating
  • Recurrent inappropriate compensatory behaviors to prevent weight gain (laxatives, enemas, other meds, fasting, exercise, vomiting)
  • Occurring ≥1×/week for 3 months
  • Self-worth heavily tied to body shape and weight
  • Cannot be a presentation better explained exclusively by Anorexia — anorexia must be excluded first

Specifiers — by Weekly Compensatory Behavior Frequency

SpecifierCompensatory Behaviors / Week
Mild1–3
Moderate4–7
Severe8–13
Extreme14+

Partial/full remission specifiers apply the same way as in anorexia.

Q — Case Revisited (Bulimia Variant): Same opening presentation, but vitals now show BP 110/60, Pulse 60, BMI 24. She binges on a half-gallon of ice cream weekly (or more) after a full meal, feels out of control during the episode, and afterward sprints ½ mile 4 times per week. Diagnosis?

▸ Click to reveal answer
Answer

Bulimia Nervosa, Moderate. BMI 24 is normal-to-high, which excludes anorexia. The weekly ice-cream episodes with a subjective loss of control meet binge-eating criteria, and the compensatory exercise (sprinting) occurring 4×/week places this in the moderate specifier range (4–7 compensatory behaviors/week).

Comorbid Diagnoses

  • Mood disorders — 50–70%
  • Anxiety disorders — 15–65%
  • Substance use disorders — 25%
  • Personality disorders — 20–80%

Assessment

Similar workup to anorexia, but findings are typically less severe. Look for Russell's sign and dental decay. Patients with bulimia most often present at a normal-to-overweight BMI — a key distinguishing feature from anorexia.

Treatment

Treat medical abnormalities, reduce binge/purge frequency, treat comorbid diagnoses. Therapy is first-line — CBT primarily, with interpersonal therapy, the Maudsley model, and DBT also used.

SSRIs / SNRIs

First-line antidepressants

TCAs / MAOIs

Lesser-used alternatives

Topiramate

Also decreases appetite/binge frequency

Ondansetron

Antiemetic; can reduce purging
☐ US Boxed Warning

Bupropion (Wellbutrin) — Contraindicated in active or recent Bulimia (or Anorexia) Nervosa. Carries a black box warning for increased seizure risk in this population, related to the electrolyte disturbances and dehydration that purging behaviors cause. On an exam, if a stem describes bulimia and asks "which medication is contraindicated," bupropion is almost always the answer.

⚠️ Also Avoid: Lithium

Lithium requires a stable, consistent hydration state to avoid nephrotoxicity. Purging behaviors (especially self-induced vomiting) create a dehydration-prone state that raises the risk of lithium toxicity.

Binge Eating Without Compensation

Binge Eating Disorder (BED)

Epidemiology

  • Prevalence assessed as a 12-month prevalence rate (measurement window, per lecture)
  • F:M ratio 2:1 — notably more equal than anorexia/bulimia's 10:1
  • Medical associations: Prader-Willi syndrome (chromosome 15 deletion), Kluver-Bucy syndrome (bilateral temporal lobe lesions)
  • Risk factors: childhood obesity, weight-based bullying

Diagnostic Criteria

Recurring episodes of binge eating, with specified associated symptoms:

  • Eating rapidly
  • Eating past the point of fullness
  • Embarrassment around eating
  • Feelings of depression, disgust, or guilt afterward
  • Eating until uncomfortably full
🔎 The Key Differentiator from Bulimia

BED's criteria list has no requirement for compensatory behavior — no purging, fasting, or excessive exercise afterward. This is exactly what separates it from Bulimia, whose diagnosis specifically requires recurrent inappropriate compensatory behaviors.

Treatment

Therapy first-line: CBT, interpersonal therapy, DBT, self-help and behavioral programs.

Lisdexamfetamine (Vyvanse)

Stimulant

Reduces binge frequency.

Sertraline

SSRI

Topiramate

Reduces binge frequency/appetite
💡 Clinical Pearl

Best outcomes come from medication + therapy combined, not either alone.

Q: A patient binges weekly, eats rapidly and past fullness, and feels guilty afterward — but uses no laxatives, vomiting, fasting, or exercise to compensate. Bulimia or BED?

▸ Click to reveal answer
Answer

Binge Eating Disorder. The absence of any compensatory behavior rules out Bulimia, whose diagnosis requires recurrent inappropriate attempts to prevent weight gain after a binge.

Lower-Yield — Know the Criteria

Pica & Body Dysmorphic Disorder

The lecturer flags both as "good to know" rather than high-yield — brief exposure is enough, but the diagnostic criteria themselves are fair game.

Pica

Repeated eating of non-food substances (cloth, dirt, gum, hair, metal, paint, paper, soap) for ≥1 month.

  • Behavior must be inappropriate for the person's developmental level
  • Not culturally supported or socially normal
  • If occurring solely in the context of another mental disorder (autism, intellectual disability, schizophrenia) or medical condition (including pregnancy or iron deficiency), it must be severe enough to independently warrant clinical attention to still count as Pica
Body Dysmorphic Disorder (BDD)

Preoccupation with a nonexistent or slight defect in physical appearance (≥1 hour/day cumulative).

  • Leads to repetitive behaviors (mirror checking, excessive grooming, skin picking) or mental acts (comparing appearance to others)
  • Causes clinically significant distress or psychosocial impairment
  • Excluded if better explained by an eating disorder — someone with anorexia who fixates on body shape is diagnosed with anorexia, not a separate BDD

Q: A patient meets full criteria for Anorexia Nervosa and also spends hours a day mirror-checking and fixating on her body shape. Does she also carry a separate BDD diagnosis?

▸ Click to reveal answer
Answer

No. BDD's criteria explicitly exclude presentations that are better explained by an eating disorder. Her appearance preoccupation is accounted for by the Anorexia Nervosa diagnosis.

LO 2 — Differentiation

Clinical Clarity

Differentiating the Disorders

This is, by the lecturer's own account, the most common source of confusion — both on exams and in clinical practice. The DSM criteria are black-and-white; real presentations are gray. Start with the driving factor behind the behavior.

📉

Anorexia — Restrict to Lose

Driving factor is ongoing weight loss. Expect a low-to-very-low BMI. Core behavior is restriction, sometimes layered with binge/purge episodes — but the goal underneath is always further weight loss.

  • BMI: low (specifiers are BMI-defined)
  • Self-view: "I am overweight," despite objective evidence otherwise
🔁

Bulimia — Binge, Then Correct

Driving factor is avoiding weight gain after a binge episode. Expect a normal-to-high BMI. Core behavior is binge → compensate, in a repeating cycle.

  • BMI: normal to elevated
  • Self-worth tied to body shape/weight, without the same degree of "I am overweight" distortion seen in anorexia
🧩 "Unspecified" Eating Disorder

Real presentations don't always fit neatly. A person might show a clear anorexia-pattern (restriction, fear of weight gain, body image distortion) at a BMI of 19 — technically above the anorexia cutoff — or have a binge/purge pattern where it's unclear if the driving factor is more anorexia-like or bulimia-like. Clinically, these get labeled an unspecified feeding/eating disorder rather than forced into a diagnosis that doesn't fully fit.

How to Approach a Differentiation Question

  1. Check the BMI first. Below normal (<18.5) points strongly toward anorexia. Normal-to-high points away from anorexia and toward bulimia or BED.
  2. Identify the driving behavior. Is the primary goal ongoing weight loss (restriction-focused), or correcting/compensating after a binge (bulimia), or a binge with no compensation at all (BED)?
  3. Confirm frequency and duration. Bulimia and BED both need recurrent episodes; bulimia specifically needs ≥1×/week for 3 months, and anorexia's subtype (restricting vs. binge/purge) is defined over the past 3 months.
  4. Rule out exclusionary criteria. Anorexia must be excluded before diagnosing bulimia. If the picture is incomplete or mixed, consider "unspecified."

🔑 Key Takeaways
  • BMI is the fastest way to narrow the differential — low points to anorexia, normal/high moves away from it.
  • Ask "what's the goal of the behavior?" — losing weight (anorexia) vs. correcting a binge (bulimia) vs. no correction at all (BED).
  • Therapy (largely CBT) is first-line across all three disorders; medication is adjunctive.
  • Avoid bupropion and lithium in active bulimia/anorexia.
  • The DSM is black-and-white; real patients are gray — "unspecified" is a legitimate clinical category, not a cop-out.

Q: A patient has a BMI of 19, restricts intake to the point of missing two menstrual cycles, and insists she is overweight despite reassurance. She has never binged or purged. Best-fit diagnosis?

▸ Click to reveal answer
Answer

Likely an unspecified feeding/eating disorder (atypical anorexia pattern). Her behavior and body-image distortion match anorexia nervosa closely, but her BMI (19) is technically above the standard anorexia cutoff (below ~18.5). Clinically and on higher-order exam questions, this "driving factor" reasoning — restriction aimed at continued weight loss — outweighs a BMI that doesn't perfectly fit the textbook cutoff.

LO 1 · Diagnostic Classification

Shared Symptom Clusters

  • Binge eating: large amount in 2 hrs + loss of control
  • Calorie deficit: fasting/restricting/exercise, outside societal norms
  • Purging: vomiting, laxatives, diuretics, other meds

Anorexia — 3-Point Dx

  • Restriction of energy intake
  • Fear of weight gain (no comfort reached at lower weight)
  • Distortion of body image
  • Specifiers: restricting vs. binge/purge type (past 3 mo)

Anorexia Severity (BMI)

  • Mild 18.5–17 · Moderate 16.99–16
  • Severe 15.99–15 · Extreme <15

Bulimia Dx

  • Recurrent binge + recurrent compensatory behavior
  • ≥1×/week for 3 months; anorexia excluded first
  • Specifiers (behaviors/wk): Mild 1–3 · Mod 4–7 · Severe 8–13 · Extreme 14+

BED & Pica/BDD Dx

  • BED: binge + rapid eating/past satiety/embarrassment/guilt — no compensatory behavior
  • Pica: nonfood substances ≥1 mo, developmentally inappropriate
  • BDD: appearance preoccupation ≥1 hr/day; excluded if better explained by an eating disorder
LO 2 · Differentiation

Driving Factor

  • Anorexia: restrict to keep losing weight
  • Bulimia: binge → compensate to avoid gain
  • BED: binge, no compensation at all

BMI Quick Read

  • Low BMI → think Anorexia
  • Normal–high BMI → think Bulimia / BED

Epidemiology Compare

  • F:M — Anorexia 10:1 · Bulimia 10:1 · BED 2:1
  • Onset — Anorexia 12–14 & ~18 · Bulimia often >18
  • Mortality — Anorexia highest of all psych dx; Bulimia high but lower

Purging Acid-Base ⭐ HY

  • Vomiting → alkalosis (↓Cl, ↓K)
  • Laxatives → acidosis (↓Na, ↑Cl)

Unspecified Eating Disorder

  • Use when driving-factor pattern is clear but BMI/other cutoff isn't fully met (e.g., anorexia-pattern at BMI 19)
LO 3 · Treatment & Contraindications

Anorexia Tx

  • Weight restoration ~1 lb/wk; watch refeeding syndrome
  • Therapy (CBT); no meds FDA-approved
  • Appetite stimulants: SSRI, olanzapine, mirtazapine, cyproheptadine, cannabis

Bulimia Tx & Contraindications

  • CBT first-line (+ interpersonal, Maudsley, DBT)
  • SSRI/SNRI, topiramate, ondansetron
  • Avoid bupropion (boxed warning — seizure risk) & lithium (dehydration → toxicity)

BED Tx

  • CBT, interpersonal therapy, DBT
  • Vyvanse (FDA-approved), sertraline, topiramate
  • Best outcome: medication + therapy combined

Comorbidity Quick Hits

  • Anorexia: depression, anxiety, OCD/OCPD, SUD, personality d/o
  • Bulimia: mood 50–70% · anxiety 15–65% · SUD 25% · personality 20–80%
