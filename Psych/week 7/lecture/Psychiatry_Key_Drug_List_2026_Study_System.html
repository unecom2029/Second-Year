Memory Hook — the master rule of thumb (RS)
Serotonin drugs (SSRIs) treat anxiety + depression. Norepinephrine drugs (SNRIs, TCAs) treat depression + chronic pain. Oversimplified but powerful — and never use a serotonin-only drug for chronic pain.

Antidepressant timing rules know cold

  • Some response in 2–3 weeks; FULL effect takes 4–6 weeks. Warn patients — do not stop at 1–2 weeks for "no improvement."
  • Continue therapy ≥1 year after a 1st episode, 2 years after a 2nd, likely lifelong after a 3rd.
  • Most patients get a response (benefit); not all reach remission (cure).
  • SSRIs/SNRIs/NDRI replaced TCAs/MAOIs as first-line because of better side-effect profile and safety in overdose — not better efficacy.
Clinical pearl (RS)
Suicide risk can rise in early remission: energy returns before mood fully lifts, giving a severely depressed patient the activation to act. Monitor everyone — treated or not — for suicidal ideation.

Antidepressants · class 1 of 5 · 1950s

Tricyclic Antidepressants (TCAs)

Very effective, very dirty. They hit SERT + NET (the goal) and M1, H1, α1 (the collateral damage). Every side effect maps to one of those receptors — reason it out, don't memorize it.

My confidence:
Class blueprint

TCAs as a class

Mechanism
Block reuptake of both NE and serotonin into the presynaptic neuron → more transmitter stays in the cleft. (For chronic pain, it's the NE blockade that matters — RS.)
Place in therapy
2nd/3rd-line for MDD after SSRI/SNRI/NDRI trials — because of side effects and overdose lethality, not weak efficacy. 25–30% of patients quit due to intolerable side effects.
Special niches (board favorites)
  • Chronic pain (trigeminal neuralgia, diabetic neuropathy) → desipramine (most noradrenergic)
  • Bedwetting (enuresis) → imipramine
  • OCD → clomipramine (most serotonergic)
Monitoring
Blood levels are obtainable — useful for malabsorption (short-gut) or suspected noncompliance.
Reason it out — every TCA side effect is a receptor
  • M1 block (anticholinergic): urinary retention, constipation, tachycardia, dry mouth, blurred vision, confusion/hallucinations in the elderly — worst with tertiary amines.
  • H1 block: sedation · α1 block: orthostatic hypotension
  • Serotonin excess: serotonin syndrome (with other serotonergics) · plus GI upset, sexual dysfunction, possible hyperpyrexia (>104°F).
  • Na⁺-channel block in the heart: the overdose killer (below).
Memory Hook — anticholinergic toxidrome
"Hot as a hare, red as a beet, dry as a bone, blind as a bat, mad as a hatter." Think atropine.
Black Box — fatal in overdose
Cardiotoxic in OD: arrhythmias kill — look for QRS prolongation on EKG. Toxicity = the three C's: Cardiotoxicity, Coma, Convulsions. ONE WEEK'S worth of medication is lethal — prescribe with extreme caution to suicidal patients. Get a baseline EKG before starting and serially with dose changes.
Major interaction — CYP2D6
Mostly 2D6. Example: add bupropion (2D6 inhibitor) to imipramine (2D6 substrate) → imipramine levels climb → arrhythmia and death. Best prescribed by a psychiatrist.

Memory Hook — tertiary vs secondary
Tertiary amines are the "T-rouble Twins" (amiTriptyline, clomipramine, imipramine, doxepin): dirtier — more anticholinergic, more sedating, more serotonergic. This is also a recurring theme: use the side-effect profile as the treatment — sedating TCA for the insomniac, noradrenergic TCA for pain, serotonergic TCA for anxiety/OCD.
Antidepressants · class 2 of 5 · 1980s–2000s

SSRIs — the workhorses

All 6 are equally effective (head-to-head data) — so every exam question is about their personalities: half-life, withdrawal, CYP2D6, and one weird flagship fact each. Failing one SSRI ≠ failing the class; choice is driven by side effects, interactions, and cost.

My confidence:
Class blueprint

SSRIs as a class

Mechanism
Selectively block SERT (serotonin reuptake) at the presynaptic neuron — nothing else. That selectivity is why they're clean.
Indications
MDD, persistent depressive disorder; drug of choice for long-term panic disorder (acute panic → short-acting benzo PRN) and OCD; social anxiety disorder, PTSD, PMDD, trichotillomania. Anxiety/OCD need HIGHER doses than depression
Side effects (10–15% quit — far less than TCAs)
  • GI upset / diarrhea — the #1 reason for discontinuation (transient)
  • Sexual dysfunction — #2 reason: ↓libido, anorgasmia, delayed/retrograde ejaculation. Dose-dependent and NOT transient. Fixes: add sildenafil or bupropion (erection), cyproheptadine/bethanechol (ejaculation).
  • Insomnia (dose in AM or add trazodone), hyperhidrosis, headache, and apathy/decreased spontaneity (not transient).
Black Box — pediatric suicidality (know the nuance)
BBW: increased suicidality in children. But when the 2007 warning slowed prescribing, adolescent suicidality went UP; with renewed SSRI use it is no longer increasing. Most psychiatrists consider them safe in children — monitor every depressed patient for SI regardless of treatment.
Interaction — serotonin syndrome
Combining with other serotonin-elevating drugs — classically MAOIs, or the antibiotic linezolid (has MAOI activity) — can cause serotonin syndrome (delirium, hyperpyrexia, convulsions, hypertension). Full showdown vs NMS & friends in the Toxidrome arena.

Flu-like symptoms · Insomnia · Nausea · Imbalance · Sensory disturbances ("brain zaps") · Hyperarousal. Worst with short-t½ drugs (fluvoxamine, paroxetine); mildest with fluoxetine. Recent data (Kalfas et al., JAMA Psychiatry 2025): mean symptom count at 1 week fell below the clinical-significance threshold; dizziness was the most prevalent symptom.
Antidepressants · class 3 of 5 · 1994→

SNRIs — serotonin + norepinephrine

SSRI benefits plus the NE effect — which buys you chronic pain coverage (and blood-pressure problems). Remember the master rule: NE = depression + pain.

My confidence:
Prototype

venlafaxine (Effexor IR/XR) — first SNRI, 1994

Mechanism — a dose-dependent ladder
Low dose = SSRI → higher dose = SNRI (adds NE) → highest (above FDA limit) = SNDRI (adds DA). Serotonin is blocked at ALL doses; NE only at higher doses.
Indications
MDD, GAD, chronic pain (off-label), related depressive/anxiety disorders.
Side effects
SSRI-like + more initial anxiety, insomnia/sedation, nausea, constipation — and raises BP (diastolic hypertension) → monitor BP serially on the drug and with dose changes.
Withdrawal
Problematic, paroxetine-tier — often months to discontinue. XR > IR today partly because withdrawal is milder.
Enantiomer sibling
desvenlafaxine (Pristiq) — expensive, short t½, very hard to stop; the "citalopram→escitalopram" move repeated.
Delta from venlafaxine
duloxetine (Cymbalta)
  • Same core profile, but with formal chronic pain indications: fibromyalgia, diabetic neuropathy (NE effect → need higher doses for pain).
  • Contraindicated in glaucoma.
  • Fatal hepatotoxicity reported → monitor LFTs q3–6 months; stop if >3× ULN (AST/ALT in the 100s).
  • CYP2D6 substrate → caution in polypharmacy.
Memory Hook
DULL-oxetine dulls PAIN but punishes the LIVER and the EYE. (pain indications · LFTs · glaucoma)
Just know they exist
milnacipran & levomilnacipran (Fetzima)

New, predominantly noradrenergic SNRIs approved for MDD. Profiles mirror the class. Likely not yet on boards.

Antidepressants · class 4 of 5 · "other" agents

Bupropion, trazodone, mirtazapine & the new kids

Three drugs with three wildly different personalities — each is the answer to a specific vignette. If the patient profile is unusual, one of these is probably the answer.

My confidence:
The activator

bupropion (Wellbutrin, Zyban) — NDRI

Mechanism
NE + dopamine reuptake inhibition — ZERO serotonin effect, unique among ALL antidepressants.
The vignette that screams bupropion
MDD with atypical features: hypersomnia, increased appetite, weight gain, mood reactivity, excessive fatigue, "leaden paralysis".
Also for
Smoking cessation (as Zyban); erectile dysfunction in patients on an SSRI (helps erection, not ejaculation); augmenting another antidepressant; flat/numb affect (it intensifies affect rather than calming).
Side effects
Insomnia, irritability, anxiety, headache, activation — but NO sexual side effects, NO weight gain.
Contraindication
Seizure disorder (esp. documented epilepsy) and eating disorders (esp. anorexia nervosa) — it lowers the seizure threshold.
The sleeper

trazodone (Desyrel; Oleptro LA)

Mechanism
↓serotonin reuptake with 5-HT2 antagonism — more serotonin left in the cleft.
Reality of use
Technically an antidepressant, but antidepressant doses are too sedating to live on — so it's used as a bedtime sedative-hypnotic / insomnia PRN, everywhere in hospitals and clinics.
Side effects
  • Sedation (the feature, not the bug)
  • Orthostatic hypotension — careful in the elderly
  • PRIAPISM — erection >4 h, surgical emergency → ER now (necrosis risk; clitoral priapism also exists)
  • Adds to serotonin load (SS possible but commonly co-prescribed without trouble)
Pearls (RS)
Tiny daytime PRN doses work for agitation in senile dementia when benzos are contraindicated — an attending-impresser. Cousin nefazodone: less sedating but caused rare fatal hepatotoxicity; fine if monitored.
The feeder

mirtazapine (Remeron)

Mechanism
α2 antagonist (cuts the presynaptic "brake" → MORE NE + 5-HT release) + potent 5-HT2 and 5-HT3 antagonist; a tetracyclic. Somewhat faster onset than peers.
Indications
MDD, GAD.
Side effects = prescriptions in disguise
  • Sedation + increased appetite/weight gain → perfect for the depressed patient who is insomniac and anorexic (take advantage of side effects!)
  • ↑cholesterol; rarely agranulocytosis
Memory Hook — "California Rocket Fuel"
Mirtazapine + venlafaxine = potentiated NE/5-HT boost for severe depression. MirtaZzzapine feeds you and puts you to sleep.

Antidepressants · class 5 of 5 · third-line

MAOIs & the rules of combination

The nuclear option: block the enzyme that destroys ALL monoamines. Effective — with dietary restrictions, actually safer than TCAs — but two famous fatal traps make them a board-question factory.

My confidence:
Class blueprint

MAOIs as a class

Mechanism
Nonselective inhibition of MAO-A and MAO-B → less breakdown → ↑serotonin, dopamine, AND norepinephrine — plus tyramine accumulation (tyramine is a product of bacterial fermentation, normally destroyed by gut MAO).
Place
3rd-line; selected severe or atypical depression; sometimes works after TCA/SSRI/SNRI failures. Psychiatrist territory.
Agents
  • phenelzine (Nardil), tranylcypromine (Parnate)
  • selegiline (Emsam) — the only antidepressant available as a PATCH; MAO-B–selective, so low doses need no diet change; also used in Parkinson disease
Fatal trap #1 — tyramine hypertensive crisis
Tyramine-rich foods — aged cheese, beer, yeast, beans, chopped liver, snails, chocolate — or sympathomimetics (TCAs, ephedrine, L-dopa, decongestants, isoniazid, tramadol) on top of an MAOI → massive pressor response. Can be FATAL.
Fatal trap #2 — Black Box: meperidine
Meperidine is ABSOLUTELY contraindicated with MAOIs — the interaction that killed Libby Zion in the 1980s (the case that reshaped resident work hours).
Memory Hook — the MAOI dinner party from hell
Picture the forbidden charcuterie board: aged cheese + beer + chocolate + chopped liver + snails + beans + yeast bread — everything fermented, aged, or funky. The host offers you a Demerol (meperidine) nightcap. You decline. You live.

Antipsychotics · first generation · 1950s–70s

Typical antipsychotics & the EPS story

One receptor (D2), four brain pathways, four different outcomes. Block the right one and psychosis improves; the other three blocks are your side-effect chapters. "Major tranquilizers"/"neuroleptics" by their old names.

My confidence:

One drug, four pathways the whole story

Class facts

  • Indications: schizophrenia (incl. schizophreniform, brief psychotic disorder), schizoaffective disorder, psychosis in general, bipolar I mania, paranoia, irritability/agitation in children (autism) and elderly (dementia), Tourette syndrome, Huntington chorea. Effective for POSITIVE symptoms only.
  • History: chlorpromazine (1954) is the potency yardstick all antipsychotics are compared against. CATIE showed typicals ≈ atypicals in efficacy; the difference is side effects. Now usually 2nd-line for non-psychiatrists; psychiatrists still use them extensively.
Memory Hook — the potency see-saw
HIGH potency (haloperidol, fluphenazine) = high EPS/NMS/prolactin, low anticholinergic/sedation/hypotension. LOW potency (chlorpromazine) = the reverse: high anticholinergic + sedation + α-block hypotension, low EPS. The see-saw never balances: what you gain on one side you pay on the other. (Rare for both: agranulocytosis, broken thermoregulation → heat stroke/hypothermia.)

The EPS timeline — 4·4·4·4 gift from the exam gods

  • Acute dystonia Rx must be parenteral (IM/IV) anticholinergics — NOT PO (a laryngospasm can't swallow a pill).
  • Akathisia: partially helped by anticholinergics, but propranolol (β-blockers) is drug of choice; diphenhydramine and lorazepam also work.
  • Tardive dyskinesia is chronic (#1–3 are acute), sometimes irreversible, and worsens initially when you stop the drug (unmasking) — remission takes MONTHS. valbenazine (tetrabenazine derivative, 2017) treats it.
Antipsychotics · second generation · 1990s→

Atypicals: the PINEs, the DONEs, two PIPs & a RIP

D2/D4 + 5-HT2 antagonists. Preferred first-line (safer, better adherence, less TD — CATIE), treat positive AND negative symptoms, and their side effects are literally encoded in their names. The suffix IS the pharmacology.

My confidence:
Memory Hook — the suffix rule (RS)
-PINE → weight gain, diabetes, metabolic syndrome, sedation  ·  -DONE → EPS, prolactin, NMS  ·  -PIP- → akathisia but overall well tolerated (kid-friendly)  ·  -RIP- → metabolically neutral, "watered-down DONE." Watch Dr. Smith's video: "The PINES, the DONES, two PIPs, and a RIP". Also: NMS + EPS = DONEs; metabolic catastrophe = PINEs.

PINE · lowest D2 potency of all
quetiapine (Seroquel)
  • Uses: schizophrenia exacerbations (esp. with severe depression), Parkinson / Lewy body dementia, bipolar I or psychotic MDD, MDD adjunct — and (an RS-flagged liability) primary-care anxiety/insomnia.
  • SEs: sedation, weight gain, T2DM, metabolic syndrome. PO only.
  • Street name "Suzie Q" — abused as a downer.
PINE · high potency
olanzapine (Zyprexa)
  • Uses: schizophrenia, bipolar mania, psychosis, acute agitation (IM).
  • Worst weight gain of ALL atypicals (>7% body wt), ↑HbA1c & triglycerides; sedating (give QHS).
  • CYP1A2 substrate — smoking induces 1A2 → levels drop up to 40% → relapse. 90% of schizophrenics smoke. "Do the math." exam + boards
  • Forms: PO, Zydis wafer, IM (agitation), 1-mo LAI (Relprevv), Symbyax (+fluoxetine combo).
PINE · in a class by itself
clozapine (Clozaril)
  • Most effective antipsychotic; for refractory patients; the exception that genuinely helps negative symptoms; the only antipsychotic proven ANTISUICIDAL.
  • Agranulocytosis ~1% → mandatory CBCs: weekly (even 2×/wk) at first; if stable ~6 months → every other week. (2025: FDA removed the REMS program.)
  • Seizures (2–6%), rare myocarditis, sedation + weight gain; CYP1A2/smoking issue like olanzapine.
  • PO only (+ Fazaclo wafer) — no LAI, and nonadherence is the Achilles heel (RS reaches for LAIs first).
DONE · high potency
risperidone (Risperdal)
  • Uses: schizophrenia, bipolar I/mania, Tourette, autism behavioral dysregulation, dementia in the elderly.
  • At high doses behaves like a high-potency typical: EPS, akathisia, ↑prolactin (gynecomastia, amenorrhea, galactorrhea).
  • Forms: PO, M-tab, Consta (2-wk LAI, fading), Perseris (4-wk SQ, 2019), Uzedy ER-SQ — 2026: also approved for bipolar I maintenance (mono or + Li/valproate).
DONE · 9-OH-risperidone
paliperidone (Invega)
  • Uses: schizophrenia; approved for schizoaffective disorder. SEs mirror risperidone (it's the metabolite).
  • LAI dynasty: Sustenna (1 mo) → Trinza (3 mo) → Hafyera (6 mo — 2 injections/yr).
  • RS's drug of choice — LAIs beat PO adherence, and patients prefer being reminded of illness 4×/yr, not twice a day.
DONE · must eat to absorb
ziprasidone (Geodon)
  • Uses: schizophrenia, acute agitation (first atypical with rapid IM; in practice Zyprexa/Haldol/Thorazine still win).
  • Take with food or it isn't absorbed; QTc prolongation → torsades risk; baseline EKG; avoid if QTc >500 ms.
  • CIs: prolonged-QTc settings, acute MI, uncompensated heart failure, arrhythmia history, and history of BULIMIA (electrolyte-driven QTc risk).
PIP · the kid-friendly ones
aripiprazole (Abilify) & brexpiprazole (Rexulti)
  • Uses: schizophrenia, bipolar I, MDD adjunct, autism behavioral dysregulation. Well tolerated → often chosen for children; watch for akathisia at higher doses; some weight gain (less than PINEs).
  • Abilify forms: PO, Maintena (1-mo LAI), Asimtufii (2-mo, 2024), Aristada (1–2 mo), MyCite (digital ingestion tracking).
  • brexpiprazole = "the other PIP," same story — plus indication for agitation in Alzheimer dementia when other measures fail.
RIP · novel class
cariprazine (Vraylar)
  • FDA-approved for bipolar I + schizophrenia (+ MDD adjunct).
  • Metabolically neutral but can cause EPS — think "watered-down DONE". PIPs/RIP are pitched as helping cognitive impairment in psychosis.
No suffix — brand-new mechanism
xanomeline/trospium (Cobenfy)
  • First muscarinic antipsychotic: xanomeline = central M1/M4 agonist; trospium = peripheral muscarinic antagonist that can't cross the BBB, included to cancel the body-side effects.
  • SEs are all peripheral-cholinergic anyway: nausea, dyspepsia, constipation, vomiting, HTN, abdominal pain, diarrhea, tachycardia, dizziness, GERD.
Me-too drugs
asenapine (Saphris) & iloperidone (Fanapt)
  • Not commonly used. Iloperidone got a new indication: acute manic/mixed episodes of bipolar I (D2 + 5-HT2A antagonist, high α1 affinity → orthostasis, dizziness, somnolence, weight gain, QT prolongation, LFT bumps, nasal congestion, tachycardia, dry mouth).

Potency pecking order (Medical Letter meta-analysis)

Olanzapine beat aripiprazole, quetiapine, risperidone & ziprasidone for psychotic symptoms; risperidone also beat quetiapine & aripiprazole — but quetiapine/aripiprazole shine for depressive symptoms. Aripiprazole, lurasidone, ziprasidone = not potent enough for first-line schizophrenia. (Also: lurasidone (Latuda), a DONE — schizophrenia/bipolar I/MDD-adjunct, well tolerated but weight gain, sedation, akathisia common; RS: too weak for monotherapy, fine as adjunct. Many axis-II/borderline patients end up on an antipsychotic.)

Sedative-hypnotic antianxiety agents

Benzodiazepines, Z-drugs, buspirone & barbiturates

All roads lead to GABA-A (except buspirone and hydroxyzine — that's the trick answer). Revisit the GABA channel: benzos = frequency, barbs = duration.

My confidence:
Class blueprint

Benzodiazepines (-pam / -lam)

Mechanism
Bind the BZ site on GABA-A → potentiate GABA by ↑ FREQUENCY of Cl⁻ channel opening → hyperpolarization → ↓ firing → calm.
Indications
ACUTE/short-term anxiety & panic; sedative-hypnotic; anticonvulsant (status epilepticus → lorazepam, or diazepam PR/IM/IV); detox (ETOH withdrawal / DT prevention → long-acting chlordiazepoxide); muscle relaxant; night terrors; sleepwalking. Bridge role: cover anxiety during the 4–6 weeks an SSRI needs to work.
Why not long-term
Somnolence, psychomotor impairment, impaired memory, and addiction — dependence develops rapidly. Medical Letter: even for anxiety without depression, an SSRI is first choice; benzos = short-term/intermittent, limited benefit in OCD/PTSD.
Toxicity
Additive CNS depression with alcohol; overdose antidote = flumazenil (competitive BZ-receptor antagonist).

BZ1-selective — the "Z-drugs"
Zolpidem (Ambien) · esZopiclone (Lunesta) · Zaleplon (Sonata)
  • Short-term insomnia: rapid onset, little morning hangover; tolerance develops → use intermittently. Abuse potential at high doses.
  • Benzo-like SEs incl. rare anterograde amnesia, plus the famous ones: parasomnias — sleep-eating, sleep-sex, sleep-DRIVING (zolpidem especially; real crashes), daytime dream-like states.
  • OD antidote: flumazenil (same receptor).
Not GABA at all — trick-answer material
buspirone (BuSpar)
  • 5-HT1A partial agonist — no GABA, no sedation, no addiction, no alcohol interaction.
  • GAD/chronic anxiety, antidepressant adjunct; takes 4–6 weeks → USELESS for acute anxiety/panic; weaker than SSRIs/benzos; don't fear titrating to 60 mg/day; some call it a placebo — patients who've tasted benzos are unimpressed.
  • SEs (resolve ~1 wk): dizziness, headache, GI upset.
The rest of the sleep aisle
hydroxyzine · ramelteon · suvorexant
  • hydroxyzine (Vistaril/Atarax): antihistamine anxiolytic — zero abuse potential, dosable 4–5×/day "in the moment"; drowsiness + anticholinergic effects. Born as an itch drug.
  • ramelteon (Rozerem): melatonin-receptor agonist for sleep-onset insomnia (OTC melatonin is the cheap version).
  • suvorexant (Belsomra): the only orexin antagonist — sleep onset AND maintenance; unique MOA, efficacy still being judged.
Elder statesmen · 3rd-line
Barbiturates (-barbital)
  • MOA: facilitate GABA by ↑ DURATION of Cl⁻ opening; depress somatosensory cortex/motor activity → sedation → hypnosis → OD = respiratory depression, coma, death.
  • Uses: sedatives, anesthesia induction (incl. ECT), occasional anticonvulsant. Contraindicated in porphyria.
  • SEs: bradycardia, hypotension, hangover, rare Stevens-Johnson, blood dyscrasias. Dependence; additive with ETOH; CYP450 INDUCERS → drug interactions.
  • Names: phenoBARBITAL (Luminal), pentoBARBITAL (Nembutal) — and rule-breaker thiopental (ECT induction, with succinylcholine).
Drugs for bipolar disorder

Mood stabilizers — lithium & the anticonvulsants

Lithium is the classic; valproate the co-star; each anticonvulsant has ONE terrifying fact to own. Note the modern reality: atypical antipsychotics have largely displaced mood stabilizers (less lab monitoring, better tolerated).

My confidence:
Prototype

lithium (Eskalith, Lithobid ER) — "Li⁺"

Mechanism
Not fully clear; best evidence: alters intracellular second messengers — notably inhibits inositol triphosphate (IP3) → stabilized neuronal activity.
Indications
Classic euphoric mania; blocks relapse into acute mania. ANTISUICIDAL in bipolar patients (baseline suicide risk ≥15%) — yet lethal in overdose. "Quite a quandary."
Acute side effects
Nausea, diarrhea, vomiting, FINE hand tremor, sedation, muscle weakness, edema, weight gain, dry mouth.
Chronic side effects learn the bolded ones
  • Polyuria/polydipsia → nephrogenic diabetes insipidus (ADH antagonist; reversible)
  • Hypothyroidism + goiter (autoimmune; ♀ > ♂)
  • Acne · psoriasis · leukocytosis (reversible)
  • Teratogenesis — EBSTEIN ANOMALY (1st-trimester organogenesis) boards
Toxicity (narrow window!)
Lethargy, ataxia, slurred speech, tinnitus, severe N/V, COARSE tremor (fine = therapeutic, coarse = toxic), arrhythmias, hypotension, seizures, delirium, coma, death.
Levels & labs
Therapeutic 0.8–1.2 (some say 1.5) mEq/L; toxicity often >1.8 but possible lower. Before starting + serially: Na⁺, Ca²⁺, K⁺, Cr, urinalysis, CBC, EKG, thyroid panel w/ TSH.
Memory Hook — "LITHIUM hits kidney & thyroid"
Lithium is a small cation the kidney handles like sodium — so it wrecks the kidney's water handling (DI) and rides Na⁺ balance (dehydration/thiazides → toxicity). Ebstein: “Lithium floats the tricuspid down” — atrialized right ventricle. Fine→coarse tremor = your built-in level meter.
The co-star
valproic acid (Depakote)
  • MOA: ↑ GABA availability (anticonvulsant). Mania efficacy ≈ lithium; also epilepsy, migraine prophylaxis.
  • SEs: somnolence, dizziness, GI upset, diplopia, ataxia, rash, weight gain, alopecia, prolonged bleeding, PCOS (esp. women w/ epilepsy).
  • ⛔ Rare FATAL HEPATOTOXICITY (monitor LFTs), pancreatitis, NEURAL TUBE DEFECTS (spina bifida — birth control!); monitor CBC (granulo-/thrombocytopenia).
  • Levels: 40–150 mcg/mL, serially.
The interaction machine
carbamazepine (Tegretol)
  • For mania when Li/valproate fail or aren't tolerated; less used than either.
  • SEs: drowsiness, dizziness, blurred vision, N/V, rash, hyponatremia.
  • ⛔ Leukopenia/AGRANULOCYTOSIS — monitor CBC + levels.
  • Inactivates OCPs; CYP3A4 inducer AND AUTOinducer — chews itself up → dose must rise early or symptoms resurge.
  • Cousin: oxcarbazepine (Trileptal) — same idea minus the interactions; RS prefers it in psych patients.
The slow-motion drug
lamotrigine (Lamictal)
  • MOA: ↓ glutamate release → membrane stabilization. Loved for bipolar depressive episodes.
  • Transient SEs: photosensitivity, headache, GI upset, dizziness, ataxia.
  • ⛔ STEVENS-JOHNSON SYNDROME (~1/1,200): titrate 25 mg every TWO weeks; rash starts on mucosa → stop immediately; off ≥1 week at >25 mg → restart titration from 25 mg.
  • Valproate doubles lamotrigine levels → titrate TWICE as slowly — THE lamotrigine DDI. know it
Supporting cast
topiramate & the acute-mania bridge
  • topiramate (Topamax): emerging; weight LOSS, renal stones, cognitive fog ("Dopamax"), decent efficacy — details in the Nervous System list.
  • iloperidone (Fanapt): now approved for acute manic/mixed bipolar I episodes (see atypicals).
  • Strategy Acute mania: mood stabilizers need 7–10 days → start an atypical and/or benzo alongside, then peel them off once the stabilizer kicks in.
Memory Hook — teratogen pairing
Lithium → Ebstein (L-E: "Lithium's Ebstein") · Valproate → Vertebrae (neural tube / spina bifida). Two drugs, two babies, two different disasters — never swap them on the exam.
Stimulants & non-stimulants

ADHD pharmacology

Two stimulant families (methylphenidate vs amphetamine — many costumes each), then the non-stimulant bench. Stimulants are highly effective; everything else is a negotiated compromise.

My confidence:
Class blueprint

Stimulants — Schedule II

Mechanism
Block DA/NE reuptake AND enhance release of DA/NE (amphetamines especially push release).
Side effects
Decreased appetite/weight, insomnia (dose after meals, not at night), anxiety/jitteriness/agitation, GI upset, dysphoria/mania, tachycardia/palpitations/HTN, possible growth suppression (drug holidays help), tics/dyskinesias, headache.
Stopping abruptly
Rebound: headache, hypersomnia, hyperphagia, depression, irritability, poor concentration.
Abuse reality check
Oral ADHD dosing has low abuse potential (vs snorted/IV meth); untreated ADHD raises later drug-abuse risk — treatment lowers it. Watch for feigned ADHD (diversion). FDA: no ER stimulants in children <6 years (weight loss).

Electroconvulsive therapy

ECT — the 80% treatment

Not a drug, but drug-adjacent and beloved by examiners. Modern ECT is anesthetized, brief, and the single most effective treatment for refractory depression.

My confidence:

The procedure

  • Electric current through the brain (uni- or bilateral) → controlled seizure. Anesthetized with thiopental (barbiturate) + succinylcholine (paralytic) so no violent convulsion.
  • A BP cuff inflated at the ankle keeps paralytic out of one foot — the visible twitch proves the seizure; EEG monitors centrally.
  • Therapeutic effect correlates with seizure DURATION (~25–60+ s, up to ~90 s); ~8 treatments over 2–3 weeks (up to 12).

Effects & place in therapy

  • Post-ictal: brief confusion/disorientation, headache, myalgia; anterograde/retrograde memory loss around treatment — usually transient (weeks), rarely longer.
  • ~80% respond — better than any antidepressant; relapse is common → maintenance ECT or drugs after.
  • For: refractory depression, intolerable drug SEs, even pregnancy, refractory mania/psychosis (rarely). Stigma (One Flew Over the Cuckoo's Nest) suppresses use.
Memory Hook
"Thio + Succ + Sock": THIOpental sleeps you, SUCCinylcholine stills you, the blood-pressure-cuff "SOCK" on the ankle lets one foot seize as proof. Longer seizure = better response; 8 sessions; 80%.
