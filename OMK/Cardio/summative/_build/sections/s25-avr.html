Section 25 · Valves & Surgery

Aortic Valve Replacement

The aortic valve opens and closes about 80 times a minute for a lifetime, and aortic stenosis is the valve problem that most often ends in surgery. Two choices define every replacement: how it goes in (open surgery or a catheter) and what goes in (mechanical or tissue). Dr. McGrath’s approach to the second one is simple — explain the trade-off, then let the patient choose.

Surgical versus transcatheter replacement

🔪

SAVR — open

  • Sternotomy, cardiopulmonary bypass, cold high-potassium cardioplegia to stop the heart in diastole, cut out the calcified valve, sew in a new one.
  • Low- and intermediate-risk, younger patients; a mechanical or tissue valve; the only option when the root or ascending aorta also needs replacing.
  • Low-risk mortality under 1%.
  • At least 5 days in hospital; back to normal life in 4–6 weeks while the sternum heals.
🎈

TAVR — catheter

  • Mostly transfemoral: a wire across the valve, then a balloon-mounted stented tissue valve inflated during rapid ventricular pacing, pushing the native leaflets aside. “Kind of like having a PCI.”
  • Originally for intermediate- to high-risk elderly patients (70s–90s); now extending to low risk. Dr. Qazi: now first-line for most AS.
  • Mortality about 2.5% even in high-risk patients; trace-to-mild paravalvular leaks are tolerated.
  • Mick Jagger was back on tour about 2 weeks after his.
⚠️ TAVR is contraindicated in pure aortic regurgitation

A TAVR valve anchors on a calcified annulus. Pure AR (and a dilated root) usually has too little calcium, so the valve would migrate. These patients need surgical valve (± root) replacement.

⚠️ TAVR durability — the slides conflict

Slide 58 lists “50% structural degeneration at 8 years.” Slide 59 and the spoken lecture say current-generation TAVR valves track like surgical tissue valves, with data out to about 9 years; the unknown is what happens after year 10. The 50% figure most likely describes early-generation valves. This uncertainty is one reason younger patients still lean toward SAVR.

Q: An 84-year-old frail woman has severe calcific aortic stenosis, a high STS risk score and good femoral arteries. What is the best option?

Answer

Transfemoral TAVR — elderly, high risk, with a calcified annulus to anchor the valve.

Q: A 53-year-old with a known bicuspid valve passes out chasing his dog. Echo shows severe AS (mean gradient 50 mm Hg) and a 5.2 cm ascending aorta. What operation, and what must be done first?

Answer

Surgical AVR with ascending-aorta replacement — symptomatic severe AS plus a bicuspid aortopathy that TAVR cannot address. First, coronary catheterisation, because about half of AVR patients have coronary disease needing bypass at the same operation.

Q: A 45-year-old with a new bileaflet mechanical aortic valve asks to switch from warfarin to apixaban. What do you tell him, and what happens when he needs a colonoscopy?

Answer

No — mechanical valves need warfarin for life; DOACs are not safe with them. For the colonoscopy, warfarin is stopped and he is bridged with heparin.

Q: A 28-year-old woman who plans to have children needs her aortic valve replaced. Which prosthesis, and what is the trade-off?

Answer

A bioprosthetic (tissue) valve, which needs only aspirin, avoiding warfarin’s teratogenicity and bleeding in pregnancy. The trade-off is structural degeneration and a later redo operation or valve-in-valve TAVR.

Q: Two days after TAVR, a patient’s heart rate falls to 35 with complete heart block. Why, and what is the treatment?

Answer

The expanded valve frame compresses the conduction system just below the aortic annulus (the His bundle runs in the membranous septum there). Treat with a permanent pacemaker. Surgical AVR carries the same risk at about 3–4%.

Q: A patient with a mechanical mitral valve and a subtherapeutic INR presents with dyspnea, a new murmur and muffled prosthetic clicks. What is the likely complication? A different patient, years after a tissue AVR, has a new harsh systolic murmur. What is that?

Answer

The first is prosthetic valve thrombosis — inadequate anticoagulation of a mechanical valve; confirm on echo or CT and treat with fibrinolysis or surgery. The second is structural degeneration of the bioprosthesis (calcified, stenotic leaflets), treated with redo surgery or valve-in-valve TAVR.

Q: A 40-year-old with Marfan syndrome has severe pure aortic regurgitation and a dilated root. Why not TAVR?

Answer

A TAVR valve needs a calcified annulus to anchor; pure AR with a dilated root has none, so the valve would migrate. He needs surgical root and valve replacement.
