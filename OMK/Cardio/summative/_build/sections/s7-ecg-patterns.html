Section 7 · The ECG & Arrhythmias

The ECG of Disease: Hypertrophy, Bundle Branch Block, Ischemia and Electrolytes

Section 6 gave you the reading order. This section is what you find when you use it on a sick heart. Four families of finding live here: a chamber that has grown, a conduction pathway that has broken, a coronary artery that has closed, and an electrolyte that has drifted. Each one changes a specific part of the tracing, and each one has a short list of criteria worth memorising outright.

Atrial enlargement: look at the P wave in leads II and V1

The P wave is the right atrium depolarizing first and the left atrium second, so the two atria each own one half of it. Lead II looks at the P wave along its length and lead V1 looks at it end-on, which is why you need both.

FindingLead IILead V1ContextPicture
Normal Upright and narrow Biphasic, like one cycle of a sine wave The right half then the left half
Left atrial enlargement Still upright but wider. Board add-on Over 120 ms, often notched — “P mitrale” Mostly negative, with a large terminal negative portion Common with ageing and a stiff left ventricle, and in athletes
Right atrial enlargement Tall and peaked. Board add-on Over 2.5 mm — “P pulmonale” Mostly positive, with a large initial positive portion In a child, think congenital heart disease
🪝 Which half of the P wave grew?

The right atrium makes the first half of the P wave and the left atrium makes the second. So right atrial enlargement makes the P taller (a bigger first half piles onto the second), and left atrial enlargement makes it wider (a bigger second half stretches it out). In V1, the lead sees the right atrium coming toward it and the left atrium going away, which is why right atrial enlargement makes V1 mostly positive and left atrial enlargement makes it mostly negative.

Left ventricular hypertrophy ★ HY

This one earns its place clinically as well as on an exam. New heart failure with left ventricular hypertrophy on the tracing starts a whole work-up. Hypertrophy also produces T-wave inversion and ST depression — the so-called strain pattern — which is easy to mistake for ischemia.

CriterionRuleNote
Left axisRequiredThe forces are pulled leftward by the extra muscle
R wave in aVL> 11 mmThe most specific criterion
S in V1 or V2, plus R in V5 or V6≥ 35 mmThe most sensitive criterion (Sokolow–Lyon)

Call left ventricular hypertrophy when you have the left axis plus either voltage criterion, or both.

R-wave progression and right ventricular hypertrophy

  • Normal progression: V1 shows a small r wave with a deep S, the R grows as you move across the chest, the transition where R equals S falls at V3 or V4, and V5 and V6 show a tall R with a small s.
  • Right ventricular hypertrophy is a right axis together with a reversed progression: a tall R already in V1, getting smaller as you move toward V6. It is rare in adults, so think congenital disease when you see it.
  • Board add-on A tall R in V1 is not only hypertrophy. It also appears in right bundle branch block, in a posterior infarction and in hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. Poor R-wave progression appears after an anteroseptal infarction, and in left ventricular hypertrophy, left bundle branch block and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease.

Wide QRS: bundle branch and fascicular blocks ★ HY

A normal QRS is under 120 ms, or three small boxes. When it is wide, the first thought is a bundle branch block: conduction delayed somewhere below the bundle of His. Picture the anatomy — the bundle of His divides into a right bundle and a left bundle, and the left bundle then splits into an anterior and a posterior fascicle.

BlockCriteriaPicture

🪝 WiLLiaM and MaRRoW Board add-on

Look at the shape of the complex in V1 and in V6. Left bundle branch block makes a W in V1 and an M in V6 — WiLLiaM. Right bundle branch block makes an M in V1 and a W in V6 — MaRRoW. The capital letters in each word tell you which bundle it is.

🧠 The difference between a bundle and a fascicle, on the tracing

A bundle branch block makes the QRS wide, because a whole ventricle has to be depolarized cell by cell. A fascicular block leaves the QRS normal, because the other fascicle still delivers the impulse to the left ventricle quickly — it just arrives from an unusual direction, which shifts the axis. So the question “is the QRS wide?” sorts the two groups before you look at anything else.

Board add-on One more wide-QRS pattern belongs here by sight: a short PR interval with a slurred upstroke on the QRS, called a delta wave. That is pre-excitation through an accessory pathway, the setting for Wolff–Parkinson–White, and it is worked through properly in Section 8.

The QT interval and what lengthens it ★ HY

Bazett’s formula corrects the QT for rate: the corrected QT equals the measured QT divided by the square root of the R–R interval in seconds. Normal is under 450 ms in men and 460 ms in women, and you worry above 500 ms.

  • The eyeball test. Mark the halfway point between two R waves. If the T wave has finished before that halfway mark, the QT is normal about 90% of the time. If it reaches or passes the mark, calculate it properly.
  • Why it matters: a long QT sets up polymorphic ventricular tachycardia, which is torsades de pointes — a twisting ribbon of complexes whose amplitude waxes and wanes. The mechanism, early afterdepolarizations during a prolonged repolarization, is in Section 10.
  • A wide QRS from a bundle branch block lengthens the QT by itself, so account for that before calling the repolarization abnormal.

Electrolytes on the tracing ★ HY

⬆️

High potassium

  • Tall, narrow, peaked T waves — the earliest change.
  • As it worsens, every interval widens: the PR lengthens, the QRS broadens, and eventually the P wave flattens and disappears.
  • A severely hyperkalemic tracing can end up looking like a sine wave.
⬇️

Low potassium

  • Flattened T waves with a prominent U wave following them.
  • The U wave merges with the T, so the QT (really a QU) looks long — which is exactly the torsades risk.
  • Low magnesium does the same thing, and the two usually travel together.

The three findings, and what each one means

FindingWhat it meansWhat you doPicture
ST elevation Transmural injury — a STEMI. Usually plaque rupture with 100% occlusion. Needs at least 1 mm of elevation in two or more contiguous leads, forming an anatomic pattern. Open the artery now — the catheterisation laboratory, or thrombolytics then transfer. “Time is muscle.”
ST depression Subendocardial injury — an NSTEMI. Rupture narrowing the artery to 70–90%, with some flow continuing. At least 1 mm below baseline, and often diffuse rather than confined to one territory. Stabilise first; this is not an indication for emergent revascularisation.
Q waves Dead myocardium — an old infarction. At least one small box wide and deep, in an anatomic pattern. The infarct is complete, usually by 12 to 24 hours. Nothing acute. Note it, and remember a Q wave is normal only in lead III.
T-wave inversion Non-specific. Ischemia, electrolytes, metabolic disturbance. In Dr. Qazi’s words, she does not know what an inverted T wave means without the context. Interpret it with the history and the rest of the tracing, never alone.
🧠 Reciprocal changes confirm a STEMI

ST elevation in one territory together with ST depression in the opposite leads is an electrical mirror image, and it confirms the diagnosis. The classic pairing is inferior ST elevation in II, III and aVF with ST depression in aVL and the anterior leads. When you are not sure whether an ST segment is really elevated, go looking for the mirror.

🔑 How a STEMI evolves over time Board add-on
  • The sequence: hyperacute T waves first (broad, tall and symmetric, the earliest sign of occlusion), then ST elevation, then Q waves with T-wave inversion as the ST segment returns toward baseline.
  • V2 and V3 thresholds are higher than elsewhere: at least 2 mm in men 40 and over, 2.5 mm in men under 40, and 1.5 mm in women.
  • A posterior infarction is read from the front as ST depression with a tall R wave and an upright T wave in V1 to V3 — the mirror image again.
  • A known left bundle branch block or a paced rhythm makes ischemia very hard to read.
🩺 Two mimics to keep off your differential by accident
  • Pericarditis also raises the ST segments, but everywhere rather than in one coronary territory, and it adds PR-segment depression (Section 23).
  • Early repolarization raises the J point in young people and is usually a normal variant. It comes from a larger transient outward potassium current in the epicardium than in the endocardium, which creates a voltage gradient early in repolarization.
  • Neither should be called a heart attack, and in both the history settles it.

Q: The axis is left. The S wave in V2 measures 22 mm and the R in V5 measures 18 mm. The R in aVL is 13 mm. What is the diagnosis, and which criterion here is the most specific?

Answer

Left ventricular hypertrophy. Both voltage criteria are met: 22 plus 18 is 40, which clears the 35 mm Sokolow–Lyon threshold, and the R in aVL is over 11 mm. The R in aVL above 11 mm is the more specific of the two; the precordial sum is the more sensitive.

Q: A tracing shows a QRS of 150 ms, a left axis, deeply negative complexes in V1 to V3 and a broad notched R wave in I, aVL and V6. What is it?

Answer

Left bundle branch block. Every criterion is present: a wide QRS, a left axis, negative V1 to V3, and a broad positive complex laterally. The W in V1 and M in V6 is the WiLLiaM half of the mnemonic.

Q: A patient has ST elevation in II, III and aVF, with ST depression in I and aVL. Which artery is occluded, and what does the ST depression add?

Answer

The right coronary artery, producing an inferior STEMI. The depression in I and aVL is a reciprocal change — the electrical mirror image — and it confirms that the elevation is real. In about 20% of people the circumflex is dominant and causes the same picture.

Q: There are Q waves in II, III and aVF, but the ST segments are flat. What does this mean, and what else does it explain about the tracing?

Answer

An old, completed inferior infarction — dead myocardium rather than acute injury. It also explains a left axis, because those inferior Q waves make aVF negative.

Q: A dialysis patient misses two sessions. The tracing shows tall, narrow, peaked T waves and a QRS that has widened since last month. What is happening, and what would the opposite electrolyte abnormality look like?

Answer

Hyperkalemia. Peaked T waves come first, then every interval widens as it worsens. Hypokalemia does the reverse of the T wave: it flattens the T and brings out a U wave, which makes the QT look long and carries torsades risk.

Q: A patient is on dofetilide for atrial fibrillation and is started on ondansetron for nausea. Potassium is 3.1 and magnesium is low. What rhythm are you worried about, and why?

Answer

Torsades de pointes. Two QT-prolonging drugs are stacked on top of each other and both of the electrolytes that protect repolarization are low. Dofetilide was the one drug Dr. Qazi named as having actually caused torsades in her own practice.

Q: A tall R wave appears in V1. Give three explanations other than right ventricular hypertrophy.

Answer

Right bundle branch block (the R′ of the rabbit ears), a posterior myocardial infarction (the mirror image of a posterior Q wave), and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. The axis and the QRS width separate them.
