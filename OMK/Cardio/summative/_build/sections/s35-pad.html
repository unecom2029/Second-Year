Peripheral Arterial Disease and Claudication

Dr. Segal’s case was a 70-year-old former smoker whose leg pain had been blamed on her back, her “arthritis” and finally her statin. It was peripheral arterial disease — common, under-recognised and deadly, because the legs are a window on the heart and brain. Two decisions run the topic: is the leg pain vascular or neurogenic, and is the limb threatened?

🎙️ “Boards love this question”

Who feels better walking uphill?

The patient with neurogenic claudication — you lean forward going uphill, and lumbar flexion opens the canal; in extension the hypertrophic ligamentum flavum compresses the nerves. The same goes for leaning on a grocery cart.

⚠️ One slide row is backwards

One App Session 2 table gives pain dissipating in “30 minutes (vascular)” versus “1–3 minutes (neurogenic)”. The guideline and NEJM tables on the same slides say the opposite: vascular claudication settles within minutes of standing still; neurogenic pain is slow and needs sitting or flexion.

The rest of the leg-pain differential

  • An AAA in a smoker — the one “you don’t want to miss” (Section 32).
  • Hip or knee arthritis (aching at joints, slow relief); venous congestion (bursting pain, relieved by elevation); chronic compartment syndrome or popliteal entrapment in young athletes; vasculitis and Buerger disease in young smokers (Section 34).
  • “Is it the statin?” Never dismiss it, but most statin symptoms are nocebo: in an N-of-1 trial (SAMSON), symptoms on placebo were about 90% of those on the statin, and lowest with no tablets.

The exam and the ankle–brachial index

Who gets it

  • About 7% of US adults; rising steeply with age; men and women equally.
  • Smoking and diabetes, smoking and diabetes. Smoking raises the risk 2–3× (2–4× on one quiz slide), and it persists for about 30 years after quitting (“the Energizer Bunny of vascular disease”). Diabetes drives severe, limb-threatening and tibial disease. Hypertension accounts for about 40% of the risk; LDL matters, but less than for coronary disease.
  • Most PAD is silent or atypical: 20–50% asymptomatic, 40–50% atypical leg discomfort, only 10–30% classic claudication.

The exam

  • BP in both arms (a difference over 10 mm Hg suggests subclavian disease); all pulses, symmetric or not; bruits (abdominal, femoral, carotid).
  • Skin: hair loss, shiny atrophic skin, thick nails, cool feet, ulcers (arterial ulcers are on the toes and pressure points, painful, punched-out Board add-on).
  • Elevation pallor and dependent rubor (the Buerger test): pale when raised, dusky red when hanging down. Rubor fades with elevation; cellulitis does not — the island patient’s “recurrent cellulitis” was CLTI.
  • Sensory and motor testing (10-g monofilament in diabetics).

The ankle–brachial index

ABI = the higher ankle pressure (DP or PT) on that side ÷ the higher of the two arm pressures.

  • Segmental pressures: a drop of 20 mm Hg or more between adjacent levels localises the block. Doppler waveforms go from triphasic to biphasic to monophasic.
  • Imaging only if revascularisation is considered. CT angiography with runoff is the diagnostic study; an angiogram (DSA) is for procedures — “the way they trick you on exams is to say the hallmark for the diagnosis is an aortogram.” MRA is an alternative (gadolinium risk). Check creatinine before contrast.
  • The vascular patient is a coronary patient: assess cardiac status and duplex the carotids.

Aortoiliac occlusive disease

  • Atherosclerosis begins in the infrarenal aorta. Focal disease in young women who smoke with high cholesterol (the worst outcomes); diffuse multi-segment disease in older men with diabetes and hypertension.
  • Hallmark: diminished or absent femoral pulses. Buttock and thigh claudication; the resting ABI can be near-normal (get an exercise ABI).
  • Collaterals from the iliac, lumbar and internal mammary arteries keep the legs alive.
🔑 Leriche syndrome — “a great board question”

A triad: buttock and hip claudication, diminished or absent femoral pulses, and erectile dysfunction — aortoiliac occlusion at the bifurcation. ED, especially in a younger man, is a vascular sign: “Don’t just write a prescription for Viagra.” Because the occlusion sits near the renal arteries, many also have kidney disease.

Below the inguinal ligament: femoropopliteal (SFA) disease — strong femorals but weak popliteal and pedal pulses — is treated with stents, CFA endarterectomy, or bypass with the great saphenous vein (the preferred conduit; 70–90% five-year patency). Tibial disease (diabetes, dialysis) presents with ulcers and gangrene; for tissue loss, open bypass to a tibial or pedal target is the procedure of choice.

Acute limb ischemia

🚨 The six P’s — “probably the most important thing you’re gonna have”

Pain, Pallor, Paresthesia, Paralysis, Pulselessness, Poikilothermia (“perishingly cold”). Board add-on Sensory loss comes before motor loss; once the foot cannot move, the limb is immediately threatened.

  • Causes: embolism, mostly cardiac — left atrial thrombus in AF (“the biggest thing we have”), LV thrombus after MI, valve vegetations, atrial myxoma; also a popliteal aneurysm. Thrombosis in situ on a plaque, a hypercoagulable state, a dissection, or a failed bypass graft. Emboli lodge at bifurcations (Board add-on commonly the common femoral).
  • An acute occlusion is far worse than the same chronic one, because there is no time for collaterals.
  • Treatment: IV unfractionated heparin first — “always the first step” — and emergent vascular surgery. If motor and sensory function are intact there is time for catheter-directed thrombolysis or percutaneous thrombectomy; otherwise open Fogarty balloon embolectomy. Then find the source (echo, rhythm monitoring, long-term anticoagulation for AF).
  • Absolute contraindications to thrombolysis: stroke or brain surgery within 2 months, major surgery within 2 weeks, significant bleeding risk.
  • Reperfusion and compartment syndrome: after about 6–8 hours without flow the limb may not be salvageable; reperfusion free radicals make muscle swell inside its fascia. Signs: pain on passive stretch, a tense calf, numbness of the first web space (deep peroneal nerve). Treatment: fasciotomy.
  • Amputation when the limb cannot be saved (“life over limb”): a below-knee amputation costs 10–40% more energy to walk, above-knee 50–70% — a frail patient may not manage it.

Management: exercise, smoking cessation, drugs and revascularization

🎯 Supervised exercise first

A 65-year-old with progressive claudication on the golf course and an ABI of 0.7: the best initial management is supervised exercise therapy — not a stent, not a bypass, not pentoxifylline (no role), and certainly not a golf cart (“anti-osteopathic!”). Exercise grows collaterals, improves oxygen use and lowers inflammation.

⚠️ A recording slip

Answering a student, the surgery recording says “the first line of treatment is going to be anticoagulation” for early claudication. The slides are right: antiplatelet therapy for claudication; IV heparin is the first step in acute limb ischemia.

Natural history — the heart is the bigger risk

  • Over 5 years: stable claudication in 70–80%, revascularisation 10–20%, critical limb ischemia only 1–2% — but MI or stroke about 20% and death 15–30%.
  • Polyvascular disease: one-year cardiovascular death, MI, stroke or hospitalisation rises from 5.3% (risk factors only) to 12.6%, 21.1% and 26.3% with one, two and three affected beds. “Atherosclerosis is a systemic illness” (Dr. Qazi) — and it almost never involves the arms.

Q: A 66-year-old smoker gets calf cramping after two blocks that goes away after 3 minutes standing still. A 70-year-old with chronic back pain gets burning, tingling leg pain standing at the sink, relieved by sitting and leaning forward. Label each, and say who feels better walking uphill.

Answer

The first is vascular claudication (exertional, reproducible, relieved by standing still). The second is neurogenic claudication from lumbar stenosis. The neurogenic patient feels better walking uphill — lumbar flexion opens the canal.

Q: Arm pressures are 136 and 140 mm Hg; the left ankle is 54. Calculate the left ABI. In a diabetic on dialysis, an ABI of 1.6 with absent pedal pulses — what does it mean, and what next?

Answer

54 ÷ 140 (the higher arm) = 0.39 — severe, in the critical-ischemia range. An ABI over 1.40 means non-compressible calcified arteries; use the toe–brachial index.

Q: A 58-year-old man has pain in both buttocks climbing a ladder, new erectile dysfunction and weak femoral pulses. Name the syndrome and the level of disease.

Answer

Leriche syndrome — aortoiliac occlusive disease at the aortic bifurcation.

Q: A 74-year-old with atrial fibrillation who stopped her apixaban has a sudden cold, pale, pulseless right leg; sensation is reduced but she can wiggle her toes. First drug, and the next step?

Answer

IV unfractionated heparin immediately, and emergent vascular surgery. With motor function intact there is time for catheter-directed thrombolysis or thrombectomy if no contraindication; otherwise Fogarty embolectomy. Then long-term anticoagulation for the AF.

Q: Six hours after a femoral embolectomy the calf is tense, passive dorsiflexion of the toes is excruciating and the first web space is numb. Diagnosis and treatment?

Answer

Compartment syndrome from reperfusion injury — emergency fasciotomy.

Q: A 65-year-old has progressive claudication on the golf course and an ABI of 0.7. What is the best initial management, and which drug addition most reduces cardiovascular and limb events?

Answer

Supervised exercise therapy with smoking cessation, a high-intensity statin and BP control. For events, low-dose aspirin plus rivaroxaban 2.5 mg twice daily reduces both MACE and MALE. Cilostazol helps walking distance but not events, and is contraindicated in heart failure.

Q: A patient with claudication asks whether he is more likely to lose his leg or have a heart attack in the next 5 years.

Answer

A heart attack, stroke or death (about 20% MI or stroke and 15–30% death over 5 years) is far likelier than limb loss (critical limb ischemia 1–2%). Treat PAD as high-risk ASCVD.
