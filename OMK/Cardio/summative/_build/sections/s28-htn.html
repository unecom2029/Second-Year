Hypertension: Subtypes, Secondary Causes and Resistance

Hypertension is “the silent killer”: it is invisible until it has damaged the heart, brain, kidney or eye. About 90–95% is primary (essential); the rest is secondary, and Dr. Segal’s case was built to teach when to go looking for it. His rule for the work-up: “don’t throw a net of labs and hope to catch the fish” — think through the history and exam, then order what the differential needs.

Measuring it properly

  • Seated with feet flat and back supported, after 5 minutes of rest (an exam table does not count); no caffeine, exercise or smoking for 30 minutes; empty bladder; arm supported at heart level; nobody talking; correct cuff; check both arms and use the higher.
  • A cuff over clothing can be off by 5–50 mm Hg. Crossed legs add 2–8; a small cuff 2–10; a full bladder, talking or an unsupported arm about 10 each.

  • Primary (essential) hypertension: many small genetic variants in BP control (such as sodium sensitivity), moderated by lifestyle — mostly sodium intake. One teaspoon of salt holds about 2,330 mg of sodium, the whole daily limit. High sodium causes oxidative stress and endothelial dysfunction (less nitric oxide) and stiffens large arteries.
  • The baroreceptors reset to whatever pressure is held long enough — why the body defends the high pressure, and why follow-up after starting a drug is in 2–4 weeks.
  • Contexts that change the drug (Section 29): diabetes or CKD with albuminuria, heart failure, a recent MI, AF, pregnancy, osteoporosis or kidney stones, benign prostatic hyperplasia.

What chronic hypertension does

  • Vessels (Gardner): chronic endothelial injury lets plasma proteins pile up under the endothelium (hyaline arteriolosclerosis), smooth muscle proliferates, and arterioles can no longer relax — loss of end-organ autoregulation and, paradoxically, end-organ hypoxia. Malignant hypertension gives hyperplastic “onion-skin” arteriolosclerosis with fibrinoid necrosis (Section 11).
  • Heart: higher afterload, concentric LVH, diastolic then systolic failure; accelerated coronary disease.
  • Brain: stroke and hemorrhage; cognitive decline. Kidney: nephrosclerosis. Eye: retinopathy (arteriolar narrowing, AV nicking, hemorrhages and exudates, papilledema). Aorta: aneurysm and dissection.
⚠️ The treatment trap

Because the arterioles are fixed-narrow and autoregulation has shifted, dropping blood pressure quickly to “normal” can under-perfuse the brain — common when elderly patients start drugs for the first time.

Secondary causes: labs and exam clues ★ HY

When to look — new or uncontrolled hypertension plus any of:

  • Drug-resistant or drug-induced hypertension
  • Abrupt onset, or onset before age 30
  • Worsening of previously controlled hypertension
  • Target-organ damage out of proportion to the degree of hypertension
  • Accelerated or malignant hypertension
  • New diastolic hypertension at 65 or older
  • Unprovoked or excessive hypokalemia

Dr. Segal’s 26-year-old with a pressure of 200/110 (120/80 a year before) ticked two boxes at once: onset under 30, and abrupt.

🩺 The Cushing reflex

High systolic pressure with a normal diastolic and a bradycardia: think of an intracranial cause (raised ICP). The body raises systolic pressure to perfuse the brain; the baroreceptors answer with bradycardia. Board add-on The full Cushing triad adds irregular respirations.

Hypokalemia, renin and aldosterone — reading the pattern

Dr. Segal’s patient: Na⁺ 144, K⁺ 3.1, bicarbonate 29 — unprovoked hypokalemia with a metabolic alkalosis in a young person with abrupt, severe hypertension.

  • What aldosterone does: at the principal cell it binds the mineralocorticoid receptor and makes more ENaC (sodium in), ROMK (potassium out) and Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase; intercalated cells secrete hydrogen ion. Result: sodium retention, hypokalemia and metabolic alkalosis.
  • Cortisol can also bind the receptor; 11β-HSD2 protects it by converting cortisol to cortisone. Licorice blocks that enzyme.

Primary aldosteronism — “something you’re going to see on boards”

  • The most common curable endocrine cause of hypertension: about 5–14% in primary care and 36% of resistant hypertension. Hypokalemia is seen in only a minority — a normal potassium does not exclude it.
  • Subtypes: bilateral idiopathic hyperplasia about 60%, aldosterone-producing (Conn) adenoma about 30%, rarer familial and malignant forms.
  • Who to screen: resistant hypertension, hypokalemia, an adrenal nodule, AF, sleep apnea, early stroke in the family. The 2025 Endocrine Society says everyone with hypertension; the 2025 ACC/AHA says resistant hypertension regardless of potassium. Dr. Segal’s honest answer: “I don’t think so… that’s a little bit much” — he screens when patients start to look resistant.
  • The test: a morning, seated aldosterone-to-renin ratio on an unrestricted diet, after correcting the potassium (hypokalemia suppresses aldosterone). Positive is about ARR 30 or more with aldosterone 10 ng/dL or more. The ratio means something only when renin is suppressed and aldosterone is inappropriately high.
  • Drug interference: beta-blockers suppress renin (false positive); ACEi, ARBs and diuretics raise it (false negative). MRAs must be stopped for 4 weeks; control BP meanwhile with α-blockers, non-dihydropyridine CCBs or hydralazine.
  • Management: if the patient wants and can have surgery, adrenal CT plus adrenal venous sampling to lateralise (CT alone misclassifies). Unilateral: laparoscopic adrenalectomy. Bilateral: medical — sodium restriction, spironolactone (eplerenone if gynecomastia), then amiloride; titrate until renin is no longer suppressed.
  • Why it matters beyond the number: against matched essential hypertension, PA raises the odds of atrial fibrillation 3.5-fold, proteinuria 2.7, stroke 2.6, LVH 2.3 and heart failure 2.1. “It’s not something that you just let smolder.”

Resistant hypertension

  • Definition: BP above goal despite 3 drugs of different classes at maximally tolerated doses, ideally including a diuretic — or controlled only on 4 or more.
  • Exclude pseudo-resistance first: poor technique (the cuff over clothing), white-coat effect (check home or ambulatory readings), non-adherence (the commonest reason; single pills and once-daily dosing help), and interfering drugs and substances (NSAIDs, decongestants, alcohol, stimulants, licorice, high salt).
  • Then look for a secondary cause — the biggest in resistant hypertension are sleep apnea (over 30%) and primary aldosteronism (6–23%).
  • Board add-on The usual fourth drug is spironolactone, which works even without proven aldosteronism.

Q: A 26-year-old with no history has a BP of 200/110 (120/80 a year ago), potassium 3.1 and bicarbonate 29. Which two features should make you look for a secondary cause, and what is the leading suspect?

Answer

Onset before 30 and abrupt onset (plus unprovoked hypokalemia). With hypokalemia and metabolic alkalosis, the leading suspect is primary aldosteronism: check an aldosterone-to-renin ratio after correcting the potassium.

Q: A patient on metoprolol has an ARR of 45 with aldosterone 8 ng/dL and renin 0.18 ng/mL/h. Is this primary aldosteronism?

Answer

Not established. The beta-blocker suppresses renin and inflates the ratio (a false positive), and aldosterone is not inappropriately high. The ARR counts only when renin is suppressed and aldosterone is non-suppressed.

Q: A 19-year-old with severe hypertension, potassium 2.9 and metabolic alkalosis has low renin and low aldosterone; his father had early hypertension. Amiloride normalises his BP. Diagnosis?

Answer

Liddle syndrome — a gain-of-function ENaC mutation: mineralocorticoid-like physiology without aldosterone, treated with an ENaC blocker.

Q: A 31-year-old woman with no atherosclerotic risk factors has new hypertension and an epigastric bruit. Renin and aldosterone are both high. Most likely cause and the classic angiogram?

Answer

Fibromuscular dysplasia of the renal artery — renovascular (secondary) hypertension with secondary aldosteronism. The angiogram shows a “string of beads.”

Q: A patient with a posterior fossa tumour develops a BP of 190/80 and a heart rate of 48. Why?

Answer

The Cushing reflex from raised intracranial pressure: systolic pressure rises to perfuse the brain, and the baroreceptors respond with bradycardia.

Q: A patient’s BP is 168/100 on amlodipine, lisinopril and hydrochlorothiazide at full doses. What must you check before calling this resistant hypertension, and what are the two commonest secondary causes to look for next?

Answer

Exclude pseudo-resistance: measurement technique, white-coat effect (home or ambulatory readings), adherence, and interfering substances (NSAIDs, decongestants, alcohol, salt). Then screen for obstructive sleep apnea and primary aldosteronism.

Q: Two patients have a BP of 210/124. One has a headache and nothing else; the other is confused with papilledema and a rising creatinine. Classify each and say how fast to lower the pressure.

Answer

The first is hypertensive urgency — oral drugs, lowered over hours to days. The second is a hypertensive emergency (encephalopathy, retinopathy, kidney injury) — ICU and IV drugs, lowering MAP by no more than about 25% in the first hour, because autoregulation has shifted and a sudden drop can cause cerebral ischemia.
