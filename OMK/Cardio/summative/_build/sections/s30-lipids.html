Dyslipidemias: Types and Pathophysiology

Dr. Scully’s point in reviewing the dyslipidemias: much of the stigma is about diet, but lipid metabolism has many genetic determinants that lifestyle cannot fix, and many dyslipidemias are secondary to something else that can be treated. This section covers how lipoproteins move, the familial disorders the boards use, the secondary causes, and the diet. The drugs are in Section 31.

Lipoprotein metabolism

Lipids are insoluble, so they travel as lipoproteins: a core of triglyceride and cholesteryl ester inside a coat of phospholipid, free cholesterol and apolipoproteins.

  • Why LDL matters most: the hepatic LDL receptor is the main exit for LDL from the blood. Anything that lowers liver cholesterol (statins) or protects the receptor (PCSK9 inhibitors) raises LDL-receptor numbers and lowers serum LDL.
  • PCSK9 binds the LDL receptor and sends it for degradation instead of recycling. People with loss-of-function PCSK9 mutations have low LDL and less cardiovascular disease — the genetic clue that produced a drug class.
  • Too much LDL enters the intima and is oxidised; macrophages eat it and become foam cells; plaques form and rupture, giving a site for thrombosis — why lipid drugs and antiplatelet drugs go together.

Describing the dyslipidemias

The primary dyslipidemias (slide 3 — “not something I want you to memorise”)

  • Hypercholesterolemia (high LDL): familial (LDL-receptor defects), familial defective apoB-100, polygenic.
  • Hypertriglyceridemia: familial; familial LPL deficiency; apoC-II deficiency.
  • Mixed: familial combined; dysbetalipoproteinemia; lysosomal acid lipase deficiency.
  • HDL disorders (rare): apoA-I, ABCA1 (Tangier disease), LCAT defects.

Familial hypercholesterolemia at the bedside

  • Tendon xanthomas and corneal arcus before 45 are highly specific for FH; xanthelasma has low specificity (Dr. Watkins).
  • Any of these should prompt lipid testing, an FH clinical score and cascade screening of relatives. Eruptive xanthomas point instead to very high triglycerides.
  • Measure lipoprotein(a) once, especially with a family history of early disease.

  • Which lipid, which drug (slide 6): high LDL — a statin; high TG — diet, omega-3 fatty acids, fibrates; low HDL — niacin (fibrates a little), though treating isolated low HDL is contested.

The dietary approach

  • Diet can lower total cholesterol by about 25%, depending on adherence and the genetic basis (Dr. Scully). Lifestyle will not be enough for everyone, so drugs are widely used.
  • The full diet evidence — the 2019 primary-prevention nutrition recommendations, the Mediterranean diet and PREDIMED (about 30% fewer heart attacks, strokes and cardiovascular deaths), and DASH and sodium — is in Section 4. The points for lipids:

Q: A patient with new hypercholesterolemia also has fatigue, cold intolerance and weight gain. What should you check before starting a statin, and why?

Answer

Thyroid function. Hypothyroidism reduces LDL-receptor expression and is a secondary cause of hypercholesterolemia; treating it may correct the lipids.

Q: A 34-year-old has Achilles tendon thickening, corneal arcus and an LDL of 260. His father died of an MI at 42. Diagnosis, defect, inheritance — and what should happen to his relatives?

Answer

Familial hypercholesterolemia (type II): absent or defective LDL receptors (or apoB-100), autosomal dominant. Tendon xanthomas and arcus before 45 are highly specific. His first-degree relatives need cascade screening.

Q: A child has recurrent pancreatitis, eruptive xanthomas, hepatosplenomegaly and milky serum. Which lipoprotein is raised, what is the defect, and is atherosclerosis risk increased?

Answer

Type I hyperchylomicronemia — lipoprotein lipase or apoC-II deficiency, autosomal recessive. Chylomicrons and TG are raised. Atherosclerosis risk is not increased; the danger is pancreatitis. Treat with a very low-fat diet.

Q: Which two antihypertensive classes are listed as causes of secondary hypertriglyceridemia, and name two causes of secondary hypercholesterolemia?

Answer

Beta-blockers and diuretics. Secondary hypercholesterolemia: hypothyroidism and nephrotic syndrome (also cholestasis, anorexia, corticosteroids, protease inhibitors).

Q: How does a loss-of-function mutation in PCSK9 protect against heart disease?

Answer

PCSK9 normally sends the LDL receptor for degradation. Without it, receptors recycle to the hepatocyte surface, more LDL is cleared, serum LDL is lifelong low and cardiovascular disease is less common — the observation behind the PCSK9-inhibitor drugs.

Q: A patient with a triglyceride level of 480 and a normal LDL asks what to change in his diet. What do you advise?

Answer

Cut refined carbohydrate, sugary drinks and alcohol, lose weight, and eat more cold-water fish (omega-3s), within a Mediterranean-style diet; exercise. Look for secondary causes (diabetes, alcohol, hypothyroidism, drugs such as beta-blockers, thiazides and estrogen).
