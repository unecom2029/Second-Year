Infective Endocarditis: Epidemiology, Pathogenesis and Organisms

Infective endocarditis is an infection of the endocardial surface — mostly the native or prosthetic valves, but also septal defects, shunts and intravascular devices. It is non-contagious and, untreated, almost invariably fatal. Dr. Gardner’s summary: it is “easily missed, and usually easy to treat.” The microbiology is the core of the lecture: which organism, which patient, which valve.

The formal modern scheme is etiology-based, but clinicians, UpToDate and the 2023 Duke criteria still use acute versus subacute as a bedside read of speed and virulence. “Don’t correct your attending.”

The numbers

  • Incidence in the US about 1–3 per 100,000 (lecture; the outline says 3–7), rising slightly — the highest among Western countries.
  • Mortality 15–20% in hospital, up to 40% at one year. About half eventually need valve surgery.
  • The flip of the antibiotic era: acute disease was about 20% and subacute 75%; now in industrialised countries about 75% is acute, mostly S. aureus.

💉 Why injection drug use is so strong a risk (a USMLE favourite)
  • Younger patients (20–30), men more than women.
  • Many injections a day with skin contamination, contaminated diluents and shared needles; saliva as a diluent brings in mouth flora.
  • More likely to be colonised with S. aureus, so S. aureus is the most common organism in people who inject drugs.
  • Particulates such as talc damage the tricuspid and pulmonic endothelium, and injected bacteria reach the right heart first.

Pathogenesis and lesions by organ

Normal endocardium resists bacteria. You need two things: an endocardial injury and a bacteremia.

  1. Endocardial injury. A congenital defect, turbulent flow (high to low velocity), a leaky valve, catheter trauma, or injected talc.
  2. A sterile platelet–fibrin nidus forms on the exposed collagen — a “sterile vegetation” (non-bacterial thrombotic endocarditis). No bugs yet.
  3. Transient bacteremia from any traumatised colonised mucosa: dental work, toothbrushing, GI, urinary or obstetric procedures. Usually cleared.
  4. Bacteria stick to the nidus and colonise it; more platelets and fibrin are laid down, and antibodies form immune complexes.
  5. Biofilm and vegetation: a sheath of fibrin, leukocytes, red cells, platelets and up to 109 bacteria per gram.
🧠 Why endocarditis is so hard to cure — biofilm

The best endocarditis pathogens, S. aureus and S. epidermidis, make biofilm. Biofilm bacteria are about 500× more antibiotic-resistant: drugs and antibodies penetrate poorly, and the bacteria slow or stop growing — while most antibiotics need actively growing bacteria. That is why treatment must be bactericidal and long (Section 22).

What the vegetation does — three ways

🩸

Seeding

  • Continuous, low-level bacteremia that seeds distant sites — osteomyelitis, septic arthritis, abscesses. S. aureus infects anything it touches.
💥

Destruction

  • Cytokines, proteases and bacteria destroy the valve: perforation, chordal rupture, ring abscess, aneurysm. New regurgitation and heart failure; heart block if an abscess reaches the conduction system.
🎯

Emboli

  • Friable vegetations break off as septic emboli: stroke, cerebral hemorrhage, brain abscess, mycotic aneurysm, infarcts of the spleen and kidney.

Endocarditis can also present as severe sepsis or septic shock, especially with toxin-producing organisms (S. aureus, S. pyogenes). And because the presentation ranges so widely, clinicians often chase the consequence — a “renal problem,” a stroke — instead of the source.

⚠️ The IDU valve on boards versus in the pediatric case

The classic board answer for a person who injects drugs is the tricuspid valve with S. aureus. The pediatric lecture’s homeless 17-year-old had a vegetation on the mitral valve. Both happen; for a question, tricuspid plus septic pulmonary emboli is the expected pattern.

Culture-negative endocarditis

  • The most common reason is antibiotics given before blood cultures, which mask ordinary staphylococci, streptococci and enterococci. So always draw three sets first.
  • The organisms that will not grow on agar: Coxiella burnetii (Q fever — farm animals, needs cell culture) and Bartonella (henselae from cats, quintana in homeless people with body lice — very slow). Also Chlamydophila, Tropheryma whipplei, Legionella, Mycoplasma, Brucella.
  • HACEK organisms grow slowly and were once a classic culture-negative cause; modern systems usually grow them. Board add-on
  • PCR has cut the culture-negative rate and shown that Coxiella and Bartonella are the commonest. Serology counts too: a Coxiella phase I IgG over 1:800 is a Duke major criterion.
  • About 25% of endocarditis is now polymicrobial.

Q: A patient develops high fever, rigors and rapidly worsening heart failure over 10 days on a previously normal valve. Which organism tops the differential, and how broad should empiric therapy be?

Answer

Acute endocarditis from Staphylococcus aureus, a “professional pathogen” that attacks normal valves. Empiric therapy must be broad, covering MRSA.

Q: A febrile man who injects heroin has cough, hemoptysis and multiple peripheral cavitating nodules on chest CT, but no murmur. Diagnosis, valve and organism?

Answer

Right-sided (tricuspid) endocarditis with septic pulmonary emboli, most likely S. aureus. Right-sided disease often has no audible murmur.

Q: A 68-year-old with iron-deficiency anemia has endocarditis, and blood cultures grow a non-enterococcal group D streptococcus. What else must you investigate?

Answer

Streptococcus gallolyticus (bovis) endocarditis is associated with colorectal cancer and inflammatory bowel disease — get a colonoscopy.

Q: Three months after an aortic valve replacement, a patient develops subacute endocarditis. Most likely organism, and its key virulence factor?

Answer

Staphylococcus epidermidis (coagulase-negative), introduced from the skin and protected by biofilm on the prosthetic material.

Q: A shepherd with fevers has echo-proven vegetations but three negative blood cultures. A second patient, homeless with body lice, has the same picture. Likely organisms, and how is the first confirmed?

Answer

The shepherd: Coxiella burnetii (Q fever), confirmed by PCR or a phase I IgG over 1:800, which is a Duke major criterion. The homeless patient: Bartonella quintana. Both are culture-negative causes.

Q: Why does endocarditis need both a damaged endothelium and a bacteremia, and why does a dentist ask about heart disease?

Answer

Normal endocardium resists bacteria; injury exposes collagen, on which a sterile platelet–fibrin nidus forms for bacteria to stick to. A dental extraction throws a large bolus of mouth bacteria, including viridans streptococci, into the blood — so patients with high-risk valve disease get prophylactic antibiotics.
