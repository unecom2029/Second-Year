Section 8 · The ECG & Arrhythmias

Arrhythmias and Heart Block: Lethal Versus Non-Lethal

Eleven study-guide objectives point at this section, which tells you how much of the exam lives here. Two questions sort nearly every fast rhythm in the lecture: is the QRS narrow or wide, and is the rhythm regular or irregular. Two more decide what you do about it: is the patient stable, and what does the history say. Everything below hangs on those four.

How arrhythmias start: three mechanisms

The electrophysiologist’s framing was that you never get away from the action potential. Every arrhythmia in this section is one of three failures, and knowing which one you are looking at tells you which drug class is going to help (Section 10).

MechanismWhat goes wrongClassic examples
Automaticity, enhanced or abnormalA focus fires too fast, or fires somewhere it should not. A problem of phase 3 and phase 4.Atrial tachycardia from an irritable focus; junctional ectopic tachycardia
Triggered activitySmall depolarizations during or just after repolarization reach threshold and trigger a whole new action potential.Early afterdepolarizations cause torsades in long QT; delayed afterdepolarizations cause ischemic ventricular arrhythmias and digoxin toxicity
Re-entry — the most common, and the usual mechanism of supraventricular tachycardiaA short circuit between two pathways with different conduction speeds and different refractory periods.AVNRT, AVRT, atrial flutter, ventricular tachycardia around a scar
🔑 Re-entry in four steps — worth being able to draw

Picture a pathway that divides into two limbs. The slow path conducts slowly but recovers quickly (a short refractory period). The fast path conducts quickly but recovers slowly (a long refractory period).

  1. A normal beat travels down both limbs and meets itself at the bottom. Nothing happens.
  2. A premature beat arrives while the fast path is still refractory, so it is blocked there and goes down the slow path only. This is unidirectional block.
  3. By the time it reaches the bottom, the fast path has recovered, so the impulse turns around and runs back up it.
  4. It re-enters the slow path and the circuit keeps spinning. Each lap throws off a new impulse, which overdrives the sinoatrial node.

That is why a premature beat starts the tachycardia when a normal beat does not: only a premature beat finds one limb still refractory.

🩺 “What else is going on?” — the setting that permits an arrhythmia

An arrhythmia almost never arrives out of nowhere, and examiners know it. Look for ischemia or an old infarct scar (examiners love an arrhythmia paired with a myocardial infarction), electrolyte disturbance in potassium or magnesium, drug-induced proarrhythmia — digoxin causes a whole host of arrhythmias and remains a test-writer favourite even though it is used less now — and autonomic tone, which changes the QT, the heart-rate variability and the conduction velocities themselves.

Atrioventricular block: measure the same three things every time ★ HY

A normal PR interval is 200 ms or less, which is one big box. A long PR means slowed conduction through the atrioventricular node. Dr. Qazi’s rule for every block is to measure the P–P interval, the R–R interval and the PR interval, in that order, and let the pattern name itself.

🧠 In every atrioventricular block, the P–P interval is constant

That is the sanity check. The sinus node is doing its job perfectly in all four blocks — the failure is downstream. If the P waves do not march out, you are looking at something other than heart block, and you should go back to the rhythm step in Section 6.

⚠️ 2:1 atrioventricular block — the trick tracing
  • When every other P wave is blocked, there are never two conducted P waves in a row, so you have nothing to compare and you cannot tell Mobitz I from Mobitz II. Call it “2:1 atrioventricular block” and let the history decide.
  • An asymptomatic 25-year-old is probably Mobitz I and benign. An 80-year-old with syncope or exhaustion is treated as Mobitz II and gets a pacemaker. A young patient with syncope should raise congenital heart block — ask about the family history.
  • Tip: look for the hidden P wave sitting inside the T wave — it looks exactly like the other P waves once you find it.

Narrow-complex tachycardias: the supraventricular family

🎯 The sentence to carry out of this lecture

“Supraventricular tachycardia is a category, not a diagnosis.”

Fast, narrow and arising above the ventricles is a family. Naming the member matters because the treatment differs.

🔑 Why typical AVNRT has no visible P wave

A premature beat goes down the slow pathway and back up the fast one. Coming up the fast pathway, it reaches the atria at almost exactly the moment it is also reaching the ventricles, so the atria and ventricles depolarize together and the P wave is buried inside the QRS. That single mechanical fact is why “regular, narrow, no P wave” is such a reliable clue.

Breaking a regular supraventricular tachycardia, step by step

  1. Is the patient stable? If not, stop here: sedate well and perform synchronised cardioversion. The lecturer’s view on energy is “go big or go home,” because being shocked is brutal and you do not want to repeat it; guidelines list 50 to 100 joules.
  2. Vagal manoeuvres first if the patient is stable with a good blood pressure. Valsalva (having the patient try to blow the plunger out of a syringe is easier to explain than “bear down”), cold water, tilting, or carotid sinus massage.
  3. Adenosine, 6 mg by rapid push with an immediate flush, then 12 mg if there is no response in a minute or two.
  4. If that fails and the patient is still stable: an intravenous beta-blocker, diltiazem or verapamil — all of which slow the atrioventricular node.
  5. If that fails: synchronised cardioversion.
  6. Long term: an electrophysiology study with catheter ablation is a class I recommendation for suitable patients, done from the groin, modifying the slow pathway. Otherwise, medical therapy with nodal blockers. Flecainide or propafenone only if there is no structural heart disease. Many young patients choose pills first and come back for ablation after two or three years of emergency visits.
🩺 Carotid sinus massage, done safely

Pressure on the carotid sinus triggers a parasympathetic reflex that slows conduction through the atrioventricular node and can break AVNRT. The technique is to listen to both carotids first, then press firmly for 10 to 20 seconds with the electrocardiogram, heart rate and blood pressure monitored.

Absolute contraindications: recent myocardial infarction; stroke or transient ischemic attack within the last three months; carotid artery occlusion; a history of ventricular tachycardia or fibrillation; a previous bad reaction to the manoeuvre. Relative: a carotid bruit — image the carotids first, because stenosis or atheroma risks occluding the other side or embolising plaque.

⚠️ A slip in the recording: sinus, not body

The recording refers to the “carotid body” having parasympathetic tone. The manoeuvre actually works on the carotid sinus baroreceptors, with cranial nerve IX carrying the afferent signal and the vagus the efferent. The carotid body is a chemoreceptor, sensing oxygen and carbon dioxide. Section 5 has the reflex in full.

Atrial flutter

  • Negative sawtooth flutter waves in II, III and aVF, and positive in V1. That combination defines typical, counterclockwise flutter.
  • The circuit is a macro-re-entry loop running counterclockwise around the tricuspid valve, climbing the muscular septum, and it depends on the cavotricuspid isthmus — the strip of tissue between the inferior vena cava and the tricuspid annulus.
  • Why the waves point that way: an electrode records current coming toward it as positive. The wavefront climbs the thick septum away from the feet, so the inferior leads see it going away and record it as negative. It climbs toward V1, high on the right chest, so V1 records it as positive.
  • Why flutter often runs faster than fibrillation. In flutter the atria fire at an organised 250–300, and the node settles into a 2:1 ratio, locking the ventricle at about 150. In fibrillation the atria fire chaotically at 360–450 or more, bombarding the node, which conducts irregularly at roughly 120 to 150 and is easier to slow with drugs. Days of flutter at a fixed 150 produce a tachycardia-mediated cardiomyopathy.
  • Long term: catheter ablation of the cavotricuspid isthmus, usually offered after even a single episode because the rate runs so high. The lecturer’s image is a traffic circle with a Jersey barrier dropped into it. Warn the patient that ablation does not prevent atrial fibrillation — about half of flutter patients develop it within roughly two years.
🔑 Adenosine in flutter is diagnostic, not therapeutic

When a fast narrow tachycardia has its P waves hidden and you cannot tell flutter from AVNRT, adenosine blocks the atrioventricular node for a few seconds and unmasks the sawtooth flutter waves underneath. It will not stop the flutter, because the circuit does not use the atrioventricular node. It does stop AVNRT, which does. So the response to adenosine itself tells you which rhythm you had.

Wide-complex tachycardia: ventricular tachycardia versus supraventricular tachycardia ★ HY

Why ventricular tachycardia is wide:

  • A normal beat rides the His–Purkinje superhighway, so both ventricles activate at once and the QRS is under 120 ms.
  • Ventricular tachycardia starts in the ventricular muscle itself — a focus, or a circuit around a scar — so it skips the conduction system and creeps cell to cell, giving a QRS of 120 ms or more that looks slurred and, in the lecturer’s word, ugly.
  • The catch: a supraventricular rhythm conducted through a bundle branch block is also travelling cell to cell for part of the ventricle, so it is also wide.
⚠️ The rule that is “constantly on the test”

If it is wide and you cannot tell, assume ventricular tachycardia, treat it as ventricular tachycardia, and work it out afterwards. If the patient is unstable, sedate and cardiovert. In the lecturer’s words, nobody will ever mark you wrong for treating a supraventricular tachycardia with left bundle branch block as ventricular tachycardia — but the reverse mistake kills people. Having a baseline tracing, so you know whether the bundle branch block was already there, helps enormously.

🫀

The clinical setting favours ventricular tachycardia

  • Organic heart disease — an ejection fraction of 20% against a normal of 55 to 65%, or an old infarct scar.
  • Potassium disturbance, with low potassium the worse one for ventricular irritability.
  • Hypoxia from infarction or lung disease, and acidosis.
  • Valve disease, including mitral valve prolapse.
  • Drugs: digoxin toxicity, antiarrhythmics, phenothiazines, tricyclic antidepressants, caffeine, alcohol, nicotine.
  • It does occur in structurally normal hearts, where it behaves more like a supraventricular tachycardia — but if you do not know the history, treat it as life-threatening.
📐

Findings on the tracing that favour it

  • Atrioventricular dissociation — P waves marching at their own rate straight through the tachycardia. Present in 25 to 33% of cases.
  • Capture or fusion beats — a sinus impulse sneaks through between ventricular beats and briefly captures the normal conduction, giving one narrow beat, or a hybrid. Only about 5% of cases, but almost diagnostic.
  • Negative precordial concordance — every QRS negative from V1 through V6, meaning activation starts at the apex and moves away from all the chest leads. Virtually diagnostic.
  • Positive concordance — all upright V1 to V6, an origin at the base. Strongly suggestive, though an accessory pathway can do it too.
  • A “northwest” axis, pointing up toward the right shoulder, so that aVR is strikingly positive.
  • A very wide QRS, over 140 ms with a right bundle shape or over 160 ms with a left bundle shape.

The lecturer’s advice for now is to concentrate on capture and fusion beats and on atrioventricular dissociation and leave the rest for your clinical years.

🧠 The initial deflection tells you which pathway was used
  • A supraventricular rhythm with a bundle branch block starts on the normal conduction system, so the first part of the QRS is sharp and fast and only the later part slurs. Ventricular tachycardia is slow and slurred from the very first millisecond.
  • Ventricular tachycardia can be irregular at onset — about 70% become regular by the twentieth beat — so regularity does not rule it out.
  • It can conduct backwards to the atria, so look at any P waves carefully before calling them dissociated.
🔑 The Brugada stepwise approach (1991), validated on 554 wide tachycardias

Work the steps in order. The first “yes” diagnoses ventricular tachycardia and you stop.

  1. Is there an absence of any RS complex in all the precordial leads?
  2. Is the longest R-to-S interval over 100 ms? Measure from the onset of the R wave to the nadir, the deepest point, of the S wave in any precordial lead.
  3. Is there atrioventricular dissociation?
  4. Do the classical morphology criteria in V1 and V6 fit?

The lecturer said explicitly that he does not expect you to know Wellens’ detailed morphology table, nor would it be tested — it is for your clinical years.

Wolff–Parkinson–White ★ HY

The lecturer flagged this as “highly tested, and life-threatening in a young person.” An accessory pathway — the bundle of Kent — connects atrium to ventricle directly, bypassing the atrioventricular node.

🚧

The atrioventricular node is a toll booth

  • In the lecturer’s description, “the most passive-aggressive structure you will ever see”: the faster the atria fire, the more it slows conduction down.
  • It will usually pass about 150 to 160 beats per minute at most, perhaps 200 in someone aged 18 to 25.
  • This is a protective property, and it is the thing an accessory pathway takes away.
🏎️

The accessory pathway has no toll booth

  • It conducts everything it receives, as fast as it receives it.
  • In atrial fibrillation, blocking the node simply sends more impulses down the pathway instead, so the ventricular rate climbs, and very fast rates degenerate into ventricular fibrillation.
  • That is the entire reason for the management rule below.
  • On the resting tracing: bypassing the node removes the isoelectric PR segment, giving a short PR. Early ventricular activation creeps cell to cell, giving a slurred upstroke — the delta wave — and a wider QRS.
  • Pattern versus syndrome. Pattern is pre-excitation on the tracing with no symptoms, present in 0.15 to 0.25% of people and in 0.55% of first-degree relatives; it can be intermittent or disappear with age. Syndrome is the pattern plus an arrhythmia. In an aviation study of 22,500 people, the pattern was present in 0.25% and only 1.8% of those had an arrhythmia.
  • Of the arrhythmias in the syndrome, roughly 80% are AVRT, 15 to 30% atrial fibrillation and about 5% flutter. Sudden death risk is 0–0.39% and is especially low in the asymptomatic. Multiple pathways, found in up to 13%, mark higher risk.
  • Who gets studied: symptomatic patients with palpitations or lightheadedness get an electrophysiology study to measure how fast the pathway conducts. Asymptomatic patients can be watched with a yearly tracing and taught the warning symptoms.
  • Associations: most patients have structurally normal hearts. When there is congenital disease it tends to be right-sided, and Ebstein anomaly — the tricuspid valve displaced down into the right ventricle — is the lesion most strongly tied to Wolff–Parkinson–White. Mitral valve prolapse goes with left-sided pathways, and familial cases with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy.
⚠️ No nodal blockers in Wolff–Parkinson–White — the one thing to know

No adenosine, no calcium channel blockers, no beta-blockers, and no vagal manoeuvres. Every one of those slows the node and pushes more conduction down the accessory pathway. This reverses the usual supraventricular tachycardia algorithm, which is exactly why it is tested so often.

What you do instead: when in doubt, sedate and cardiovert. The drug of choice is procainamide, given at 30 mg/min up to a maximum of 17 mg/kg, then 2 to 4 mg/min for maintenance. Stop the infusion when the arrhythmia is suppressed, if the patient becomes hypotensive, or if the QRS widens by 50% or more. Watch for hypotension, QRS widening and torsades.

Long QT and the genetic arrhythmia syndromes

The case that opens this part of the lecture is a 24-year-old runner who “went down with a seizure.” The tracing showed torsades de pointes at about 300 per minute, with the axis twisting around a point so the amplitude waxed and waned. The seizure was cerebral hypoperfusion — patients in cardiac arrest often convulse. After an automated external defibrillator shock, the sinus tracing showed a QT of roughly 600 ms.

  • Congenital long QT syndrome affects about 1 in 5,000 and is mostly autosomal dominant. More than fifteen genes are known; types 1 through 3 account for 85 to 90%. Most are potassium-channel defects affecting phases 2 and 3, which lengthen the action potential, allow early afterdepolarizations, and set off torsades.
  • It presents with recurrent syncope — in the lecturer’s words, “they just go down,” not subtle — and with sudden cardiac death.
  • The Schwartz score combines the tracing, the clinical history and the family history: 1 point is low probability, 2 to 3 intermediate, and 4 or more high.
  • Board add-on Acute torsades is treated with intravenous magnesium sulfate, with cardioversion if the patient is unstable. Correct the potassium and magnesium and stop every QT-prolonging drug.

Palpitations and syncope: what the work-up has to exclude

Two of the objectives for this section are phrased as excluding a dysrhythmia or heart block in a patient with palpitations or syncope. Put the pieces above into an order.

  1. A twelve-lead tracing in sinus rhythm. It is the cheapest test in medicine — the lecturer’s line was that nothing for twenty dollars gives you more information. Look specifically for a delta wave (Wolff–Parkinson–White), a long QT, the coved ST elevation of Brugada, voltage criteria suggesting hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, Q waves from an old infarction, and the PR interval and QRS width for conduction disease.
  2. A rhythm strip during symptoms, which is what ambulatory monitoring and wearables are for. A wearable flags atrial fibrillation from irregularity of the R–R interval, so frequent premature beats can fool it, and an irregular report always needs visual confirmation.
  3. Ask about the setting. Syncope with exertion or with emotional stress in a young person points to the genetic syndromes above and to hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. Syncope with no prodrome at any age is more worrying than syncope with a long warning.
  4. Check the reversible contributors: potassium and magnesium, thyroid function, the medication list for anything that prolongs the QT, and alcohol or stimulant use.
  5. Family history of sudden death under 30, or of unexplained drowning or car accidents.
🧠 Lethal versus non-lethal, in one view

Immediately lethal: ventricular fibrillation and pulseless ventricular tachycardia, which need defibrillation; torsades, which needs magnesium and often a shock; and pre-excited atrial fibrillation, because of what it can become.

Potentially lethal, by what it leads to: Mobitz II and complete heart block can progress to asystole and need a pacemaker; sustained monomorphic ventricular tachycardia can degenerate into fibrillation; atrial fibrillation and flutter cause stroke and, left fast, a tachycardia-mediated cardiomyopathy.

Generally non-lethal: sinus arrhythmia, first-degree block, Mobitz I, a wandering atrial pacemaker, and AVNRT, which is miserable but rarely dangerous in a structurally normal heart.

Q: The P–P interval is regular and the R–R interval is regular, but the PR interval changes from beat to beat. The atrial rate is 80 and the ventricular rate is 36. What is the diagnosis?

Answer

Third-degree, or complete, atrioventricular block. Both chambers are marching out regularly but on independent schedules — atrioventricular dissociation — which is why the PR interval is meaningless here.

Q: Why does it matter whether a second-degree block is Mobitz I or Mobitz II, and what single feature is easiest to find on the strip?

Answer

Mobitz I is benign; Mobitz II is high grade and needs a pacemaker. The easiest feature is that in Mobitz I the PR interval after the dropped beat is shorter than the PR before it, which is much easier to see than the gradual lengthening. In Mobitz II the PR is constant throughout.

Q: A stable 30-year-old has a narrow regular tachycardia at 180 with no visible P waves. Vagal manoeuvres fail. What is the next step, at what dose, and what should the monitor show as it wears off?

Answer

Adenosine 6 mg by rapid intravenous push with a flush, repeated at 12 mg if needed. As it wears off you should see transient atrioventricular block — the PR lengthening and then a dropped beat — before sinus rhythm returns. The underlying rhythm is almost certainly typical AVNRT, where the P wave is buried in the QRS.

Q: Adenosine is given for a narrow tachycardia. It does not terminate the rhythm, but regular sawtooth waves appear during a few seconds of block. What does that tell you?

Answer

That the rhythm does not depend on the atrioventricular node: it is atrial flutter. Adenosine was diagnostic rather than therapeutic. Definitive therapy is cavotricuspid isthmus ablation, and the patient is anticoagulated exactly as for atrial fibrillation.

Q: During a wide regular tachycardia at 160, one narrow complex appears that looks like the patient’s normal beats. What is it, and what does it prove?

Answer

A capture beat: a sinus impulse got through the atrioventricular node while the ventricular circuit happened to be resetting. It proves the atria and ventricles are running independently — atrioventricular dissociation — and therefore that this is ventricular tachycardia.

Q: A 22-year-old has an irregular, very fast, wide-complex tachycardia whose QRS width varies from beat to beat. The blood pressure is stable. Which drugs are contraindicated, and what do you give?

Answer

This is pre-excited atrial fibrillation in Wolff–Parkinson–White. Contraindicated: adenosine, calcium channel blockers, beta-blockers and vagal manoeuvres — all of them divert conduction down the accessory pathway. Give procainamide, or sedate and cardiovert. Cardiovert immediately if the patient becomes unstable.

Q: A 19-year-old faints while swimming. The corrected QT is 520 ms and an uncle died suddenly at 25. What is the diagnosis, what is the mechanism of the fatal rhythm, and what should she avoid?

Answer

Congenital long QT syndrome, usually an autosomal dominant potassium-channel defect. A prolonged phase 2 to 3 allows early afterdepolarizations, which trigger torsades de pointes. She should avoid QT-prolonging drugs — antiemetics, antifungals, antidepressants, fluoroquinolones — and keep her potassium and magnesium normal.

Q: A stable 70-year-old with a prior myocardial infarction and an ejection fraction of 25% has a wide regular tachycardia. The QRS is negative in every lead from V1 to V6. What is the rhythm, and why?

Answer

Ventricular tachycardia. Negative precordial concordance is virtually diagnostic, and the clinical setting fits perfectly: scar from an old infarct plus a low ejection fraction is the classic substrate for re-entrant ventricular tachycardia.
