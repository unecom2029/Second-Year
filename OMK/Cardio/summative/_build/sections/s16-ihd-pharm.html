Section 16 · Atherosclerosis & Ischemia

Ischemic Heart Disease Pharmacology

Dr. Scully’s organising sentence: “We could increase supply of oxygen, or we could decrease demand — and one tends to be much easier than the other.” Plaque and endothelial dysfunction make supply hard to raise, so nearly every antianginal works by lowering demand — heart rate, contractility, preload or afterload. For each drug he wants the name, indication, mechanism, adverse effects, contraindications and interactions, applied to two cases: stable and vasospastic angina.

The treatment map

🏃

Stable (exertional) angina

  • Abort an attack: sublingual nitroglycerin.
  • Prevent: a beta-blocker (the best monotherapy), ± a calcium channel blocker or a long-acting nitrate.
  • Still refractory: ranolazine.
🌙

Vasospastic (variant, Prinzmetal) angina

  • Abort: sublingual nitroglycerin.
  • Prevent: a calcium channel blocker (a dihydropyridine or diltiazem) ± a nitrate.
  • No beta-blockers.
🛡️

Risk reduction for everyone

  • Antiplatelet therapy.
  • A statin (Section 31).
  • Smoking cessation.
  • Control of hypertension and diabetes.

Nitrates, beta blockers, calcium channel blockers and ranolazine

Nitrates — nitroglycerin, isosorbide dinitrate, isosorbide-5-mononitrate

  1. Bioactivation. The organic nitrate is converted to nitric oxide by mitochondrial aldehyde dehydrogenase (ALDH2).
  2. Signalling. Nitric oxide activates soluble guanylyl cyclase, raising cGMP, which activates protein kinase G and relaxes smooth muscle.
  3. Where. Mostly the venous capacitance vessels.
  4. Result. Lower preload, smaller end-diastolic volume, less wall stress, less oxygen demand. A small rise in supply from dilating epicardial arteries “isn’t the major driving force.”
  • Pharmacogenetics: ALDH2 polymorphisms, common in people of East Asian descent, reduce bioactivation, so nitrates work less well.
  • Adverse effects: the vasodilator trio of flushing, hypotension and headache, plus reflex tachycardia. Board add-on Methemoglobinemia; reflux from a relaxed lower esophageal sphincter.
  • Contraindication: low blood pressure.
  • Tolerance: long-acting nitrates lose effect with continuous use, so prescribe a nitrate-free interval, usually overnight (take the patch off at night). The “Monday morning headache” of munitions workers was tolerance lost over the weekend.

Long-acting nitrates are not first-line as monotherapy.

Beta-blockers — the best monotherapy for exertional angina

  • Mechanism: block β1, lowering heart rate and contractility (and wall tension and blood pressure), so oxygen demand falls. A slower rate also lengthens diastole, the time when the coronaries fill.
  • Dosing: titrate to a resting heart rate of about 50 while limiting the peak exertional rate (the lecture’s figure).
  • Adverse effects: fatigue, bradycardia, heart block, bronchospasm with non-selective drugs.
  • Contraindications: vasospastic angina; IV verapamil at the same time; cardiogenic shock; asthma or COPD is a relative contraindication.
  • Board add-on Avoid beta-blockers with intrinsic sympathomimetic activity (pindolol, acebutolol): partial agonism raises demand.

Calcium channel blockers

  • Dihydropyridines (amlodipine, nifedipine): arterial dilation lowers afterload, so the ventricle needs less work for the same output; they also dilate coronaries. Adverse effects: peripheral edema, flushing, reflex tachycardia. Board add-on Avoid short-acting nifedipine, whose reflex tachycardia can worsen ischemia.
  • Non-dihydropyridines (verapamil, diltiazem): lower afterload and directly lower heart rate and contractility. Adverse effects: bradycardia, AV block, constipation (verapamil), worsening of a low ejection fraction. Avoid in severe aortic stenosis and HOCM, and with a beta-blocker IV.
  • In vasospastic angina the calcium channel blocker is the preventive drug, and CCB plus nitrate is an effective pair.

Each drug cancels the other’s unwanted effect: the beta-blocker stops the nitrate’s reflex tachycardia, and the nitrate stops the beta-blocker’s rise in end-diastolic volume.

Ranolazine — for refractory angina

  • The problem it fixes: ischemic myocytes have an increased late-phase sodium current. Sodium loads the cell, the sodium–calcium exchanger brings in calcium, and calcium overload raises diastolic wall stress, which raises oxygen demand.
  • Mechanism: blocks the late sodium current, so less calcium overload and less diastolic wall stress.
  • It does not change heart rate or blood pressure Board add-on — useful when those are already low.
  • In the trials, women (XX) benefited more than men (XY).
  • Adverse effects: dose-dependent QT prolongation, dizziness, constipation. Board add-on A CYP3A4 substrate: avoid strong CYP3A4 inhibitors and other QT-prolonging drugs. Contraindicated in cirrhosis.
⚠️ A slide mix-up: ivabradine versus ranolazine

One Application Session 1 slide gives ivabradine’s mechanism as “inhibits the late sodium current.” That is ranolazine. Ivabradine blocks the funny current (If) through HCN channels in the sinus node, slowing the heart without changing contractility; its signature side effect is luminous visual phenomena. The heart-failure lecture gives the correct mechanism (Section 18).

Vasospastic angina: the board favourite

  • The patient: a 34-year-old with chest pain that wakes him from sleep, no pain on exertion, worse exercise tolerance in the morning, and a normal ECG between attacks. Board add-on Smoking, cocaine, amphetamines and triptans are triggers.
  • Diagnostic criteria: nitrate-responsive rest angina, especially night to early morning; hyperventilation can precipitate it; transient ischemic ECG changes (ST elevation or depression, new U waves) only during an attack; documented spasm on angiography; calcium channel blockers, but not beta-blockers, suppress it.
  • Provocation testing: hyperventilation, ergot or intracoronary acetylcholine. Acetylcholine contracts coronary smooth muscle through M3 receptors — “M1s in the CNS, M2s in the heart, M3s everywhere else.” Board add-on In healthy vessels acetylcholine acts on endothelial M3 receptors to release nitric oxide and dilate; with endothelial dysfunction the direct smooth-muscle contraction wins.
  • Why no beta-blocker: blocking β2-mediated dilation leaves unopposed α vasoconstriction, which makes spasm worse. The lecturer noted this has been contested recently but is “still ingrained in the literature and the recommendations.”

Q: A 34-year-old man wakes with chest pain several nights a month and has poor exercise tolerance in the mornings only. His ECG and troponins are normal between episodes. What do you prescribe to stop an attack, what to prevent one, and what must you avoid?

Answer

Vasospastic angina. Abort with sublingual nitroglycerin; prevent with a calcium channel blocker (amlodipine or diltiazem). Avoid beta-blockers, which leave unopposed α vasoconstriction and worsen spasm.

Q: A man taking sublingual nitroglycerin for angina asks his doctor for sildenafil. What is the danger, and what is the mechanism?

Answer

Severe, refractory hypotension. Nitrates raise cGMP through nitric oxide and guanylyl cyclase; PDE5 inhibitors block cGMP breakdown, so smooth-muscle relaxation is amplified.

Q: A patient wears a 24-hour nitroglycerin patch and finds it stops working after a week. What has happened, and what is the fix?

Answer

Nitrate tolerance. Prescribe a nitrate-free interval — take the patch off overnight.

Q: Why do nitrates relieve exertional angina mainly by acting on veins, and why does adding a beta-blocker make them work better?

Answer

Venodilation lowers preload, so end-diastolic volume, wall stress and oxygen demand all fall; the coronary effect is minor because ischemic arterioles are already maximally dilated. The fall in blood pressure triggers a baroreflex tachycardia through sympathetic β1 outflow, which the beta-blocker prevents.

Q: A patient with refractory angina, a resting heart rate of 52 and a blood pressure of 98/60 is already on a beta-blocker and a nitrate. Which antianginal can be added without lowering either number further, and what must be checked first?

Answer

Ranolazine, which blocks the late sodium current and does not change heart rate or blood pressure. Check the QT interval and the medication list for other QT-prolonging drugs and strong CYP3A4 inhibitors.

Q: A patient with stable angina also has hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy. Which antianginal class should be avoided, and why?

Answer

Nitrates (and dihydropyridine vasodilators). Lowering preload shrinks the left ventricular cavity and worsens the dynamic outflow obstruction. A beta-blocker is the right choice here.

Q: A patient with an acute inferior STEMI has a heart rate of 48 and a blood pressure of 82/50. Which two drugs from the standard bundle should be held?

Answer

The beta-blocker (bradycardia and hypotension) and nitroglycerin (hypotension, and a likely right ventricular infarct with an inferior STEMI). Aspirin, the P2Y12 inhibitor, the anticoagulant and the statin still go ahead, along with emergency PCI.
