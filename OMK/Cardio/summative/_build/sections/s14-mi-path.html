Myocardial Infarction: the Morphology and Histology Timeline

Dr. Gardner’s “infarct clock” is the most reliably tested table in cardiac pathology. Learn it as a story rather than a grid: the muscle dies, neutrophils arrive, macrophages clean up, granulation tissue fills the hole, and collagen scars it. Each stage has a look under the microscope, a look to the naked eye and — this is the clinical payoff — a complication that belongs to it (Section 15).

⚠️ The sources put the wavy fibres in different hours

The lecture slide puts wavy fibres at ½–4 hours, at the border. First Aid says 0–4 hours; the UWorld library table says 4–12 hours. Everyone agrees on what matters: wavy fibres are the first light-microscope change, seen within the first day, before neutrophils. Dr. Gardner also admitted the 0–½ hour electron-microscope changes would be hard to call blind.

🔑 The four anchors to memorise
  • Day 1 — coagulative necrosis begins; wavy fibres; looks normal or dark to the eye. Board add-on This is the window when ventricular arrhythmia kills.
  • Days 1–3 — neutrophils. Board add-on First Aid ties the early fibrinous pericarditis to this window.
  • Days 3–10 — macrophages, yellow and soft. The rupture window.
  • Weeks to months — granulation tissue, then scar. The true-aneurysm window.
⚠️ Why the timeline matters clinically

The wall is softest at about 3–7 days. Neutrophils and macrophages have digested the dead muscle, and collagen has not arrived yet. That is the rupture window for the free wall, the septum and the papillary muscle (Section 15).

What it looks like to the naked eye

  • Transmural versus subendocardial. A completely occluded artery kills the full wall thickness (the STEMI pattern). A partial or transient occlusion kills only the inner third, the subendocardium, which is the last zone the coronary blood reaches and is under the highest wall stress (the NSTEMI pattern).
  • Board add-on A very fresh infarct can be shown at autopsy with TTC stain: living muscle stains dark red-brown, the infarct stays pale because it has lost its dehydrogenase enzymes.

Reperfusion injury

Opening the artery saves muscle, but the returning blood does some damage of its own.

  • When flow returns to damaged myocytes, calcium floods in and the myofibrils hypercontract. Under the microscope that gives contraction band necrosis: dense, hypereosinophilic transverse bands across the myocytes.
  • Board add-on Returning oxygen also generates free radicals (reactive oxygen species), and the returning blood leaks through damaged capillaries, so a reperfused infarct is often hemorrhagic.
  • Contraction bands also appear at the margins of an unreperfused infarct at 12–24 hours, but a diffuse band of contraction necrosis after a stent or thrombolysis is the reperfusion picture.

Q: A man dies 2 days after a large anterior myocardial infarction. What will the infarct look like grossly and under the microscope?

Answer

Grossly, mottled with a yellow-tan centre. Microscopically, coagulative necrosis with loss of nuclei and striations and a brisk neutrophil infiltrate — the 1–3 day window.

Q: A patient gets a stent 3 hours after an LAD occlusion. A biopsy of the territory shows hypereosinophilic myocytes crossed by dense transverse bands. What is this, and what caused it?

Answer

Contraction band necrosis from reperfusion injury. Calcium floods into damaged myocytes when flow returns, and the myofibrils hypercontract.

Q: An autopsy shows an infarct whose border is filled with loose connective tissue containing many small new blood vessels, fibroblasts and a little early collagen. How old is it?

Answer

About 10–14 days — established granulation tissue with new vessels and early collagen. Macrophages dominate before that (3–10 days) and dense scar after about 2 months.

Q: Why is the left ventricular free wall most likely to rupture about 3–7 days after an infarct, rather than on day 1 or at week 3?

Answer

At 3–7 days neutrophils and macrophages have digested the dead muscle but collagen has not yet been laid down, so the wall is at its softest. On day 1 the necrotic muscle still holds its shape; by week 3 granulation tissue and collagen are reinforcing it.

Q: A 60-year-old diabetic woman has progressive heart failure but has never reported chest pain. Autopsy shows many patchy grey-white scars throughout the left ventricle. Name the entity.

Answer

Chronic ischemic heart disease from multiple silent, healed infarcts; her diabetic neuropathy blunted the pain. Grey-white scar means each infarct was more than about two months old.
