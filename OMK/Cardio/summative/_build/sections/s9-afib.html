Section 9 · The ECG & Arrhythmias

Atrial Fibrillation: Risk Stratification and Management

The electrophysiologist gave this topic its own warning: “You will not escape atrial fibrillation.” It is the most common arrhythmia in the United States and in the world, and it turns up in surgery, in primary care, in obstetrics and in the intensive care unit. Two dangers drive everything you do about it — stroke and heart failure — and the management sequence below is built around them.

Recognising it

  • Irregularly irregular R–R intervals, with no pattern you can predict.
  • No P waves and no isoelectric baseline — and the practical version of that, from the lecture, is that no two “P waves” look the same.
  • A variable ventricular rate, which may be fast or slow.
  • The QRS is usually narrow, unless there is a pre-existing bundle branch block, an accessory pathway, or rate-related aberrancy.
  • Fibrillatory waves are described as fine (under 0.5 mm) or coarse (over 0.5 mm). Coarse ones can mimic P waves and cause a misdiagnosis.
  • The lecturer’s reassurance about exams: irregular plus clean narrow complexes is almost always atrial fibrillation, and you will not be handed a strange atypical tracing.

An episode currently counts as atrial fibrillation at 30 seconds or longer. The lecturer expects that cut-off to be replaced by a burden-based definition within about two years, which is worth knowing if a question seems to turn on the number.

Why there is no complete cure: two different diseases wearing one name

🧑

The young patient

  • Triggered activity from the pulmonary veins, which are richly supplied with autonomic nerve tissue.
  • Here atrial fibrillation behaves like a nervous-system or genetic disease — long QT syndrome and Brugada syndrome can both cause it.
  • Ablation works by electrically isolating those veins, which the lecturer describes as wrapping them in electrical tape.
👴

The older patient

  • Hypertension, diabetes and valve disease remodel the atrium into a maze of scar with altered autonomic supply.
  • Impulses get lost in it and find many ways out, so there is no single circuit to destroy.
  • After a hundred thousand or more beats a day for decades, ablation cannot cure all of it.

Triggers and causes

  • Hypertension — years of high afterload cause left ventricular hypertrophy, the atria then pump against a stiff ventricle, distend and scar. Ischemic heart disease.
  • Valve disease, especially mitral stenosis or regurgitation.
  • Electrolytes — low potassium or magnesium after a heavy night, a viral illness or poor fluid intake. This cause is reversible, so look for it.
  • Acute infection. Thyrotoxicosis, which gives rapid atrial fibrillation with a suppressed thyroid-stimulating hormone, is often reversible and is rate-controlled with a beta-blocker while the thyroid is treated.
  • Alcohol, the third-ranked risk factor: a direct myocardial toxin with autonomic effects on top. Board add-on Atrial fibrillation after a binge is called “holiday heart.” Sympathomimetic drugs.
  • Pulmonary embolism (it distends the right ventricle and then the right atrium), pericardial disease, acid–base disturbance, pre-excitation, cardiomyopathy, obesity, diabetes and sleep apnea.
⚠️ Two pairings examiners love

Atrial fibrillation together with ST elevation, which is rare in real life but common on tests, and atrial fibrillation together with thyrotoxicosis. If a vignette gives you new atrial fibrillation plus tremor, weight loss and heat intolerance, the thyroid is the answer and the beta-blocker is the treatment.

🔑 It is not mainly palpitations

Fatigue and shortness of breath are the most common symptoms — heart-failure-type complaints, typically “I cannot climb a flight of stairs any more.” Only about 50 to 60% of patients feel palpitations at all. A vignette describing new exertional breathlessness in an older patient with an irregular pulse is describing atrial fibrillation, not just deconditioning.

Acute management, step by step

  1. Diagnose it. Irregular R–R intervals, and P waves that do not match each other.
  2. Ask how long it has been going on. One hour versus two weeks changes everything that follows, particularly whether you can cardiovert safely.
  3. Assess anticoagulation with the CHA₂DS₂-VASc score, which is moving toward CHA₂DS₂-VA.
  4. Choose rate or rhythm control. Acutely this is usually rate control first.
  5. Treat the underlying cause. Is the potassium 2? Is the thyroid off? Is there an infection? Fixing the cause may fix the rhythm.

  • A young person in atrial fibrillation for a couple of hours with no risk factors can be rate-controlled, or sedated and cardioverted, with no long-term anticoagulation.

⚠️ Test trap: “it is only flutter”

Atrial flutter gets exactly the same anticoagulation as atrial fibrillation, scored with the same CHA₂DS₂-VASc risk profile. This is a class 1 guideline recommendation. The two rhythms are usually, in the lecturer’s word, “a family” — the same atria tend to produce flutter, fibrillation, premature atrial beats and focal firing at different times.

Rate control versus rhythm control

  • The AFFIRM trial in the late 1990s found rate control and rhythm control broadly equivalent, and that answer shaped practice for years.
  • Today rhythm control is favoured long term for most symptomatic patients and for those with reduced heart function. Rate control remains reasonable in older patients with many other illnesses.
  • Rate control drugs act on the atrioventricular node: beta-blockers (class II) and verapamil or diltiazem (class IV), with digoxin as an additional option. Rhythm control drugs act on the atrial muscle: class I sodium-channel blockers and class III potassium-channel blockers. Section 10 works through all of them.
  • Long term, treat the whole patient and not just the rhythm. The pillars are stroke risk, optimising every modifiable risk factor, and symptom management. The risk-factor list is heart failure, exercise, arterial hypertension, diabetes, tobacco, obesity, ethanol and sleep — treating these alone can cut the number of episodes substantially.
🩺 Why atrial fibrillation hurts sick patients so much

Up to 15% of medical intensive care patients are in atrial fibrillation, and it hurts them in two ways:

  • Losing the atrial kick costs preload — the electrophysiologist quotes about 15% of ventricular filling.
  • A fast rate shortens filling time on top of that. A stiff ventricle, from sepsis or hypertrophy, is especially preload-dependent and suffers most.
  • A persistently high rate also produces a tachycardia-mediated cardiomyopathy.
  • As the lecturer put it, atrial fibrillation “comes out at your worst” — with a cold, a pneumonia or an appendicitis — and the high sympathetic tone of being ill makes the rate hard to control.
⚠️ Where the sources disagree on the atrial kick

The electrophysiologist quotes the atrial kick as about 15% of ventricular filling. Dr. Morganelli’s physiology lecture (Section 1) puts it at about 10% at rest, and Dr. Qazi quoted 20 to 25%. All three are making the same point — the contribution is modest at rest but matters a great deal when the ventricle is stiff or the rate is fast. If an exam forces a number, the usual board answer is 10 to 30% depending on ventricular compliance.

Q: A 72-year-old woman with hypertension and diabetes is found to be in atrial fibrillation. What is her CHA₂DS₂-VASc score, and what follows from it?

Answer

Age 65 to 74 gives 1, hypertension 1, diabetes 1 and female sex 1, for a total of 4 (or 3 using CHA₂DS₂-VA, which drops the sex point). A score of 2 or more means anticoagulate, usually with a direct oral anticoagulant.

Q: One patient’s atrial fibrillation stopped on its own after 5 days. Another’s ran continuously for 3 weeks and needed cardioversion. Classify each.

Answer

The first is paroxysmal — it ended within 7 days. The second is persistent — longer than 7 days and it required an intervention. The second patient stays classified as persistent even if drugs later keep the episodes short.

Q: A 34-year-old presents with new atrial fibrillation at 140, a tremor, weight loss and a suppressed thyroid-stimulating hormone. What is the trigger, and what is the first-line rate control?

Answer

Thyrotoxicosis, which is a reversible cause. Rate-control with a beta-blocker while the thyroid disease itself is treated; the rhythm often resolves with it.

Q: What is the most common presenting symptom of atrial fibrillation?

Answer

Fatigue and shortness of breath, not palpitations — only about half of patients notice palpitations at all.

Q: A patient with typical atrial flutter and a CHA₂DS₂-VASc score of 3 is about to be discharged. The intern says anticoagulation is not needed because it is flutter and not fibrillation. Is that right?

Answer

No. Flutter carries the same stroke risk and the same anticoagulation recommendation as atrial fibrillation, using the identical score. This is a class 1 recommendation and a favourite trap.

Q: A 45-year-old is brought in after a weekend of heavy drinking with palpitations and an irregularly irregular narrow-complex rhythm. He has no other medical history. What is the likely diagnosis, what is the eponym, and does he need long-term anticoagulation?

Answer

Atrial fibrillation triggered by alcohol — “holiday heart.” His CHA₂DS₂-VASc score is 0, so he does not need long-term anticoagulation. Rate control or cardioversion for the episode, plus checking and replacing potassium and magnesium, and counselling about alcohol.
