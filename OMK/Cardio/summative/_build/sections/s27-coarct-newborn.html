Section 27 · Congenital Heart Disease

Coarctation and the Newborn Murmur

The pediatric cardiologist’s plea for this topic was blunt: “Check femoral pulses on every newborn.” It takes diligence, and it is how coarctation is caught before the ductus closes. The second half of this section is the question every nursery raises: a newborn has a systolic murmur — how likely is it to be structural, and when does it need an echo?

Coarctation in the newborn: the exam findings ★ HY

🩺 The lecture’s case: a 6-hour-old with cool, mottled legs

A term boy with no risk factors. The nurse is worried: mildly fast breathing (40–50) and a fast heart rate (about 150), a murmur, and mottled, cool legs. Saturation 94% in arms and legs; glucose 60. A grade 2–3/6 systolic murmur radiating to the back; weak femoral pulses; arm pressures 25–30 mm Hg higher than leg pressures. The lower body gets oxygenated blood — just not enough of it.

  • About 5% of congenital heart disease; boys more than girls, 2:1.
  • The narrowing is usually near where the ductus arteriosus inserts (juxtaductal).
  • Associations: Turner syndrome (25–30% have coarctation — “suspect Turner? Get an echo”) and a bicuspid aortic valve. Board add-on Berry aneurysms of the circle of Willis.
  • Diagnosis: echocardiography; angiography or MRA defines the anatomy.
  • Treatment depends on the pressure gradient: balloon angioplasty (catheter up from the femoral artery) or surgical resection and re-anastomosis. A critical, ductal-dependent coarctation needs PGE₁ first to reopen the ductus. Board add-on
🧠 Coarctation in the older child or adult Board add-on

A non-critical coarctation can go unnoticed until later: upper-body hypertension (a secondary cause in the young — Section 28), headaches, cold legs or claudication, a brachio-femoral delay, an interscapular murmur, and rib notching on CXR from enlarged intercostal collaterals. Complications: heart failure, aortic rupture or dissection, intracranial hemorrhage from berry aneurysms, and endocarditis.

The relative incidence of structural lesions

  • Congenital heart disease occurs in about 1 in 125 live births — the most common birth defect.
  • The lecture’s chart, in order: VSD (over 30%), then PDA, pulmonary stenosis, ASD, coarctation, bicuspid aortic valve, tetralogy of Fallot and transposition.
  • The lecturer’s update: with routine echo, many bicuspid aortic valves are now found by chance, so VSD and bicuspid aortic valve are about equally common in practice. (A bicuspid valve affects 1–2% of people, males twice as often.)

⚠️ Two incidence figures to read carefully

The lecture chart gives VSD at about 31% of congenital heart disease; Dr. Gardner’s table gives 42%. One pediatric slide says VSD occurs in “2 to 5% of births”; board sources give 2–5 per 1,000 births. Either way, VSD is the commonest.

The significant newborn systolic murmur and the predictive value of echo

What the lecture says

  • A systolic murmur in the nursery with an otherwise normal baby — pink, good saturation, good femoral and brachial pulses, clear lungs — can reasonably be watched: it might be a closing PDA or PFO.
  • But a murmur that persists or gets louder after the first weeks “shouldn’t be there by now” — refer. The lecture’s 4-month-old with a louder grade 2–3 murmur had a large VSD on echo.
  • The cardiologist’s work-up is a history, an exam, an ECG and an echo. Echo with colour Doppler is the definitive test — “the lighter the colour, the faster the flow.”
  • Features that make a murmur sound structural: any diastolic murmur, grade 3 or more or a thrill, harsh or holosystolic, an abnormal S2 or a click, loudness that does not change with position, and symptoms (poor growth, cyanosis, sweating with feeds).

What the predictive value means Board add-on

  • A murmur heard in the first days of life is common and often transitional (the closing ductus, a PFO, peripheral pulmonary flow). Even so, published newborn series find structural disease on echo in a substantial fraction — roughly a third to a half of newborns referred with a murmur, far more than for an older child’s murmur. The lecture gives no figure, so treat this range as approximate.
  • That is why a persistent or pathologic-sounding newborn murmur gets an echo rather than reassurance: the clinical exam alone misclassifies a meaningful share, and echo is close to definitive for structural disease.
  • The converse matters too: many critical lesions have no murmur at all (transposition, hypoplastic left heart, critical coarctation before the ductus closes). The absence of a murmur does not exclude serious disease, which is why every newborn has pulse-oximetry screening for critical CHD after 24 hours: right hand (pre-ductal) and a foot (post-ductal); a saturation under 95% in either, or a big difference between them, gets an echo.

Q: A newborn has weak femoral pulses and an arm blood pressure 30 mm Hg higher than the leg pressure. What is the diagnosis, and which syndrome should you think of in a girl?

Answer

Coarctation of the aorta. In a girl, think of Turner syndrome (45,X) — 25–30% have coarctation, and many have a bicuspid aortic valve.

Q: A 2-day-old boy who seemed well becomes pale, tachypneic and acidotic, with absent femoral pulses and a falling urine output. What is happening, and what is the first drug?

Answer

Critical coarctation presenting as the ductus closes — the lower body was being supplied through it. Start prostaglandin E1 to reopen the ductus, then repair.

Q: A 16-year-old has a blood pressure of 158/92 in the right arm, cold feet when running, and notching of the inferior rib margins on a chest X-ray. Diagnosis and the mechanism of the rib sign?

Answer

Coarctation of the aorta found late. Enlarged intercostal collateral arteries, carrying blood around the narrowing, erode the undersides of the ribs.

Q: A newborn has a soft systolic murmur on day 1 but is pink, feeding well, with normal pulses and saturation. At 4 months the murmur is louder (grade 2–3). What do you do, and what is the likely finding?

Answer

Watching on day 1 was reasonable (a closing PDA or PFO). A murmur that persists and gets louder at 4 months “shouldn’t be there by now”: refer for ECG and echo. The lecture’s case was a large VSD.

Q: Which congenital lesion is the most common, and why does the lecturer say the bicuspid aortic valve now rivals it?

Answer

The VSD (about 31–42% of congenital heart disease). With routine echocardiography, many bicuspid aortic valves (1–2% of people) are found by chance, so in practice the two are about equally common.

Q: A newborn passes the nursery exam with no murmur. Why is that not reassurance against critical heart disease, and what screening test closes the gap?

Answer

Many critical lesions — transposition, hypoplastic left heart, critical coarctation while the ductus is open — have no murmur. Pulse-oximetry screening after 24 hours (right hand and a foot) catches them: a saturation under 95% or a pre-/post-ductal difference prompts an echo.
