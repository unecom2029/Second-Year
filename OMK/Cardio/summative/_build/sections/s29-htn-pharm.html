First-line classes and compelling indications

Why one drug is often not enough

  • Lower BP with one drug and two compensations fire: the baroreceptor reflex raises sympathetic outflow (heart rate, contractility, vasoconstriction), and reduced renal perfusion raises renin, with sodium and water retention. Block one branch and the other can bring the pressure back — the rationale for two complementary drugs.
  • Lifestyle for everyone: weight loss (about 1 mm Hg per kg), DASH or Mediterranean diet, low sodium and more potassium, exercise, moderate alcohol, removing causes such as oral contraceptives and stimulants. Each gives about 1–10 mm Hg; together about 10–20.

🧠 A dry cough predicts angioedema

Nearly every patient who develops ACE-inhibitor angioedema had the cough first. Counsel patients, and switch a persistent cough to an ARB, which keeps the renin–angiotensin benefit without bradykinin build-up. Early laryngeal signs are hoarseness and inspiratory stridor.

Combinations to avoid

  • ACEi + ARB, or either + aliskiren — stacked hyperkalemia, kidney injury, hypotension.
  • Beta-blocker + verapamil or diltiazem — additive nodal and myocardial depression.
  • Two sympatholytics (α1 antagonist + clonidine, beta-blocker + clonidine) — one branch hit twice, side effects stacked.
  • ACEi or ARB in anyone who may become pregnant.

Hypertension in pregnancy

✅

Use

  • Labetalol — β1/β2 + α1 block; the α1 block prevents reflex vasoconstriction, so placental flow is preserved (metoprolol would reduce it). Avoid in asthma.
  • Nifedipine ER (and amlodipine).
  • α-methyldopa — central α2 agonist; the longest safety record, less effective.
  • Hydralazine — acute use only.
⛔

Avoid

  • ACE inhibitors, ARBs, aliskiren — fetal kidney toxicity and low amniotic fluid (worst in the 2nd and 3rd trimesters; if she is already on one, switch — no need to panic).
  • Nitroprusside.
  • Spironolactone — anti-androgenic.
  • Atenolol — low birth weight.

ACOG: hypertension in pregnancy is ≥ 140/90 twice, 4 hours apart; severe range ≥ 160/110; chronic if before pregnancy or before 20 weeks.

🔑 Preventing electrolyte adverse events
  • Check potassium and creatinine 1–2 weeks after starting or raising an ACEi, ARB, MRA or ENaC blocker, and after adding a diuretic.
  • Pair drugs whose potassium effects cancel — a thiazide with an ACEi or ARB; but potassium can still go either way, so monitor.
  • Never two RAAS blockers; avoid potassium supplements and NSAIDs with them; be careful in CKD.
  • Watch sodium in older women on thiazides; watch magnesium on loops, and remember low potassium or magnesium makes digoxin toxic and the QT long.
  • Board add-on The “hyperGLUC” thiazide hook: hyperGlycemia, hyperLipidemia, hyperUricemia, hyperCalcemia.

Q: A 60-year-old with a confirmed BP of 152/96, not frail, no drug intolerances. One drug or two, and which?

Answer

Stage 2, under 75, not frail: two drugs, ideally one pill — a dihydropyridine CCB plus an ACE inhibitor or ARB (an ACEi or ARB plus a thiazide is also acceptable).

Q: A patient taking lisinopril and losartan (prescribed by different doctors) presents with weakness and peaked T waves. Mechanism?

Answer

Double RAAS blockade: less aldosterone, so less ENaC-driven potassium secretion — hyperkalemia. ACEi plus ARB is a combination to avoid.

Q: A hypertensive patient has recurrent calcium oxalate stones and osteopenia. Which antihypertensive helps all three, and how? What electrolyte changes will it cause?

Answer

A thiazide. NCC block lowers cell sodium in the distal tubule, driving more basolateral Na⁺/Ca²⁺ exchange and more calcium reabsorption. Expect low sodium, low potassium, metabolic alkalosis, high calcium and high uric acid.

Q: A patient on furosemide for heart failure has a potassium of 2.9, a magnesium of 1.3, a low calcium and a bicarbonate of 33. Explain each.

Answer

NKCC2 block removes the lumen-positive potential in the thick ascending limb, so calcium and magnesium are not reabsorbed between cells. Extra sodium reaching the principal cell is taken up through ENaC, leaving the lumen negative, so potassium and hydrogen ion are secreted — a hypokalemic metabolic alkalosis.

Q: Which first-line class most often causes ankle edema, and why does a diuretic not fix it well? Which CCB causes the most reflex tachycardia?

Answer

Dihydropyridine CCBs: arteriolar dilation raises capillary hydrostatic pressure — it is not volume overload, so diuretics help little; adding an ACEi or ARB does. Nifedipine causes the most reflex tachycardia.

Q: A 27-year-old at 16 weeks of pregnancy has a BP of 160/114 on two readings. Classify it, name three drugs to use, and four to avoid.

Answer

Chronic hypertension (before 20 weeks), in the severe range. Use labetalol, nifedipine ER or α-methyldopa (hydralazine acutely). Avoid ACE inhibitors, ARBs and aliskiren, nitroprusside, spironolactone and atenolol. Watch for superimposed pre-eclampsia.

Q: A man on spironolactone for resistant hypertension develops tender breast enlargement and a potassium of 5.8. Explain both, and what would you switch to?

Answer

Spironolactone blocks androgen receptors (gynecomastia) and the mineralocorticoid receptor (less ENaC, so potassium is retained). Switch to eplerenone for the gynecomastia — but it still raises potassium, so reduce or stop other potassium-raising drugs and monitor.
