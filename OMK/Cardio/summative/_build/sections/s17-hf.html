Heart Failure: Reduced Versus Preserved Ejection Fraction

Congestive heart failure is the point at which the heart cannot meet the body’s metabolic demands. Dr. Gardner put its prognosis bluntly: about 50% five-year survival, comparable to many cancers. Dr. Segal closed the case session with the four questions this section is built around: Is this heart failure, and what type? Is the patient congested? Is perfusion adequate? Which treatments help now and later?

The pathophysiology of each ★ HY

How overload becomes failure

  1. Three common causes, two kinds of overload. Hypertension gives pressure overload; valve disease gives pressure overload (stenosis) or volume overload (regurgitation); myocardial infarction gives regional dysfunction with volume overload.
  2. More cardiac work raises wall stress and stretches the myocytes.
  3. Hypertrophy and/or dilation. Protein synthesis rises, immediate-early genes and the fetal gene programme switch on.
  4. Fibroblasts proliferate and lay down fibrosis, and the existing blood supply cannot keep up with the bigger muscle.
  5. Dysfunction: systolic or diastolic failure, arrhythmias (poor vascularity, interrupted conduction) and neurohumoral activation.

HFrEF versus HFpEF: the mechanics

💔

HFrEF — a pumping problem

  • Contractility falls: the ESPVR slope drops. Usually from myocyte loss in ischemic heart disease, and/or high afterload.
  • Less is ejected, so blood is left behind: ESV rises, then EDV rises and the ventricle dilates.
  • A smaller stroke volume out of a bigger EDV means the EF falls.
  • The Starling curve shifts down and to the right.
🧱

HFpEF — a filling problem

  • Compliance falls (fibrosis, hypertrophy), or relaxation is slow: the EDPVR steepens.
  • Less EDV at a higher end-diastolic pressure, which backs up into the lungs.
  • Stroke volume falls because EDV falls, but contractility is intact, so the EF is preserved.
  • Now thought to be about as common as HFrEF, and under-explored and under-treated.
⚠️ A recording slip to ignore

The physiology recording ends the HFpEF explanation with “so in this case you would see a reduced ejection fraction.” The slide is right: in HFpEF the stroke volume is reduced but the ejection fraction is preserved, because contractility is unchanged.

The neurohumoral spiral: why the failing heart gets worse

  1. Falling cardiac function lowers arterial pressure, and the baroreceptor reflex fires.
  2. Sympathetic outflow rises: α-mediated vasoconstriction and tachycardia.
  3. β1 on the juxtaglomerular cells releases renin, angiotensin II rises and vasoconstricts: more afterload.
  4. Angiotensin II releases aldosterone: sodium and water retention, volume expansion, more preload.
  5. More afterload and more preload raise oxygen demand and worsen the failure. In Dr. Scully’s words, “some of our compensations end up making this condition worse.”
  • Why the natriuretic peptides do not rescue it: chronically dilated atria and ventricles become resistant to their own natriuretic peptide, so the kidney keeps reabsorbing sodium despite volume overload (Section 13 has BNP).
  • That is why every mortality-reducing drug in Section 18 cuts a link in this chain.

Left- versus right-sided signs and symptoms

🫁

Left heart failure — “short of breath”

  • Usually from MI or hypertension.
  • Backward: blood backs up into the lungs — dyspnea, DOE, orthopnea, PND, crackles, pulmonary edema.
  • Forward: poor organ perfusion — fatigue, cool extremities, low urine output.
  • Board add-on Orthopnea: lying flat shifts blood from the legs to the chest. PND: overnight reabsorption of edema adds venous return. “Heart-failure cells” are hemosiderin-laden alveolar macrophages.
🦶

Right heart failure — “swollen ankles”

  • The most common cause of right heart failure is left heart failure.
  • Venous back-up: raised JVP, a positive hepatojugular reflux, peripheral edema, ascites, effusions, splenomegaly.
  • Congestive hepatomegaly, the “nutmeg liver”, which can progress to cardiac cirrhosis.
  • Isolated right failure: an RV infarct, pulmonary hypertension and cor pulmonale (lung disease such as COPD), tricuspid disease, primary RV cardiomyopathy.

Dr. Segal’s caveat: the clean right/left split is for boards. In clinic the signs are usually mixed, because one side failing eventually drags the other down.

Dr. Qazi: in practice cardiologists use the NYHA class together with the ACC/AHA stage (A–D), and “that’s enough to classify the patient.” Board add-on Stage A is at risk with no structural disease, B is structural disease without symptoms, C is symptomatic, D is refractory.

The acutely decompensated patient: wet or dry, warm or cold

  • Wet and warm is the commonest: diurese, on the ward. Wet and cold is cardiogenic shock: ICU, inotropes plus diuretics.
  • Dr. Qazi: “Get the patient back on the Starling curve.” Reduce preload first (diurese), then work on afterload.
  • Never start a beta-blocker while decompensated — it is a negative inotrope (Section 18).

What alcohol and cocaine do

  • Alcohol causes a dilated cardiomyopathy: indirectly through sympathetic and RAAS activation and hypertension, directly through oxidative stress, fibrosis and impaired excitation–contraction coupling. Malnourished drinkers can also have thiamine deficiency, “wet” beriberi — a high-output failure.
  • Cocaine blocks noradrenaline reuptake. In a patient on a β1-selective blocker such as metoprolol, the β effects are blunted but α vasoconstriction is unopposed, so afterload rises — “the one thing you don’t want to increase in heart failure.” Carvedilol and labetalol block both.

Labs and diagnostics

Build the differential first

  • Dyspnea in buckets: cardiac (coronary disease, heart failure, cardiomyopathy, arrhythmia, valves, pericardium), pulmonary (COPD, asthma, pneumonia, embolism, interstitial disease), anemia, neuromuscular, and anxiety — but rule out the serious things first.
  • Edema by Starling forces: raised capillary hydrostatic pressure (heart failure, venous insufficiency, dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers) versus low oncotic pressure (cirrhosis, nephrotic syndrome, malnutrition). “Pitting edema is nothing more than salt and water trapped in the interstitium.”

The exam

  • JVP = height above the sternal angle plus 5 cm. A resting heart rate of 90 is a worry: often sympathetically driven.
  • S4 = a non-compliant ventricle. S3: physiologic in the young, but in heart-failure patients a marker of volume overload (Dr. Qazi).
  • The Mallampati score doubles as an OSA screen — and sleep apnea worsens heart failure.

Imaging and the ECG

  • Echocardiogram: chamber sizes, EF, hypertrophy pattern, valves, and diastolic function graded 1–3. Grade 1 usually means a normal left atrial pressure (and is normal for age in the elderly); grade 2 means a raised left atrial pressure.
  • ECG: ischemia, Q waves, LVH (“11 and 35” — Section 7), AF, bundle branch block.
  • Chest X-ray: Kerley B lines (short horizontal lines at the bases from fluid in the interlobular septa, “very characteristic of heart failure”), cephalization, a wide vascular pedicle, cardiomegaly, pleural effusions.
  • The H₂FPEF score estimates the probability of HFpEF: Heavy (BMI over 30) 2, Hypertensive (2 or more drugs) 1, atrial Fibrillation 3, Pulmonary hypertension (PASP over 35) 1, Elder (over 60) 1, Filling pressure (E/e′ over 9) 1. Dr. Segal’s patient scored 5 — over 80% probability.

Q: A 60-year-old woman with type 2 diabetes, hypertension and obesity has 3 weeks of exertional dyspnea, orthopnea and ankle swelling. JVP is 7 cm, there are basal crackles, an S4 and no S3. Echo shows an EF of 65%, concentric hypertrophy and grade II diastolic dysfunction. Diagnosis and mechanism?

Answer

Heart failure with preserved ejection fraction. Impaired relaxation of a concentrically hypertrophied, stiff ventricle: a filling problem with a raised end-diastolic pressure. Grade II diastolic dysfunction implies a raised left atrial pressure; the S4 is the atrium hitting the stiff ventricle.

Q: A patient’s end-diastolic pressure–volume relationship is much steeper than normal while the end-systolic line is unchanged. What type of failure is this, and what happens to stroke volume and ejection fraction?

Answer

HFpEF. A stiff ventricle fills less, so EDV and stroke volume fall, but with contractility unchanged the EF is preserved.

Q: At autopsy, a liver has a mottled red-and-tan cut surface. What is the most likely cardiac cause, what does it usually come from, and what can it progress to?

Answer

Right heart failure, which is usually secondary to left heart failure. Chronic passive congestion gives the nutmeg liver, which can progress to cardiac cirrhosis.

Q: A hemodialysis patient with a large upper-arm fistula has dyspnea, edema, a wide pulse pressure, a raised cardiac output and a low systemic vascular resistance. What kind of heart failure is this?

Answer

High-output heart failure from the AV shunt: high demand, high cardiac output, low resistance. Thyrotoxicosis, severe anemia and wet beriberi do the same.

Q: A patient with HFrEF has cool, mottled extremities, confusion, a rising creatinine, a JVP of 16 cm and crackles. Which hemodynamic profile is this, and where should he be treated?

Answer

Wet and cold — cardiogenic shock. ICU, with inotropes plus decongestion (diuretics, vasodilators, ultrafiltration).

Q: A 70-year-old man with HFrEF on metoprolol has used crack cocaine and now has worsening pulmonary edema with a high blood pressure. Which receptor effect explains the deterioration?

Answer

Unopposed α1 vasoconstriction. Cocaine floods the synapse with noradrenaline; the β1-selective blocker leaves the α receptors free, so afterload rises. Carvedilol or labetalol, which block α too, avoid this.

Q: A patient has bilateral pitting ankle edema, an albumin of 1.9 g/dL and 4+ proteinuria, with a normal JVP and no orthopnea. Which Starling force is deranged?

Answer

Low plasma oncotic pressure (nephrotic syndrome) — not the raised capillary hydrostatic pressure of heart failure, which would come with a high JVP.
