Aneurysms and Dissections

Dr. Gardner’s theme again: a small weakness in a high-pressure pipe, given decades, becomes a catastrophe. An aneurysm is the wall stretching outward; a dissection is the wall splitting from within. The vascular surgeon added two things he promised would be on the test — the aneurysm surveillance chart (“memorize that chart”) and what to do with type A versus type B dissection.

Why vessel walls give way

🧵

Weak material

  • Poor connective tissue: Marfan (fibrillin-1), vascular Ehlers–Danlos (collagen III), altered TGF-β signalling (Section 33).
🔥

Remodelling

  • Inflammation and matrix turnover: MMP activity, chronic plaque inflammation, hypertension lifting damaged endothelium.
🩸

Ischemia of the wall

  • Thick walls need their own blood supply, the vasa vasorum; narrow it and the media dies back.
🦠

Infection

  • Tertiary syphilis inflames and obliterates the vasa vasorum of the thoracic aorta; a mycotic aneurysm is an infected wall (often from endocarditis emboli).

Microscopic features Board add-on

  • Thoracic aneurysm and dissection: cystic medial degeneration — loss and fragmentation of elastic fibres and smooth muscle cells, with pools of mucoid (proteoglycan) ground substance in the media. Seen in Marfan, with hypertension and with age.
  • Abdominal aneurysm: transmural inflammation and extracellular-matrix degradation (MMPs), a thinned media, overlying atherosclerosis and often laminated mural thrombus.
  • Syphilitic aortitis: obliterative endarteritis of the vasa vasorum with plasma cells, giving a wrinkled “tree-bark” intima.

Abdominal aortic aneurysm: screening and surgical thresholds ★ HY

  • Mostly asymptomatic — found as a pulsatile abdominal mass or by chance. Symptoms: abdominal or back pain, early satiety, hydronephrosis.
  • Rupture: severe back or abdominal pain, a pulsatile mass and shock. Board add-on Straight to the operating room if unstable.
  • Four complications: rupture, embolisation, dissection, fistulisation (aortoduodenal or aortoenteric).
  • Grows about 0.2–0.3 cm a year. Risk of rupture is raised by hypertension, COPD, female sex, smoking and steroids.
  • Screening: men 65–70 and those with a family history (slide). Board add-on USPSTF: a one-time ultrasound in men 65–75 who have ever smoked.
  • Imaging: an eggshell calcified rim on X-ray; ultrasound for screening and surveillance; CT with contrast is the gold standard.

🔑 Indications for surgery

Symptomatic at any size · over 5.5 cm · growth of more than 5 mm in 6 months · an eccentric or saccular shape. A non-compliant patient who will not change their behaviour may be operated on sooner. And always: stop smoking.

Repair and its complications

  • EVAR needs suitable anatomy: an aortic neck over 15 mm long, an angle under 60°, big enough access arteries and calcification under 50% of the circumference. Fenestrated and branched grafts keep the renals and SMA open. Open repair is now mostly for those who cannot have a stent graft.
  • Complications: kidney injury, leg ischemia, ischemic colitis, spinal cord ischemia, sexual dysfunction, endograft migration or leak (endoleak — why EVAR needs lifelong imaging Board add-on).
  • The classic ICU question: the night after aortic repair, an unstable patient on norepinephrine passes bloody diarrhea in the first 12–24 hours — ischemic colitis of the sigmoid from lost IMA flow; confirm with sigmoidoscopy. At surgery, pulsatile back-bleeding from the cut IMA means good SMA collaterals (tie it); a weak trickle means the colon depends on it (re-implant).
  • Graft infection (dull pain, fever, a high white count): antibiotics, remove the graft, extra-anatomic bypass. Aortoenteric fistula: a known graft plus GI bleeding, often a small “herald bleed” first.

Dissection: classification and management ★ HY

What happens

  1. An intimal tear — on a diseased, hypertensive wall, or from blunt deceleration trauma.
  2. Blood tracks through the media, forward and sometimes back toward the aortic valve ring.
  3. Three bad endings (Gardner): breach of the adventitia with fatal hemorrhage; extension into the great vessels or coronary ostia, giving stroke or MI; or re-entry and a chronic “double-barrelled aorta”.
  • Rupture is actually rare; the usual harm is ischemia downstream as the flap cuts off branches — the renals, the gut, the legs, the brain. Dr. Ciraulo: “I think more lethal than atherosclerotic disease.”
  • Ascending dissections can also cause acute AR and tamponade.
  • Timing: acute under 2 weeks (complicated or uncomplicated); chronic over 2 weeks.

Who, and how it presents

  • Risks: hypertension (Board add-on the strongest risk factor), aortic wall disease (Marfan, EDS, Loeys–Dietz, Turner), bicuspid aortic valve, cocaine (“the most dramatic hypertensive crisis”), pregnancy, trauma.
  • Excruciating, tearing chest pain that moves to the back and downward as it propagates — “someone’s trying to rip my back apart”, against the infarct’s “elephant on my chest”.
  • Board add-on A pulse or BP difference of more than 20 mm Hg between the arms, a widened mediastinum on chest X-ray, a new AR murmur.
  • It can present as a stroke: Dr. McGrath’s 48-year-old security guard collapsed with right-sided weakness, and the stroke CT angiogram found a type A dissection occluding the left carotid.
  • “It’s not time to break out the manipulation box. That’s the time to get a CAT scan.”

Diagnosis and medical management

  • CT angiography of the chest and abdomen; TEE when the patient is unstable Board add-on. Exclude dissection before giving anticoagulants or thrombolytics to a patient with chest pain.
  • Board add-on “Impulse control”: an IV beta-blocker first (esmolol, labetalol) to a heart rate of about 60 and SBP 100–120, before any vasodilator such as nitroprusside — a vasodilator alone causes reflex tachycardia, which raises the shear force (dP/dt) on the aortic wall. Opioids for pain.
  • Paraplegia after descending repair: the clamp cuts flow to the artery of Adamkiewicz, supplying the spinal cord. Prevention: shorter clamp times or aortic bypass, and a lumbar CSF drain to protect cord perfusion. (The recording placed the artery around T4–T8; it usually arises at T9–T12 Board add-on.)

Q: An asymptomatic 70-year-old man who smoked for 40 years has a 4.8 cm AAA on ultrasound. What is the plan?

Answer

Ultrasound surveillance every 3 months (the 4.5–5.0 cm band), plus risk-factor control — above all, stop smoking.

Q: A 68-year-old woman has a 5.2 cm AAA; her brother died of a ruptured aneurysm. Repair or watch?

Answer

Repair. In the 5.0–5.5 cm band, female sex and a familial aneurysm are on the chart’s list of reasons to operate.

Q: A hypertensive 60-year-old has sudden tearing pain between the shoulder blades. CTA shows a dissection starting just beyond the left subclavian artery and running to the iliacs. He is stable, with good pulses and kidney function. Classify and manage.

Answer

DeBakey III, Stanford B, uncomplicated — medical blood-pressure control: an IV beta-blocker first (heart rate about 60), then a vasodilator if needed, to an SBP of 100–120. TEVAR if it becomes complicated.

Q: A tall 25-year-old with long fingers and a dislocated lens has sudden tearing chest pain radiating to the back and a new diastolic murmur. Diagnosis, defective protein, and management?

Answer

A type A aortic dissection, with aortic regurgitation from root involvement, in Marfan syndrome (fibrillin-1). Emergency surgery.

Q: Twenty hours after open AAA repair, a patient on norepinephrine passes bloody stool. Diagnosis, test, and what intraoperative finding should have prompted re-implanting the IMA?

Answer

Ischemic colitis of the sigmoid — sigmoidoscopy. The IMA should be re-implanted when its back-bleeding is weak (poor collaterals from the SMA); pulsatile back-bleeding means it can be tied.

Q: Which part of the aorta does tertiary syphilis affect, how, and what is the microscopic lesion of a Marfan thoracic aneurysm?

Answer

The thoracic (ascending) aorta — obliterative inflammation of the vasa vasorum starves the media (a “tree-bark” intima). Marfan aneurysms show cystic medial degeneration: fragmented elastic fibres and mucoid pools in the media.

Q: After repair of a descending thoracic aneurysm, a patient cannot move his legs. Mechanism and prevention?

Answer

Spinal cord ischemia from lost flow through the artery of Adamkiewicz during cross-clamping. Prevent it with short clamp times or aortic bypass, and a lumbar CSF drain.
