Myocarditis and Pericarditis

The second half of the infection lecture was, in the lecturer’s words, a “fairly brief” pass — “Myo and Peri, sounds like a sixties singing group that didn’t make it.” The exam task is to tell the three layers apart: the endocardium (bacteria, valves, emboli), the myocardium (mostly viruses, heart failure and arrhythmia in a young person) and the pericardium (that distinctive pain, a rub and diffuse ST elevation).

Myocarditis: signs, symptoms and causes

Inflammation of the myocardium — infectious, immune-mediated or toxic — damaging the myocytes, the conduction system, autonomic nerves and interstitium. It is often unsuspected: about 4% of unrelated autopsies show it, and more in young sudden deaths.

Four mechanisms of damage

  1. Direct cell damage by the agent itself (mostly viruses).
  2. Cytokines and cell-mediated immunity — a general immune response, such as a cytokine storm.
  3. Antigen-specific cytotoxicity — a focused immune attack on one microbial antigen.
  4. Toxin cytotoxicity — the best example is diphtheria.

Many cases are idiopathic and simply assumed viral, so the true share is unknown. COVID-19: myocarditis after infection is about 1 in 5,000; after vaccination about 1 in 15,000–50,000, with a very benign course.

🩺 Chagas disease — called out because its epidemiology is changing
  • Trypanosoma cruzi enters through the feces of the triatomine (“kissing”) bug. After a transient acute phase, amastigotes lodge in heart muscle and in the nerves of the gut.
  • Decades later about 20% develop cardiomyopathy, arrhythmia and sudden death, or megacolon and megaesophagus.
  • Endemic to South America and now considered endemic to the US. Think of it in late middle age with unexplained cardiac symptoms and the right exposure. Board add-on Right bundle branch block is a classic ECG clue.

Signs and symptoms

FormPicture

AcuteNew, unexplained heart failure, chest pain, arrhythmia, dyspnea or leg swelling in a young patient, developing over 3 months or less
ChronicOver 3 months; cardiomyopathy alternating with stability — can end as a dilated cardiomyopathy (Section 19)
FulminantSudden cardiac collapse, cardiogenic shock
  • Often asymptomatic. Common features: fever, chest pain, unexplained sinus tachycardia, dyspnea, heart failure, palpitations, syncope, sudden death; S3 or S4, heart block or a new bundle branch block, cardiomegaly.
  • Ask about a preceding viral prodrome — flu-like, upper respiratory or GI.
  • Gardner’s typical patient: an otherwise healthy young person with URI symptoms plus cardiac symptoms and a mild troponin rise.

Diagnosis

  • Troponin raised — troponin T lasts longer, troponin I is more cardiac-specific — and Board add-on it tends to stay at a plateau rather than rise and fall like an infarct. CRP and ESR up; BNP up.
  • ECG: ST elevation, tachy- or bradyarrhythmias. Echo: dilation of all four chambers, diffuse hypokinesis. Board add-on Cardiac MRI shows myocardial edema and patchy late enhancement.
  • Endomyocardial biopsy is definitive (histology, immunohistochemistry, viral PCR) but invasive — reserved for rapidly progressive heart failure. PCR can find bystander viruses.
  • Virus isolated from another site, or rising antibody titres, is only circumstantial.

Treatment

  • Mostly supportive: treat the heart failure and arrhythmias — diuretics, renin–angiotensin blockers, beta-blockers (Section 18), up to mechanical support and transplant.
  • Dr. Gardner’s single most important step: bed rest — “get them off their feet.” Board add-on No competitive sport for 3–6 months, because exertion during myocarditis risks sudden death.
  • Sometimes IVIG or interferon for infectious forms; specific antibacterial, antifungal or antiparasitic drugs when the agent is known. Antivirals have no proven efficacy.

Pericarditis: signs, ECG and organisms

The sac

A double wall: an outer fibrous layer that does not stretch, and an inner serous membrane (parietal and visceral layers) with 15–50 mL of fluid between. It keeps the heart in place, prevents over-filling, lubricates, and sometimes blocks the spread of infection from nearby organs.

The signs — the pain is the giveaway

  • Sharp, stabbing anterior chest pain, worse with coughing, inspiration and lying flat.
  • Better sitting up and leaning forward.
  • Radiates to the left trapezius ridge.
  • A pericardial friction rub — scratchy and squeaky, essentially found only in acute pericarditis (“that’s the rub”). It usually has three parts and is best heard at the left lower sternal border, leaning forward.
  • Acute onset, a course of about 3 weeks, and it can recur. A non-specific prodrome: low-grade fever, fatigue, cough, dyspnea, hiccups.

The ECG

  • Diffuse ST elevation with PR depression. The ST elevation is everywhere, not confined to one coronary territory. Textbooks say it is concave (“saddle”), but the electrophysiologist has seen plenty that are not — lean on distribution, PR depression and the history.
  • Board add-on PR elevation in aVR, and no reciprocal ST depression (except aVR and V1), also favour pericarditis over a STEMI.
  • Four stages over weeks: (1) diffuse STE ± PR depression; (2) ST and PR normalise; (3) diffuse T-wave inversion with an isoelectric ST (weeks 2–4); (4) normal again (1–2 months).

Diagnosis — 2 or more of 4

  1. Typical sharp, pleuritic chest pain, better sitting up.
  2. A pericardial friction rub.
  3. ECG changes: widespread ST elevation or PR depression.
  4. A new or worse pericardial effusion on echo (not needed on its own).

Supporting tests: CBC, ESR and CRP; troponin to look for myocardial involvement; chest X-ray; TTE for effusion.

🎈

Myopericarditis

  • Pericarditis predominates, with some myocardial involvement (troponin up).
  • Ventricular function is normal.
💪

Perimyocarditis

  • Myocarditis predominates, with pericardial features.
  • Ventricular function is reduced.

Treatment

  • NSAIDs + colchicine (colchicine cuts recurrence). Glucocorticoids only if NSAIDs cannot be used. Board add-on After an MI, aspirin is the NSAID of choice.
  • Empiric parenteral antimicrobials if bacterial or fungal disease is suspected, with drainage of a purulent effusion.
  • Antivirals: efficacy not proven.

Effusion and tamponade

  • Fluid in the sac stops the heart expanding, so it cannot fill; cardiac output and blood pressure fall. How fast the fluid collects matters more than how much.
  • Tamponade — Beck triad: hypotension, distended neck veins, muffled heart sounds. Plus tachycardia, a narrow pulse pressure and pulsus paradoxus (an inspiratory systolic drop over 10 mm Hg). Board add-on Electrical alternans on the ECG (the heart swinging in the fluid).
  • Imaging: echo shows the effusion with right atrial and ventricular compression; a large effusion gives a “water-bottle” heart on chest X-ray.
  • Treatment: drain it — echo-guided pericardiocentesis (subxiphoid), or a surgical subxiphoid pericardial window, which removes a piece for pathology and usually does not recur.
  • Settings in the lectures: a uremic dialysis patient (McGrath), a child 3 weeks after VSD surgery (post-pericardiotomy syndrome), cancer (a bloody effusion), and post-MI free-wall rupture (Section 15).
🧠 Constrictive pericarditis Board add-on

The scarred end-stage of pericarditis (TB worldwide; prior surgery or radiation in the US). The stiff shell limits filling, so the picture is right-sided failure: high JVP, edema, ascites. Clues: Kussmaul sign (JVP rises on inspiration), a pericardial knock in early diastole, and pericardial calcification. It mimics restrictive cardiomyopathy (Section 19); treatment is pericardiectomy.

Q: A 30-year-old has sharp chest pain radiating to the left trapezius, worse lying flat and better leaning forward, a week after a cold. The ECG shows diffuse ST elevation with PR depression. Diagnosis, the feature that argues against a STEMI, and first-line treatment?

Answer

Acute (viral) pericarditis. The ST elevation is diffuse, not in one coronary territory, with PR depression and positional pleuritic pain. Treat with NSAIDs plus colchicine.

Q: A 28-year-old develops new heart failure two weeks after a febrile illness. Endomyocardial biopsy shows lymphocytic infiltrates with myocyte necrosis. Which viruses are most often found in adults today, and which older answer shares a receptor with adenovirus?

Answer

Parvovirus B19 and HHV-6 in current data. Coxsackie B is the classic older answer; it and adenovirus use the coxsackie–adenovirus receptor. Management is supportive, with no exertion.

Q: A hiker in Connecticut with a recent expanding rash presents with syncope and complete heart block. Diagnosis?

Answer

Lyme carditis (Borrelia burgdorferi) — about 10% of Lyme patients develop conduction disease.

Q: A child with a grey pharyngeal pseudomembrane dies a week later of an arrhythmia. What is the mechanism?

Answer

Diphtheria toxin myocarditis — historically the main cause of diphtheria deaths, more than airway obstruction.

Q: A dialysis patient has weeks of dyspnea, JVD, distant heart sounds, a narrow pulse pressure and a 20 mm Hg inspiratory drop in systolic pressure. Diagnosis, and two ways to treat it?

Answer

Pericardial tamponade from a uremic effusion (Beck triad plus pulsus paradoxus). Drain it: needle pericardiocentesis, or a surgical subxiphoid pericardial window.

Q: How do myopericarditis and perimyocarditis differ?

Answer

Myopericarditis: pericarditis dominates and ventricular function is normal. Perimyocarditis: myocarditis dominates and ventricular function is reduced.

Q: When a pathologist peels back the pericardium at autopsy, shaggy strands connect it to the heart. Name the pattern and two common causes. What would a bloody effusion suggest instead?

Answer

Fibrinous (“bread-and-butter”) pericarditis — after an MI (early, or Dressler) or cardiac surgery; also uremia. A hemorrhagic effusion suggests malignancy (or TB).
