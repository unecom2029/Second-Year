Cardiovascular Health in Women

No summative objective maps to Dr. Qazi’s lecture, so this is a short version. The message: heart disease is the leading killer of women. Women share every traditional risk factor with men, and they also carry female-specific risk enhancers from their reproductive history — menarche, pregnancy complications and menopause.

Abbreviations used in this section CVD cardiovascular disease · CHD coronary heart disease · MI myocardial infarction · SCAD spontaneous coronary artery dissection · MINOCA MI with non-obstructive coronary arteries · ASCVD atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease · MHT menopausal hormone therapy · WHI Women’s Health Initiative · HERS Heart and Estrogen/progestin Replacement Study · VTE venous thromboembolism · TIA transient ischemic attack · RR / HR relative risk / hazard ratio · LDL low-density lipoprotein · HDL high-density lipoprotein · BP blood pressure

How ischemia presents differently

Two myths, corrected

  • “Heart disease is a man’s disease.” CVD causes 1 in 3 deaths in US women, about one a minute; more than 60 million US women live with it.
  • “Heart attacks happen after menopause.” More than 10,000 US women under 45 have an MI each year, and under 65 a woman’s acute-MI mortality is almost twice a man’s.
  • Men have more CVD when young; the sexes are level at 60–79, and women overtake men at 80 and over.
  • Age counts as a coronary risk factor from over 50 in women (over 40 in men).

What looks different in women Board add-on

  • More often atypical or accompanying symptoms with MI — dyspnea, fatigue, nausea, jaw or back pain — and so later diagnosis and treatment.
  • Diabetes and smoking raise coronary risk proportionally more in women.
  • Causes that cluster in women: SCAD (young and peripartum women, fibromuscular dysplasia), Takotsubo cardiomyopathy (postmenopausal women after emotional stress; Section 19), MINOCA and coronary microvascular dysfunction.

Pregnancy-related cardiovascular conditions

Pregnancy is a cardiovascular stress test. A woman who develops hypertension or diabetes under its load is showing a susceptibility that comes back later, so ask about pregnancy history in every woman.

  • Female-specific enhancers on the slides: age at menarche, postmenopausal status and pregnancy complications. Premenstrual syndrome is linked to later hypertension (HR 1.4; 3.3 for hypertension before 40).
  • Menarche: a U-shaped curve, lowest at 13; menarche at 10 or younger gives RR 1.27 for CHD, and late menarche (17 or older) also raises it.
  • Preeclampsia (hypertension and proteinuria, usually after 32 weeks; 2–8% of pregnancies) predicts heart failure, stroke, CHD and later hypertension. Gestational diabetes predicts type 2 diabetes; gestational hypertension and multiple gestation predict heart failure.
  • Pregnancy loss: miscarriage roughly 1.4× CHD; stillbirth HR 2.65 and more than three miscarriages HR 8.90 for later MI. Preterm delivery also raises later cardiovascular hospitalisation.
  • A 50-year cohort found the strongest predictors of CVD death were glycosuria (HR 4.2), early-onset preeclampsia (3.6) and pre-existing hypertension (3.5); combinations multiply risk. Obstetricians are many young women’s primary-care doctors — the chance to spot risk early.

Menopause and hormone therapy

  • ACC/AHA guidelines give postmenopausal status the same weight as male sex; early natural menopause (44 or younger) carries more CVD.
  • Estrogen helps the vessels (eNOS-driven vasodilation, angiogenesis, less fibrosis and oxidative stress). Its loss brings sympathetic activation and insulin-resistance features — higher BP, visceral fat, dyslipidemia (Board add-on higher LDL and triglycerides, lower HDL).
  • But menopause is not proven causal: in the alternative paradigm, risk is already climbing through the reproductive years and menopause only adjusts it.
  • WHI and HERS: hormone therapy did not reduce cardiovascular events and may have increased them — but those women were more than 10 years past menopause.
  • The timing hypothesis: MHT started within 10 years of menopause and before age 60 appears to cut CHD by about 50% and all-cause mortality by about 30%. Started late, it does nothing. ELITE: estradiol slowed carotid intima thickening only in women under 6 years post-menopause.
  • First, though, control the traditional risk factors: BP, cholesterol, diet, smoking, weight, and at least 30 minutes of exercise most days.

Q: A 38-year-old has had preeclampsia in two pregnancies and gestational diabetes in one. Why does this change her wellness visit?

Answer

These are female-specific ASCVD risk enhancers: preeclampsia predicts heart failure, CHD, stroke and hypertension, and gestational diabetes predicts type 2 diabetes. Screen her for hypertension, diabetes and lipids early, and treat her risk factors more aggressively.

Q: Why did WHI and HERS show no cardiovascular benefit from hormone therapy, and who might benefit?

Answer

Their participants were mostly more than 10 years past menopause. Under the timing hypothesis, MHT started within 10 years of menopause and before 60, in a woman at low cardiovascular risk, appears protective; late initiation is not.

Q: A 51-year-old, a year past menopause, has migraine with aura and a 10-year ASCVD risk of 6%. Which hormone-therapy traffic-light category is she in?

Answer

Yellow — caution (migraine with aura and a 10-year risk of 5–7.4%).
