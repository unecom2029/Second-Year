Mesenteric Ischemia

Dr. Ciraulo called it “probably one of the most lethal things in the world… because people don’t pay attention to it.” The story is an older patient with a poor appetite and vague belly pain who turns up with dead bowel. Take abdominal pain in elderly patients seriously — especially when they have atrial fibrillation.

Abbreviations used in this section SMA / IMA superior / inferior mesenteric artery · SMV superior mesenteric vein · AF atrial fibrillation · NOMI non-occlusive mesenteric ischemia · CTA CT angiography · AAA abdominal aortic aneurysm

The blood supply

  • Celiac (foregut), SMA (midgut), IMA (hindgut).
  • Two collateral highways: the pancreaticoduodenal arcade (celiac to SMA) and the marginal artery (SMA to IMA). They are why one slowly narrowing vessel is often silent, and why the surgeon checks IMA back-bleeding during AAA repair (Section 32).
  • Board add-on The watershed zones — the splenic flexure and the rectosigmoid — are where low-flow ischemic colitis shows up.

Diagnosis and management ★ HY

🔑 Acute mesenteric ischemia in one line

An older patient with AF and severe abdominal pain out of proportion to a soft, benign-feeling abdomen Board add-on — get a CT angiogram now. Lactate, leukocytosis and peritonitis come late, when the bowel is already dying.

  • High index of suspicion first. Plain film: ileus, pneumatosis (late). Duplex can show SMA stenosis.
  • CTA is the diagnostic study — it shows the bowel and the vessels; the SMA clot “shows up really nicely, especially on the sagittal view.” A mesenteric angiogram is the gold standard and lets you treat at the same time.
  • “When in doubt, operate” — “better to have a negative laparotomy than to go in and see this.” At surgery: restore flow, resect what is dead, and often second-look the bowel Board add-on. About 100 cm of small bowel is needed to live without parenteral nutrition (recording).
  • Chronic disease: mesenteric duplex (about 80% sensitive and specific), then CTA as the test of choice; angiography with endovascular treatment.

Q: An 80-year-old with atrial fibrillation who is not anticoagulated has sudden severe periumbilical pain. She is writhing, but her abdomen is soft with mild tenderness. What is the diagnosis, the first imaging, and the definitive treatment?

Answer

Acute mesenteric ischemia from an SMA embolus (pain out of proportion to the exam). CT angiography, then embolectomy, resection of any dead bowel, and lifelong anticoagulation.

Q: A 60-year-old woman who smokes has had abdominal pain 30 minutes after every meal for months. She has lost 9 kg because she is afraid to eat. Diagnosis, best test and treatment?

Answer

Chronic mesenteric ischemia (intestinal angina). CTA, then revascularisation (endovascular or bypass) before the bowel infarcts.

Q: A patient in septic shock on high-dose norepinephrine develops abdominal pain and rising lactate. CTA shows patent mesenteric arteries. What is it, and how is it treated?

Answer

Non-occlusive mesenteric ischemia — vasospasm from low flow and vasopressors. Bowel rest, antibiotics, fluid resuscitation and improving cardiac output; resect bowel only if it infarcts.
