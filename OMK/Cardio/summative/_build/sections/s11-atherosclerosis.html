Section 11 · Atherosclerosis & Ischemia

Arteriosclerosis Versus Atherosclerosis

Dr. Gardner framed the whole vascular lecture as ordinary cell pathology happening inside a high-pressure plumbing system. Injury, inflammation and repair are the same processes you met in general pathology; the pressure is what turns a small lesion into a heart attack forty years later. This section sorts out the two “annoying words”, then follows a plaque from the first injured endothelial cell to the event that kills.

Two annoying words

🔑 The umbrella and the subset

Arteriosclerosis means any hardening of the arteries — it is the umbrella term. Atherosclerosis is the one kind caused by an atheroma, the lipid-filled plaque in the intima. Every case of atherosclerosis is arteriosclerosis; most arteriosclerosis is not atherosclerosis.

⚠️ Two look-alike names

Fibromuscular intimal hyperplasia is acquired, after a stent, a balloon or a graft. Fibromuscular dysplasia is a rare, largely inherited condition in which segments of the arterial wall are replaced by connective tissue, giving a “string of beads” angiogram and aneurysms at several sites. Board add-on Fibromuscular dysplasia classically affects the renal arteries of young women and causes secondary hypertension (Section 28).

The response to vascular injury

Everything in this section starts with the endothelium, so it is worth being clear about what a healthy one does.

  • Intact endothelium means no clot, no inflammation and a relaxed vessel. It makes prostacyclin, thrombomodulin, heparin-like molecules and plasminogen activator, and it dilates the vessel with nitric oxide.
  • Damaged or “activated” endothelium flips the switch: clotting, leak, inflammation and constriction. Atherosclerosis, hypertension, vasculitis and thrombosis are all versions of that one switch.
  • Strip the endothelium off and collagen is exposed, so platelets bind immediately (through von Willebrand factor) and clotting starts.
  • On the lab sheet, endothelial dysfunction shows up as prolonged clotting times, a raised D-dimer and a raised CRP.
  • Diseases with major endothelial dysfunction: diabetes, which is ultimately a microvascular disease, hypertension, heart and kidney failure, inflammatory bowel disease, stroke and atherosclerosis.

Cellular and mechanical consequences of injury

  1. Mechanical: flow. Turbulent flow at branch points and ostia stresses the endothelium, while smooth laminar flow protects it. That is why plaques sit at branch points, and why they are patchy, not circumferential.
  2. Mechanical: pressure. Hypertension damages the endothelium directly and, over years, lifts it off and lets plasma proteins pile up underneath (the hyaline change above).
  3. Cellular: the stereotyped response. Any injury that strips the endothelium, or reaches the media, makes smooth muscle cells migrate across the internal elastic lamina into the intima, proliferate and lay down matrix. The result is intimal thickening.
  4. The long-run direction is stenosis. Intimal thickening partly resolves, but the tendency is toward a narrower lumen. In large vessels, repeated injury is atherosclerosis.

How a plaque progresses, and its natural history ★ HY

The lecture’s model is response to injury: atherosclerosis is an aberrant, chronic wound-healing reaction in the intima.

  1. Endothelial injury. Hyperlipidemia (LDL is directly toxic to endothelium), hypertension and turbulent flow, smoking, glycation in diabetes, and viruses that “nick” the endothelium.
  2. LDL enters the intima and is oxidized. The injured endothelium releases cytokines and puts out adhesion molecules.
  3. Monocytes enter and become macrophages, which eat the oxidized LDL and become foam cells. Collections of foam cells are the fatty streak.
  4. Platelets adhere and release growth factors such as platelet-derived growth factor.
  5. Smooth muscle cells migrate in from the media and proliferate, with more matrix and T cells, building fibrous tissue. This makes the fibrofatty atheroma.
  6. Lipid accumulates, cells die and calcium is deposited, forming a necrotic core that is often visible on X-ray.

Anatomy of the plaque

  • Everything sits inside the intima, inside the internal elastic lamina. The media may thin underneath it, but the plaque is intimal.
  • From the lumen down: endothelium, then the fibrous cap (smooth muscle cells, macrophages, foam cells, collagen), then the necrotic core (cell debris, foam cells, calcium and cholesterol crystals, which dissolve during processing and leave clefts on histology).
  • The four components in one line: cells, matrix, lipid and calcium.

Where plaques form

In the order the lecture gave them, which is also the First Aid order: abdominal aorta (and iliacs), coronary arteries, popliteal arteries, internal carotids, circle of Willis. Board add-on First Aid’s hook is “a copy cat named Willis”.

Thin cap versus thick cap: the natural history

💥

Thin-cap plaque (vulnerable)

  • A big lipid core under a thin cap, with many macrophages.
  • Prone to rupture and hemorrhage into the plaque, which exposes the thrombogenic core.
  • Sudden thrombosis of the vessel: myocardial infarction and sudden cardiac death.
  • Board add-on Often not the tightest stenosis before it ruptures.
🧱

Thick-cap or fibrocalcific plaque (stable)

  • More collagen, more calcium.
  • Chronic injury, surface erosion and calcification make it grow slowly.
  • A critical stenosis that limits flow on exertion: stable angina (Section 12).

The outcomes of a plaque

  • Thrombosis on the surface, occluding the vessel.
  • Hemorrhage into the plaque, causing sudden instability or occlusion.
  • Rupture with embolism downstream. Board add-on Cholesterol emboli after angiography give livedo reticularis, blue toes and acute kidney injury with palpable pulses.
  • Ongoing inflammatory remodelling that weakens the wall into an aneurysm, which can rupture or dissect (Section 32).
  • The summary sentence the lecture wants: myocardial infarction, stroke and peripheral vascular disease are all consequences of atherosclerosis.
🩺 “Atherosclerotic cardiovascular death in one slide”

The left main coronary artery at autopsy: lipid, foam cells and cholesterol clefts under a thin cap; hemorrhage into the plaque breaks through into the lumen; thrombus occludes the artery; fatal infarction. In Dr. Gardner’s words, forty years of slow injury, then a sudden event. Board add-on Fatty streaks are already present in the aortas of teenagers.

Risk factors and epidemiology

Hyperlipidemia

the main driver

LDL is directly toxic to the endothelium and is the raw material of the foam cell.

Smoking

initial injury

A major contributor to the first endothelial injury.

Hypertension

injury

Pressure and turbulent flow damage the endothelium.

Diabetes

injury

High glucose glycates endothelial surface proteins.

Age, male sex, family history

fixed

Family history roughly means inherited lipid handling and clotting responses. Board add-on Postmenopausal status is a fixed risk factor too.

  • Risk factors multiply rather than add. At age 55, stacking high systolic pressure, high cholesterol, low HDL, diabetes, smoking and LVH on the ECG takes the 10-year cardiovascular risk past 50%.
  • Online calculators show that a typical person’s 10-year cardiovascular risk is about the same as their 10-year cancer risk.
  • Atherosclerosis rarely travels alone: it co-exists with hypertension, ischemic heart disease and heart failure. That is why Dr. Scully pairs it with the lipid drugs (Section 31) and the antiplatelet drugs, which stop clot forming on a ruptured plaque.

Q: An 80-year-old has an incidental plain film showing “tram-track” calcification of both femoral arteries. His pulses are normal and he has no claudication. What is the lesion, and why is he asymptomatic?

Answer

Mönckeberg medial calcific sclerosis. The calcium sits in the media, so the lumen is not narrowed. It is a consequence of ageing and is usually harmless.

Q: A kidney biopsy from a patient with a blood pressure of 230/140, headache and papilledema shows arterioles with concentric, laminated wall thickening and fibrinoid necrosis. Name the lesion. How would the arterioles of a patient with 20 years of well-controlled diabetes differ?

Answer

Hyperplastic (“onion-skin”) arteriolosclerosis, the lesion of malignant hypertension. The diabetic patient would show hyaline arteriolosclerosis: a homogeneous, glassy pink wall from leaked plasma protein, without the layers.

Q: Six months after a coronary stent, a patient’s angina returns. Angiography shows the stented segment has narrowed again. What process is responsible?

Answer

Fibromuscular intimal hyperplasia — the stereotyped response to injury. The stent damaged the wall, smooth muscle cells migrated from the media into the intima, proliferated and laid down matrix, and the lumen narrowed.

Q: Which cell becomes a foam cell, what does it eat, and in which layer of the artery does the whole atheroma — necrotic core included — sit?

Answer

A macrophage, derived from a monocyte, that has ingested oxidized LDL. The atheroma sits in the intima, inside the internal elastic lamina.

Q: Why do atherosclerotic plaques cluster at branch points and ostia rather than along straight segments?

Answer

Turbulent flow there activates and injures the endothelium — faster turnover, apoptosis and a rough surface — whereas laminar flow keeps the endothelium in its quiet, protective state.

Q: A 52-year-old man dies suddenly. At autopsy, the left anterior descending artery has a plaque narrowing it by only 50%, with a large lipid core, a thin fibrous cap that has torn, and occlusive thrombus on top. A second plaque elsewhere narrows a vessel by 85% but has a thick collagenous cap. Which plaque killed him, and what was the other one more likely to cause?

Answer

The thin-cap (vulnerable) plaque: it ruptured, exposed its thrombogenic core and caused occlusive thrombosis. The thick-cap fibrocalcific plaque is a stable critical stenosis, which typically causes exertional (stable) angina. Degree of stenosis does not predict which plaque ruptures.
