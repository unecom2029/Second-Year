Connective Tissue Disorders of the Aorta

Dr. Gardner’s first reason why a vessel wall gives way is weak material: a faulty structural protein, or faulty signalling around it. Given decades of high-pressure flow, that weakness shows up as an aortic root aneurysm, a dissection or an arterial rupture — often in someone young. The lecture compared Marfan with vascular Ehlers–Danlos; Loeys–Dietz and Turner complete the board list.

Why the aorta fails

  • Fibrillin-1 builds the microfibrils that scaffold elastic fibres, and it normally sequesters TGF-β. In Marfan the elastic lamellae fragment and TGF-β signalling rises, giving cystic medial degeneration of the root (Section 32). Board add-on
  • Collagen III gives hollow organs and arteries their tensile strength; in vascular EDS medium arteries, bowel and uterus can rupture without warning.
  • The aortic root stretches, the cusps are pulled apart (aortic regurgitation), and the weak media dissects — usually type A.
  • A bicuspid aortic valve is itself an aortopathy: every patient needs the ascending aorta imaged (Section 24).

Management Board add-on

  • Regular imaging of the aortic root and ascending aorta (echo, then MRA or CTA), and screening of first-degree relatives.
  • Slow the dilation: a beta-blocker (less shear stress) and/or an ARB (losartan), which also damps TGF-β signalling.
  • Prophylactic aortic root replacement before it ruptures — in Marfan typically around a 5.0 cm root (earlier with rapid growth or a family history of dissection), and at smaller sizes in Loeys–Dietz. A valve-sparing root operation keeps the patient’s own valve.
  • Avoid contact sports and heavy isometric lifting; avoid fluoroquinolones Board add-on; plan pregnancy carefully (high dissection risk).
  • Vascular EDS: avoid invasive procedures where possible — even angiography can tear vessels.
  • Turner syndrome: “suspect Turner? Get an echo” for coarctation and BAV; image the aorta before and during any pregnancy.

Q: A tall 25-year-old with long fingers and an upward lens dislocation has a 4.9 cm aortic root and mitral valve prolapse. Diagnosis, gene, and how do you reduce his dissection risk?

Answer

Marfan syndrome, FBN1 (fibrillin-1), autosomal dominant. Serial root imaging, a beta-blocker and/or losartan, avoiding contact sports, and prophylactic root replacement at about 5.0 cm (sooner with rapid growth or a family history of dissection).

Q: A 28-year-old with thin translucent skin and easy bruising dies of a spontaneous splenic artery rupture. Her mother died of a ruptured colon at 35. Which gene, and what is unusual about the arterial risk compared with Marfan?

Answer

Vascular Ehlers–Danlos syndrome — COL3A1 (collagen III). Unlike Marfan, where the risk centres on the aortic root, vEDS carries a high risk of rupture of medium-sized arteries (and bowel and uterus), often without a preceding aneurysm.

Q: A child with hypertelorism, a bifid uvula and tortuous neck arteries has an aortic root of 4.2 cm. Diagnosis, pathway, and why the surgical threshold differs?

Answer

Loeys–Dietz syndrome — mutations in the TGF-β receptor pathway. It dissects at smaller diameters than Marfan, so the root is replaced earlier.

Q: A short 16-year-old girl with a webbed neck and primary amenorrhea has arm hypertension and weak femoral pulses. Which two cardiovascular lesions are most likely, and what later aortic risk follows?

Answer

Turner syndrome with coarctation and a bicuspid aortic valve. Both add to the risk of aortic dilation and dissection, especially in pregnancy.

Q: Why does Marfan syndrome cause aortic regurgitation, and why is mitral valve prolapse so common?

Answer

The dilating aortic root stretches the sinuses and pulls the aortic cusps apart, so they cannot coapt. The abnormal connective tissue also makes the mitral leaflets myxomatous and floppy — MVP in over 90%.
