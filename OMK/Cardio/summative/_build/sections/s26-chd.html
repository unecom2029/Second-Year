Congenital Heart Disease: Cyanotic Versus Acyanotic

Congenital heart disease is the most common birth defect — about 1 in 125 live births, “you are going to see it.” It forms in the first 8 weeks of gestation, and about 90% has no known cause. The pediatric cardiologist’s one-line physics is the key to every shunt: blood goes from high pressure to low. Sort each lesion by two questions — is the baby blue, and is lung blood flow up or down?

Mechanisms: the fetal circulation and the first breath

  • In the fetus, gas exchange happens in the placenta. Oxygenated blood returns by the umbilical vein and ductus venosus to the right atrium.
  • About a third crosses the foramen ovale to the left atrium; most of the rest goes RV to pulmonary artery and crosses the ductus arteriosus to the aorta, bypassing the fluid-filled lungs.
  • At the first breath the lungs open, PVR falls and oxygen rises. The ductus closes functionally within about 24 hours; left-atrial pressure now exceeds right and pushes the foramen ovale flap shut (sealed by 6–12 months).
  • Any hole left open now shunts left to right, from the high-pressure side.
  • The septa form in early gestation: three septa divide the tube (atrial, ventricular, and the spiral septum splitting the truncus into aorta and pulmonary artery), and the endocardial cushions divide the AV canal. A step that goes wrong leaves a hole or swapped great arteries.

Acyanotic lesions: left-to-right shunts

Everything that reaches the body is oxygenated, so the baby is pink. The cost is extra pulmonary blood flow and volume overload: tachypnea, poor feeding, sweating with feeds, failure to thrive, heart failure — and, if untreated for years, pulmonary hypertension.

Eisenmenger syndrome — when a left-to-right shunt reverses

  1. A long-standing left-to-right shunt (VSD, ASD, PDA) floods the lungs.
  2. The pulmonary vessels remodel, and pulmonary hypertension becomes fixed.
  3. RV pressure climbs above LV pressure, and the shunt reverses to right-to-left.
  4. Late cyanosis, clubbing and polycythemia appear. At this point closing the defect no longer helps. Board add-on An uncorrected PDA gives differential cyanosis — blue toes, pink fingers — because the reversed shunt enters below the arm vessels.

Cyanotic lesions: the five T’s ★ HY

Deoxygenated blood reaches the systemic circulation. These babies are blue early, often in the first hours or days, and many depend on the ductus.

Tetralogy of Fallot in more depth

  • The narrow pulmonic outflow forces deoxygenated RV blood across the VSD into the overriding aorta — a right-to-left shunt.
  • Tet spell: crying, feeding or agitation lowers SVR (or raises PVR), worsening outflow obstruction and right-to-left shunting — blue lips and limbs, hyperpnea. Knee-to-chest or squatting raises SVR and pushes blood back to the lungs.
  • Repair is soon after birth or later in infancy, depending on how tight the pulmonary stenosis is. The first operation was the Blalock–Taussig shunt — subclavian artery to pulmonary artery — developed by Alfred Blalock, Helen Taussig and lab technician Vivien Thomas (the film Something the Lord Made).
  • Board add-on Caused by anterosuperior displacement of the infundibular septum; associated with 22q11 deletion.

Cyanotic features and the chest X-ray ★ HY

  • Central cyanosis — circumoral, tongue and lips — in a baby with clear, equal breath sounds. The lecture’s case: a saturation of 80% on 100% oxygen.
  • Board add-on The hyperoxia test: a lung problem improves on 100% oxygen; a cyanotic cardiac shunt does not.
  • Later signs of chronic cyanosis: clubbing, polycythemia (and its stroke and clotting risk), squatting in older children with tetralogy, and paradoxical emboli or brain abscess. Board add-on

Dr. Gardner’s point about the genetics: about 90% of congenital heart defects are still genetically unexplained, and the known genes are mostly transcription factors — probably shifting the timing of development of parts of the heart.

Presentations, complications and comorbidities

  • Reasons to refer to a pediatric cardiologist: a possibly structural murmur (any diastolic murmur, grade 3 or more, a thrill, harsh or holosystolic, an abnormal S2 or click, or one that does not change with position), exertional chest pain, a possible arrhythmia, recurrent syncope.
  • Large left-to-right shunts: tachypnea, poor feeding, sweating with feeds, failure to thrive, frequent respiratory infections, heart failure — and Eisenmenger syndrome if untreated.
  • Cyanotic lesions: blue at birth or within days; ductal-dependent lesions (HLHS, critical coarctation, transposition with an intact septum) crash into shock as the ductus closes — start PGE₁.
  • ASD and PFO: atrial arrhythmias and paradoxical embolism in adults.
  • Endocarditis risk with VSDs, bicuspid valves and repaired disease (Section 21).
  • Comorbidities come with the syndrome: DiGeorge (thymic aplasia and T-cell deficiency, hypocalcemia, cleft palate), Turner (horseshoe kidney, ovarian failure), Down (duodenal atresia, early Alzheimer disease, leukemia), Noonan (short stature, webbed neck) Board add-on.

Q: A newborn stays at 80% saturation on 100% oxygen, with clear lungs and a 3/6 systolic murmur. The CXR shows a boot-shaped heart. Name the four defects, and the one that decides how blue the baby is.

Answer

Tetralogy of Fallot: VSD, pulmonary (RVOT) stenosis, RVH and an overriding aorta. The degree of pulmonary stenosis sets how much blood shunts right to left.

Q: A 5-day-old who was well at discharge presents in shock with weak pulses everywhere. Echo shows a tiny left ventricle. What drug do you start immediately, and why?

Answer

Prostaglandin E1, to reopen the ductus arteriosus. In hypoplastic left heart syndrome the RV supplies the body through the ductus.

Q: A newborn with transposition of the great arteries and an intact ventricular septum turns deeply cyanotic on day 2. Why now?

Answer

The ductus arteriosus is closing. Without a VSD, the PDA (and the foramen ovale) was the only place the two parallel circulations could mix. Give PGE₁ and consider balloon atrial septostomy.

Q: A 40-year-old with a leg DVT suddenly has a stroke. A bubble study shows right-to-left passage of contrast at the atrial level. Mechanism? And what heart sound would a large ASD give?

Answer

Paradoxical embolism through a patent foramen ovale or ASD: a venous clot crosses to the left atrium and enters the systemic circulation. A large ASD gives a fixed split S2 from RV volume overload.

Q: A 28-week preterm infant has a continuous murmur, bounding pulses and increasing respiratory difficulty. Lesion and drug? Which drug would you give instead if the baby’s circulation depended on that vessel?

Answer

A PDA, closed with IV indomethacin (or ibuprofen), which blocks prostaglandin synthesis. To keep a ductus open in a ductal-dependent lesion, give PGE₁.

Q: A 25-year-old who had an unrepaired VSD as a child now has cyanosis, clubbing and a hematocrit of 62%. What has happened, and can the VSD simply be closed now?

Answer

Eisenmenger syndrome: years of left-to-right flow caused fixed pulmonary hypertension, RV pressure rose above LV pressure and the shunt reversed. No — with fixed pulmonary hypertension, closing the defect does not help and is dangerous.

Q: Match each to its lesion: trisomy 21; 22q11 deletion; maternal lithium; Williams syndrome; congenital rubella; maternal diabetes.

Answer

Trisomy 21: AV septal defect. 22q11: truncus arteriosus and tetralogy. Lithium: Ebstein anomaly. Williams: supravalvular aortic stenosis. Rubella: PDA (and pulmonary artery stenosis). Maternal diabetes: transposition.
