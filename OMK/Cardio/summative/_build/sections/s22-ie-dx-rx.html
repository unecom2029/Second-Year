Section 22 · Infection & Inflammation

Infective Endocarditis: Diagnosis and Management

The infectious-disease lecturer called the clinical picture “a mess”: anything from rapidly progressive to indolent, so the diagnosis is often delayed. The way through is the same every time — suspect it in anyone with a typical organism in the blood plus a risk factor, draw three sets of cultures before any antibiotic, image the valves, and score it with the 2023 Duke criteria. Treatment is long, bactericidal and often surgical.

Clinical presentation and exam signs

Fever

about 90%

With chills, anorexia, night sweats, myalgia, arthralgia. Common but not diagnostic.

New or changed murmur

about 85%

Holosystolic (mitral regurgitation), diastolic (aortic regurgitation), or holosystolic tricuspid — often absent in right-sided disease.

Splenomegaly, petechiae

about 40% each

Petechiae on the conjunctiva, palate and limbs, more with S. aureus.

Presenting complication

common

Heart failure, stroke, a “renal problem”, back pain from vertebral osteomyelitis.

🧠 Immune complex or embolus?

Immune complexes: Osler nodes, Roth spots, glomerulonephritis, a positive rheumatoid factor. Emboli: Janeway lesions, splinters, petechiae, organ infarcts. Board add-on “Osler = Ouch”, and FROM JANE: Fever, Roth spots, Osler nodes, Murmur, Janeway lesions, Anemia, Nail-bed hemorrhage, Emboli.

Blood cultures, echo and the diagnostic criteria ★ HY

Laboratory findings — mostly non-specific

  • Blood cultures: the bacteremia is continuous but low-level (under 100 organisms per mL), so draw three separate sets, before antibiotics.
  • Blood: anemia (of chronic disease), thrombocytopenia in 15–20%, raised ESR and CRP, hypergammaglobulinemia, and a positive rheumatoid factor in 50–60% — an antibody against the Fc portion of IgG, made in response to circulating immune complexes.
  • Urine: proteinuria, hematuria, pyuria, casts, bacteriuria.
  • A full dental evaluation is recommended — a clue to viridans streptococci or anaerobes.
⚠️ A disagreement on the white count

The lecturer said leukopenia is “not usually seen in IE”; the course outline lists it among the hematology findings. For an exam, a high white count fits acute disease and anemia fits subacute disease; leukopenia is not a typical clue.

🧠 Definite = “2 · 1 + 3 · 5”

Two majors, one major plus three minors, or five minors. Possible is “1 + 1, or 3.” The 2023 version is the most sensitive and works best for left-sided disease.

The full work-up of a vignette

  1. Suspect it: a typical organism in the blood (staphylococci, streptococci, enterococci), or fever plus a murmur, plus a risk factor (IDU, a prosthetic valve, recent dental work, dialysis, immunosuppression).
  2. Look for the peripheral signs and the complication that brought them in.
  3. Three blood-culture sets before antibiotics; basic labs, urinalysis, rheumatoid factor.
  4. Stable? Wait for the cultures. Acutely ill? Draw the three sets, then start empiric antibiotics immediately.
  5. TTE first, then TOE if negative with high suspicion, or for a prosthetic valve; CT for abscess, PET for prosthetics.
  6. Score it with Duke.
  7. Narrow to targeted therapy once the organism is known, and decide on surgery.
  8. Find the source: dental evaluation; colonoscopy for S. gallolyticus; remove infected lines or devices.

Antibiotic regimens and their mechanisms ★ HY

Principles

  • Aggressive — the response is slow because of biofilm and ongoing emboli.
  • Bactericidal, not bacteriostatic — the drug itself must kill the organisms already inside the vegetation, where host defences cannot reach.
  • Long — usually 4–6 weeks IV.
  • Empiric: cover MRSA and MSSA (including S. epidermidis), streptococci and enterococci with vancomycin plus cefepime or ceftriaxone.

🧠 Synergy: why gentamicin is paired with a cell-wall drug for enterococci

Penicillin or ampicillin alone only inhibits enterococci. Damaging the cell wall lets the aminoglycoside get in to its ribosomal target, and together they are bactericidal — which endocarditis requires. Board add-on

Surgical indications

About half of patients eventually need valve surgery, usually replacement. Surgery also drains exudate and removes splenic or renal abscesses. Board add-on The main indications:

  • Heart failure from valve destruction (the commonest indication).
  • Uncontrolled infection: a perivalvular abscess, new heart block, a fistula, or bacteremia persisting despite appropriate antibiotics.
  • Difficult organisms: fungi, highly resistant organisms; S. aureus on a prosthetic valve.
  • Prevention of embolism: a large vegetation (over 10 mm) with embolic events.
  • Prosthetic-valve dehiscence or relapse after a full course.
🩺 Dr. McGrath’s cases
  • A 34-year-old woman who injected heroin had tricuspid endocarditis with septic pulmonary emboli. The vegetation did not clear on antibiotics, so the surgeon did a radical tricuspid repair plus 6 weeks of IV antibiotics — repair first on the right side. She relapsed twice (aortic, then gram-negative), ending in a bioprosthetic replacement.
  • A 38-year-old with an old rheumatic mitral repair developed prosthetic-valve endocarditis two weeks after dental work without antibiotic cover, and needed a redo mitral replacement.
🔑 Who gets antibiotic prophylaxis before dental work Board add-on

Only the highest-risk hearts: a prosthetic valve or prosthetic repair material, prior endocarditis, certain congenital heart disease (unrepaired cyanotic, or within 6 months of repair, or with a residual defect), and a transplanted heart with valve disease. Only for procedures that manipulate gingiva or the periapical region or perforate the mucosa. The usual drug is amoxicillin before the procedure. Mitral valve prolapse or a bicuspid valve alone does not qualify.

Q: An acutely ill patient with suspected endocarditis is in the emergency department. What must happen before the first antibiotic dose, and what is the empiric regimen?

Answer

Draw three separate blood-culture sets — antibiotics before cultures are the commonest cause of culture-negative endocarditis — then start vancomycin plus cefepime (or ceftriaxone) to cover MRSA, MSSA, coagulase-negative staphylococci, streptococci and enterococci.

Q: A patient has a fever of 38.6 °C, known mitral valve prolapse with regurgitation, and Osler nodes. Blood cultures and echo are pending. How many Duke criteria are met, and what is the category so far? What would make it definite?

Answer

Three minor (predisposition, fever, an immunologic phenomenon), so possible endocarditis. One major criterion — a typical organism in two culture sets, or a vegetation on echo — would make it definite (1 major + 3 minor).

Q: The transthoracic echo shows no vegetation in a patient with a high suspicion of endocarditis. What is the next imaging step, and why?

Answer

A transesophageal echo — about 95% sensitive against about 65% for the transthoracic study — which also sizes the vegetation to estimate embolic risk.

Q: Three weeks after dental work, a man with old rheumatic mitral disease has low-grade fevers, painful nodules on his finger pads and retinal hemorrhages with pale centres. Organism, mechanism of the skin and eye findings, and the usual drug?

Answer

Subacute endocarditis from viridans streptococci on a damaged valve. Osler nodes and Roth spots are immune-complex deposits. If penicillin-sensitive: penicillin G or ceftriaxone for 4 weeks.

Q: Why are bactericidal drugs required for endocarditis, and why is gentamicin added to ampicillin for enterococcal disease?

Answer

The dense, poorly perfused vegetation and biofilm protect the organisms from host defences, so the drug must kill them; suppression lets them regrow. Ampicillin alone only inhibits enterococci; its cell-wall damage lets gentamicin reach the 30S ribosome, and the pair is synergistically bactericidal.

Q: A patient with MRSA prosthetic-valve endocarditis is on vancomycin. Which drug is added, what is its mechanism, and why is it chosen here?

Answer

Rifampin, which inhibits DNA-dependent RNA polymerase. It penetrates biofilm on prosthetic material; it is never used alone because resistance develops fast. (Vancomycin works by binding D-Ala-D-Ala.)

Q: On day 10 of appropriate antibiotics for S. aureus aortic-valve endocarditis, a patient’s PR interval lengthens and then he develops complete heart block. What has happened, and what does it mean for management?

Answer

A perivalvular (aortic ring) abscess has extended into the conduction system. This is uncontrolled infection — an indication for surgery. Confirm the abscess on TOE or cardiac CT.
