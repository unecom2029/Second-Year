Complications After STEMI

Three days after leaving hospital with a right coronary stent, Dr. Watkins’s patient walked into the office breathless, unable to lie flat, with a heart rate of 130 and a new holosystolic murmur with a thrill. His teaching point: sudden dyspnea plus a new murmur after an infarct is a mechanical complication until proven otherwise, and the two critical tests are the ECG and the echocardiogram. This section sorts every complication by when it happens, because the timing usually gives the answer.

Mechanical complications ★ HY

Necrotic muscle “blows out”, giving sudden, severe heart failure, respiratory failure and shock. They follow transmural (STEMI) infarcts almost exclusively, and are commoner with a first infarct, single-vessel disease, late or no reperfusion, older age and female sex.

⚠️ Where the sources disagree on mechanical complications
  • Free-wall rupture timing. Dr. Gardner gives 3–7 days (the soft-wall window); Dr. Watkins gives two peaks, 24 hours and 3–5 days; First Aid says 5–14 days; UWorld says half within 5 days and the rest up to 2 weeks. For an exam: first week to two weeks, peaking around days 3–7.
  • The papillary-rupture murmur. The case slide describes a holosystolic apical murmur to the axilla. UWorld points out that acute MR is often only early systolic, and silent in up to half of cases, because left atrial and ventricular pressures equalise quickly. A flash pulmonary edema with no murmur after an inferior MI can still be papillary rupture.

🧠 Matching pairs

Pair each rupture with its artery and its sound. Inferior infarct, apical murmur to the axilla: papillary muscle. Septal rupture, sternal-border murmur with a thrill and an oxygen step-up. Anterior infarct, no murmur, muffled sounds and PEA: free wall.

🩺 After an infarct, the other organs suffer too

Low flow everywhere causes ischemia–reperfusion injury and systemic inflammation: acute kidney injury (in about 40% of patients who survive CPR), acute liver injury with raised enzymes (“shock liver”), and acute lung injury or ARDS. Overall mortality after an infarct is about 30% in the first year, then about 3% a year.

Q: Four days after an anterior MI, a patient becomes hypotensive with a new harsh holosystolic murmur at the left sternal border and a palpable thrill. A pulmonary artery catheter shows oxygen saturation of 62% in the right atrium and 80% in the pulmonary artery. Diagnosis?

Answer

Ventricular septal rupture. Oxygenated blood shunting left to right causes the oxygen step-up. Papillary muscle rupture would give an apical murmur radiating to the axilla with no step-up.

Q: An elderly woman, 4 days after her first, large anterior STEMI, coughs, has sudden chest pain and loses her pulse while the monitor still shows sinus rhythm. What happened?

Answer

Left ventricular free-wall rupture causing hemopericardium and tamponade, presenting as pulseless electrical activity. Day 4 is the soft-wall window.

Q: Three days after an inferior STEMI, a man develops sudden severe dyspnea, pink frothy sputum and hypotension. A soft murmur is heard at the apex. Which structure has failed, and why that one?

Answer

The posteromedial papillary muscle has ruptured, causing acute mitral regurgitation and flash pulmonary edema. It is supplied only by the posterior descending artery (usually from the RCA), whereas the anterolateral muscle has a double supply.

Q: An inferior-wall STEMI patient’s heart rate falls to 38. Which artery is occluded, and why is the rate slow?

Answer

The right coronary artery, which supplies the SA node in about 60% and the AV node in about 90% of people. Ischemia causes sinus bradycardia or AV block. Treat with atropine, then pacing.

Q: Five weeks after an MI, a patient has fever, pleuritic chest pain that eases sitting forward, and a friction rub. What is it, and what is the mechanism? How would the same symptoms on day 2 differ in cause?

Answer

Dressler syndrome (post-cardiac injury syndrome): an autoimmune pericarditis in which antibodies to cardiac antigens released by the infarct form immune complexes in the pericardium. On day 2 the same picture is early fibrinous pericarditis — direct inflammation over a transmural infarct, not autoimmunity.

Q: Two months after a large anterior MI, a patient has worsening heart failure. His ECG still shows ST elevation and deep Q waves in V1–V4. Echo shows a thin segment that bulges outward during systole. Diagnosis, and what two other complications does it carry?

Answer

A true left ventricular aneurysm — a scarred, dyskinetic wall. It also causes mural thrombus with embolism and ventricular tachycardia from re-entry around the scar. Unlike a pseudoaneurysm, it rarely ruptures.

Q: After an inferior STEMI, a patient is hypotensive with distended neck veins but clear lungs. Nitroglycerin is given and the blood pressure falls further. What has been missed, and what should be given instead?

Answer

A right ventricular infarct. The failing right ventricle depends on preload, so nitrates (and diuretics, and opioids) drop its output. Give IV fluids, and confirm with ST elevation in the right-sided lead V4R.
