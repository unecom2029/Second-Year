Valvular Heart Disease

Section 3 covers how each valve lesion sounds and what it does to the pressures. This section is the other half: what the diseased valve looks like, why it got that way, and what is done about it. Dr. Gardner’s two-line summary is the frame: the important valve diseases are on the left (it carries the cardiac output); aortic stenosis is a bicuspid or ageing valve and insufficiency an expanding root; mitral stenosis is rheumatic and insufficiency is myxomatous or from heart failure.

The valve plane, and why the left side matters

  • Two pumps in series: “How much of the cardiac output goes out the pulmonary artery?” 100%.
  • The atrioventricular valves (tricuspid, mitral) are complex — leaflets tethered by chordae to papillary muscles, with the ventricle itself part of the apparatus. The semilunar valves (pulmonic, aortic) are simple check valves. That is why the AV valves can far more often be repaired.
  • All four grow from the fibrous skeleton; the aortic and mitral valves touch (fibrous continuity).
  • Valves have no blood supply of their own and are immune-privileged — one reason replacements work so well (Gardner).
  • Failure modes: stenosis is a pressure load; regurgitation is a volume load (Section 17).

Aortic stenosis and regurgitation

Aortic stenosis — the most common valve problem needing surgery

  • A bimodal disease. A bicuspid valve (1–2% of people — the commonest congenital valve problem in adults) presents in the 40s to 60s. A normal three-leaflet valve calcifies with age and presents in the 70s to 90s.
  • Pathology of calcific AS: micro-injury, scarring and calcification give thick, fused, immobile leaflets. Catherine Otto’s work found inflammatory cells and actual lamellar bone in the leaflets. Statins did not slow it.
  • A bicuspid valve always comes with an aortopathy, so screen for an ascending aortic aneurysm and coarctation (contrast CT of the chest). Dr. McGrath’s 53-year-old with a bicuspid valve had a 5.2 cm ascending aneurysm replaced with the valve.
  • Progression is “almost a log curve” — slow, then rapid. The LV compensates with concentric hypertrophy until it cannot.
  • Symptoms and why: dyspnea (diastolic dysfunction of a hugely hypertrophied LV), angina with clean coronaries (high LV pressure squeezes the subendocardium in diastole), exertional syncope (fixed output), and sudden death.
  • Once symptoms appear, about half the patients are dead within 2 years without replacement. Board add-on Classic survival: angina about 5 years, syncope 3, heart failure 2.
  • Board add-on Heyde syndrome: AS with GI bleeding from angiodysplasia, because shear stress across the valve cleaves von Willebrand factor.
  • Management: no medical therapy slows it, so monitor, then replace. Dr. Qazi intervenes once it is severe on echo; guidelines trigger on severe AS with any symptom or with an EF under 50%. Severe AS is preload-dependent: avoid nitrates and over-diuresis. Details of TAVR versus SAVR are in Section 25.

Aortic regurgitation

  • The leaflets fail to meet, because of the root or the valve. An aortic root aneurysm (Marfan) stretches the sinuses and pulls the cusps apart; a dissection can unzip a commissure; endocarditis destroys a leaflet.
  • Board add-on The commonest cause in resource-rich countries is a bicuspid valve or root dilation; rheumatic disease in resource-limited ones. Acute AR comes from dissection or endocarditis and presents as sudden pulmonary edema and shock.
  • Volume overload: the LV dilates and compensates for decades — some patients present with an EF of 10–20%.
  • Surgical triggers (slide 61): severe AR; moderate AR with decompensation; EF under 50%; end-systolic dimension over 55 mm or end-diastolic over 75 mm — “remember the sarcomere: overstretch kills.”
⚠️ “Valves gone wrong — a bad deal”: why valves are fixed early

Dr. McGrath’s upstream cascade: aortic regurgitation overloads the LV, which dilates; the mitral annulus dilates, giving mitral regurgitation; the LA overloads, giving pulmonary hypertension; the RV overloads and the tricuspid annulus dilates, giving tricuspid regurgitation; the RA dilates, and the patient ends with massive cardiomegaly, edema and organ failure. Fixed pulmonary hypertension and RV failure make surgery far harder, so valves are fixed earlier rather than later.

Mitral stenosis, regurgitation and prolapse

Mitral stenosis

  • Most common cause worldwide: rheumatic heart disease — everywhere in South Asia, where balloon valvuloplasty is bread-and-butter, but rare in the US. About 80% of rheumatic MS is in women, and pregnancy often unmasks it. Board add-on
  • Pathology: fused commissures and leaflets, thick short chordae, calcification — the “fish-mouth” valve. A latent period of 20–40 years after rheumatic fever.
  • Consequences: a big LA, AF and LA thrombus (stroke can be the first sign), pulmonary edema and hemoptysis, pulmonary hypertension, right heart failure.
  • Severe when the valve area is ≤ 1.5 cm² (normal about 4).
  • Treatment:
    • Beta-blockers (or rate control) to lengthen diastole and filling time, and diuretics for congestion.
    • Anticoagulation for AF — Board add-on warfarin, not a DOAC, in rheumatic MS.
    • Percutaneous balloon mitral commissurotomy for severe symptomatic disease with a suitable valve — it “buys time to tune the patient up”.
    • Surgical commissurotomy or mitral valve replacement; a tissue valve in women of childbearing age to avoid warfarin, with a mechanical valve at a later redo.

Mitral regurgitation and prolapse

  • Myxomatous degeneration (MVP): long, floppy leaflets infiltrated with proteoglycans, billowing into the LA. Tension stretches the chordae, so it worsens; a ruptured chord gives a flail leaflet and acute heart failure — surgery before discharge. Associated with Marfan and Ehlers–Danlos; a not-uncommon cause of sudden death in young women (Gardner).
  • Most MVP does not cause significant MR or need surgery.
  • Secondary (functional) MR: the valve is normal but a dilated LV stretches the annulus and pulls on the papillary muscles, or an inferior infarct tethers the posterior leaflet (ischemic MR; Section 15 for rupture).
  • Pathophysiology: LA volume overload, LA dilation, AF, pulmonary hypertension, right heart failure.
🔑 Repair or replace?
  • Organic (degenerative) MR: repair. Repair keeps the valve and its chordae and papillary muscles helping the LV, and a good repair lasts a lifetime. Dr. McGrath repairs over 95% of repairable valves — send patients to a surgeon with those odds.
  • Functional MR: replace. The LV keeps remodelling, so repairs fail at 1–3 years.

Rheumatic heart disease

Section 3 covers the rheumatic murmurs; here is the pathology Dr. Gardner wants.

  1. Group A streptococcal pharyngitis in a child or teenager, untreated.
  2. Molecular mimicry: antibodies and T cells against the streptococcal M protein cross-react with cardiac self-antigens — in about 3% of infections.
  3. Acute rheumatic fever: polyarthritis, pancarditis, subcutaneous nodules, erythema marginatum, Sydenham chorea. Board add-on “J♥NES”.
  4. Healing is fibrosis: chronic scarring of the valves, decades later — mitral stenosis.
  • Valves involved: mitral > aortic > tricuspid > pulmonic. Rheumatic tricuspid or pulmonic disease almost never occurs without mitral disease. Board add-on
  • Histology: Aschoff bodies — clumps of T cells, plasma cells and macrophages; the activated macrophages with wavy “caterpillar” chromatin are Anitschkow cells Board add-on. Chronic valves: thick leaflets, fused commissures, short thick chordae.
  • Now rare in the West because strep throats are treated; common in low-income countries — Dr. McGrath’s Iranian partner operated on rheumatic mitral disease every year.

🧠 Why carcinoid attacks the right side Board add-on

Serotonin from hepatic metastases of a carcinoid tumour reaches the right heart and lays down fibrous plaques on the tricuspid and pulmonic valves, giving TR and pulmonic stenosis. The lungs break serotonin down, so the left-sided valves are spared — unless there is a right-to-left shunt or a bronchial primary.

Q: A 55-year-old has severe calcific aortic stenosis — “early” for age-related disease. What is the most likely underlying valve anatomy, and what two other aortic problems should you look for?

Answer

A congenitally bicuspid aortic valve, which calcifies decades earlier than a normal one. Every bicuspid valve has an aortopathy: screen for an ascending aortic aneurysm and coarctation.

Q: Why does severe aortic stenosis cause angina with normal coronary arteries, and why does the onset of symptoms matter so much?

Answer

Massive concentric hypertrophy and a very high LV pressure compress the subendocardium in diastole, when the coronaries fill, while the thick muscle needs more oxygen. Once angina, syncope or dyspnea appears, about half are dead within 2 years without valve replacement.

Q: A 35-year-old woman who grew up in India has an opening snap, a low diastolic rumble at the apex, atrial fibrillation and hemoptysis. What is the lesion, what does the valve look like, and what are the treatment steps?

Answer

Rheumatic mitral stenosis: thick leaflets, fused commissures and short chordae — a fish-mouth orifice. Treat with beta-blockers (longer filling time), diuretics, warfarin for the AF, and balloon commissurotomy if severe and the valve is suitable; otherwise surgery.

Q: A 54-year-old with a known mitral click develops weeks of worsening exertional dyspnea and a new MR murmur; TEE shows a torn chord to the posterior leaflet. A second patient with ischemic cardiomyopathy has severe MR with a normal-looking valve. Repair or replace, and why?

Answer

The first is organic (degenerative) MR — repair, which keeps the valve apparatus helping the LV and lasts a lifetime. The second is functional MR — replace (and treat the LV), because the ventricle keeps remodelling and repairs fail within 1–3 years.

Q: A 40-year-old with Marfan syndrome has severe aortic regurgitation with an end-systolic LV dimension of 58 mm and an EF of 48%. Is surgery indicated, and what operation?

Answer

Yes — an EF under 50% and an end-systolic dimension over 55 mm are both triggers (“overstretch kills”). He needs surgical aortic root and valve replacement; TAVR is contraindicated in pure AR with a dilated root.

Q: A patient with flushing, diarrhea and liver metastases develops edema, ascites and a holosystolic murmur that gets louder with inspiration. Which valves are involved, and why not the left side?

Answer

Carcinoid heart disease: fibrous plaques on the tricuspid (regurgitation) and pulmonic (stenosis) valves. The lungs inactivate serotonin, so the left-sided valves are spared.
