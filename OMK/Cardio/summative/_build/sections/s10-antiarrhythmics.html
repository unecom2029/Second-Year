Section 10 · The ECG & Arrhythmias

Antiarrhythmic Drugs

Unlike the other cardiology pharmacology lectures, this one is new material rather than a refresher, and Dr. Scully builds the whole thing on a single skill: knowing which ion carries each phase of the action potential, separately in nodal tissue and in myocytes. Once you have that, every class tells you its own story — which tissue it targets, what it does to the action potential, what it is indicated for, and what goes wrong with it.

The organising idea: rate drugs hit the node, rhythm drugs hit the muscle ★ HY

⏱️

Rate control targets nodal tissue

  • The tissue with automaticity: the sinoatrial and atrioventricular nodes.
  • The nodal upstroke is carried by calcium, and the rate is set by sympathetic tone.
  • So: class II (beta-blockers) and class IV (verapamil, diltiazem). Digoxin and adenosine also slow the node.
🎼

Rhythm control targets myocytes

  • Atrial and ventricular muscle.
  • Either the sodium upstroke of phase 0, or the potassium repolarization that ends the plateau.
  • So: class I (sodium-channel blockers) and class III (potassium-channel blockers).

Dr. Scully’s practical version: when you hear someone say “we are going to try a rate approach for this patient’s atrial fibrillation,” they mean a beta-blocker or a calcium channel blocker. “Rhythm control” means a class I or class III drug.

🔑 The three facts the whole lecture rests on
  • Phase 0 is calcium in nodal cells and sodium in myocytes. Potassium repolarizes both. That single sentence explains why calcium channel blockers act on the node and sodium-channel blockers act on muscle.
  • Only the myocyte has a plateau, and it is a calcium plateau. It prolongs contraction to get a good squeeze for ejection.
  • The pacemaker cell has automaticity because of the funny current, carried by sodium. It is “funny” because hyperpolarization, not depolarization, is what opens it.
🧠 How autonomic tone sets the rate — both limbs meet at one enzyme

Sympathetic stimulation acts at β1 receptors through Gs, raising adenylyl cyclase activity and cyclic AMP, which activates protein kinase A. That phosphorylates the calcium channels and phospholamban, giving a steeper phase 4, faster conduction and a stronger contraction. Parasympathetic stimulation acts at M2 receptors through Gi, lowering cyclic AMP, leaving the cell more hyperpolarized with a flatter phase 4 and a slower rate. Both converge on adenylyl cyclase, and therefore on protein kinase A.

Anything that makes phase 4 steeper, makes the threshold more negative, or leaves the resting potential less negative will speed the heart up. Raise the threshold without changing the slope and the drift simply takes longer to get there, so the rate falls. The fastest pacemaker always wins: sinoatrial node 60 to 100, atrioventricular node and bundle of His 40 to 60, Purkinje fibres 20 to 40.

⚠️ The goal, and the catch

The aim is to restore and maintain normal sinus rhythm without causing proarrhythmia. Dr. Scully’s warning is blunt: “They are all proarrhythmic at the right dose.” These drugs are also not cleanly selective, and several act in more than one class — sotalol and amiodarone most obviously.

Class I — sodium-channel blockers

All class I drugs block the fast sodium channel that carries phase 0 in myocytes, so the upstroke and the conduction velocity both slow. In nodal tissue they also raise the threshold slightly and flatten phase 4, but that effect is minor, which is why class I counts as a rhythm drug. Some of them also block potassium channels, and that is where the three subclasses part company.

🔑 Use-dependent block, and why it matters for ischemic tissue

Sodium channels cycle through resting, open and inactivated states. Class I drugs bind with high affinity to the open and inactivated states, which makes the block use-dependent: the faster the tissue fires, the more block builds up. That is also why they work preferentially on ischemic tissue, which cannot hold its membrane potential, fires more often, and therefore keeps its channels in the bindable states.

🚕

Upstroke slowing: the “salty CAB driver”

  • From the biggest effect on phase 0 to the smallest: C > A > B.
  • Or, in increasing order, the salty cheeseburger with Bacon And Cheese: B < A < C.
🐻

Repolarization: Goldilocks

  • IA is too long — it prolongs repolarization.
  • IB is too short — it shortens it.
  • IC is just right — no change.
  • Prolonged repolarization means a prolonged refractory period, which means torsades risk for IA, just like class III.
🧠 Why a long refractory period causes torsades

If repolarization is too slow, the voltage-gated sodium channels recover from inactivation before the membrane has dropped back below threshold. They fire again, and the membrane oscillates. Those oscillations are early afterdepolarizations — triggered activity — and repeated ones become torsades de pointes. This is the shared mechanism behind class IA and class III toxicity.

⚠️ Quinidine raises digoxin levels — a stated board favourite

Quinidine displaces digoxin from plasma proteins and reduces its renal clearance, so free digoxin rises. Drug bound to plasma protein is generally inactive, because the effect tracks the free drug — displace it and you have effectively given a bigger dose. Quinidine’s effects on potassium change digoxin’s pharmacodynamics on top of that. Dr. Scully said outright that drug interactions with digoxin are a board favourite. Board add-on The memory hook for the subclass is “the QUEEN PROofreads DISsertations” — Quinidine, Procainamide, Disopyramide.

Class IB — lidocaine and mexiletine

  • They block sodium channels in the open and inactivated states but dissociate rapidly, so the block is mild and washes off between beats in healthy tissue.
  • The result is an almost preferential effect on damaged myocardium, with little effect on normal muscle — ischemic tissue fires more often and holds its channels in the bindable states.
  • Lidocaine is intravenous, and is also the local anesthetic. It is second-line emergency therapy for ventricular arrhythmias; in stable patients it is reserved for troublesome premature ventricular contractions or ventricular tachyarrhythmias. Its adverse effects are central nervous system effects — confusion, dizziness, seizures, because it blocks central sodium channels too — plus methemoglobinemia and tremor. Adjust the dose for hepatic function.
  • Mexiletine is the oral relative, used for chronic ventricular arrhythmias. Board add-on Phenytoin also has class IB activity.
  • Board add-on The hook: class IB is Best after a myocardial infarction; class IC is Contraindicated. Class IB drugs also treat digitalis-induced arrhythmias.

Class IC — flecainide and propafenone

  • Marked slowing of the phase 0 upstroke — the biggest effect of the three subclasses — with repolarization unchanged. They suppress premature ventricular contractions.
  • They widen the QRS, and more so with exertion. The QRS is ventricular depolarization, so a flatter upstroke makes it take longer, and use-dependence means faster rates produce more block.
  • Flecainide is indicated for sustained ventricular tachycardia and for prevention of atrial fibrillation in a structurally normal heart. Adverse effects: new or worsening arrhythmia, sinus-node dysfunction, markedly reduced conduction velocity, and central effects including blurred vision, headache and ataxia.
  • Propafenone is its partner. Board add-on It also has weak beta-blocking activity.
⚠️ The vignette clue for class IC

A prior myocardial infarction, scar, heart failure or any structural disease in the history means avoid class IC — these drugs are proarrhythmic in ischemic and structural heart disease. Dr. Scully’s phrasing was that paying attention to the context clues in your vignettes can keep you from causing an arrhythmia. Board add-on The CAST trial showed higher mortality with class IC drugs after myocardial infarction.

🩺 How a class IC drug produces a tracing that looks like ventricular tachycardia

A case from the arrhythmia lecture: a 42-year-old athlete with known atrial flutter “on meds” arrives with a regular wide-complex rhythm at 250, and the machine reads it as ventricular tachycardia. What actually happened is that the class IC drug slowed the flutter circuit enough that the atrioventricular node could now conduct it 1:1, while use-dependent sodium block widened the QRS further at that rate. The giveaway is that the QRS onset deflects rapidly, and the history is flutter on an antiarrhythmic.

Always give an atrioventricular nodal blocker alongside a class IC drug — that is how this is prevented.

Class II — beta-blockers

  • They block sympathetic input to the pacing regions, giving a flatter phase 4 because the funny-current drift slows, and they prolong repolarization at the atrioventricular node.
  • A longer nodal refractory period reduces re-entry: an aberrant impulse arriving at a node that is still refractory simply stops.
  • Indications: supraventricular and ventricular arrhythmias, especially those triggered by sympathetic activation — exercise, emotional stress, test-taking, public speaking. Also stable exertional angina, mortality reduction after myocardial infarction, and hypertension (though not first-line there).

Class III — potassium-channel blockers

They block the repolarizing potassium currents, so phase 2 and phase 3 both lengthen and the effective refractory period grows. Potassium flows out against the calcium coming in during the plateau; when the calcium channels close, potassium finishes the job. Dr. Scully said explicitly that you will not be examined on the distinction between the delayed and rapid delayed-rectifier subtypes — the effect on the action potential is the same.

⚠️ The class III risk: a long QT becomes torsades

The plateau is the distance between depolarization and repolarization, so prolonging it prolongs the QT. Torsades occurs in about 2% of patients on ibutilide and 1–3% on dofetilide. In Dr. Scully’s words, that seems like a low number — it is 2 in 100 people. That is why these drugs are not started as outpatient medications.

Contraindications: any concurrent QT-prolonging drug, congenital long QT syndrome, and any other class I or class III drug at the same time. Which class I subclass also prolongs repolarization? IA — the Goldilocks porridge that was too long.

Amiodarone — “it does all the things”

Amiodarone has class I, II, III and IV activity, given intravenously or orally. It was once used for everything, then abandoned as its adverse effects emerged, and it is now back on “every algorithm and every protocol” because the monitoring is understood. Indications: unstable ventricular tachycardia, recurrent ventricular fibrillation, supraventricular arrhythmias, and off-label prevention of recurrent paroxysmal atrial fibrillation and flutter. Contraindications: second- and third-degree heart block, symptomatic bradycardia, cardiogenic shock, and polymorphic ventricular tachycardia with a prolonged QT, which it may worsen.

Class IV — verapamil and diltiazem

🩸

Dihydropyridines — the “-dipines”

  • Arterial vasodilators, lowering systemic vascular resistance.
  • They act better at the peripheral vasculature than at the heart, which is why they are not antiarrhythmics.
🫀

Non-dihydropyridines

  • Negative inotropes and chronotropes: less contractility, slower rate, slower conduction.
  • Verapamil is the best at nodal tissue; diltiazem does both jobs, each less effectively.
  • They act preferentially on pacemaker tissue, which depends on calcium for its upstroke. Blocking L-type channels slows the upstroke in the atrioventricular node and therefore slows conduction, keeping the node refractory so ectopic signals cannot get through.
  • Indications: re-entrant paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia where the atrioventricular node is part of the circuit; atrial fibrillation with rapid ventricular response, for rate control; hypertension; and ischemic heart disease including vasospastic angina.
  • Caution in conduction-system disease — both drugs can worsen existing sinoatrial and atrioventricular node abnormalities.
  • Adverse effects: bradycardia, atrioventricular block, heart failure; hypotension; edema with diltiazem; constipation with verapamil.
⚠️ Intravenous verapamil plus a beta-blocker is contraindicated

The combination can cause irreversible electromechanical dissociation — drug-induced heart failure in which there is electrical activity with no effective contraction. Separately, the non-dihydropyridines raise plasma digoxin by competing for its renal excretion.

Outside the classes

Adenosine — “have you tried turning it off and on again?”

  • Mechanism: adenosine binds the P1 purinergic receptor, a G-protein coupled receptor, working through Gi/o; the βγ subunit opens potassium channels, which hyperpolarizes sinoatrial, atrial and atrioventricular nodal cells. The result is a big pause — brief asystole or severe bradycardia — after which the underlying rhythm takes back over.
  • Indication: conversion of narrow-complex paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia, very commonly pre-hospital and in the emergency department, and highly effective.
  • Try no drug first: vagal manoeuvres — Valsalva (asking the patient to blow the plunger out of a syringe is an easier instruction than “bear down”), cold water, carotid sinus massage, tilting.
  • Give it like a hand grenade: 6 mg all at once with an immediate saline flush. It is fast on and fast off, so it has to reach the heart at high concentration.
  • What the patient feels: a sense of impending doom, like being kicked in the chest, with flushing, headache and chest pain. Warn them.
  • Bronchoconstriction can last up to 30 minutes in asthma — that was the clue in Dr. Scully’s hockey-player case, where the resistance on the bag-valve mask rose after the drug was given.
  • Board add-on Theophylline and caffeine block adenosine receptors, so larger doses are needed. Avoid it in pre-excitation and in asthma. It is also used diagnostically, to unmask flutter waves (Section 8).
  • It fixes re-entry, not sinus tachycardia. As the lecturer put it, if the patient is tachycardic because she needs 15 litres of fluid, adenosine will not help.

Digoxin

🔑 The mechanism, in one chain

Digoxin blocks the sodium–potassium ATPase, so intracellular sodium rises. The sodium–calcium exchanger can then no longer extrude calcium, and may even run backwards. Sarcoplasmic reticulum calcium stores grow, calcium-induced calcium release is bigger, and contractility rises. That is the inotropic effect, and it is why digoxin appears in heart failure (Section 18).

Separately, it has a direct antiarrhythmic effect on the conduction system — less atrioventricular nodal automaticity, a longer nodal refractory period and slower conduction — plus autonomic effects: reduced sympathetic outflow, sensitised baroreceptors and increased vagal tone. Indications: heart failure, and rate control in atrial fibrillation and flutter.

⚠️ Potassium is the key to everything about digoxin
  • Hypokalemia increases the digoxin effect and causes toxicity. Low extracellular potassium phosphorylates the pump, which raises digoxin’s binding affinity.
  • Hyperkalemia reduces its clinical effect, because potassium competes with digoxin at the pump.
  • Signs of toxicity? Check the serum potassium first. In Dr. Scully’s words, we do not have to give an antidote if all we have to do is fix their potassium.
  • It has a narrow therapeutic index, so dose it with kidney function and potassium in mind.

Atropine

  • A non-selective muscarinic antagonist that crosses the blood–brain barrier, so its effects go well beyond the heart.
  • Cardiac indications: symptomatic bradycardia and atrioventricular block. It blocks vagal M2 input, so the rate rises. The relevant rhythms are sinus bradycardia, first-degree block, second-degree Mobitz I and II, and complete heart block.
  • First-line for symptomatic bradycardia is atropine; second-line is the catecholamines, the sympathetic amines. Dr. Scully said he would not ask “here is an indication, what is the drug?” except for bradycardia — so this pairing is worth knowing cold.
  • A paradox worth knowing: at low doses atropine can briefly slow the heart before the expected tachycardia. The slide explains this as M1 blockade showing itself before M2 blockade.
🩺 The anticholinergic toxidrome, and the right antidote

Mad as a hatter (confusion and delirium) · dry as a bone (dry mucosa, no sweating) · blind as a bat (blurred vision, cycloplegia) · red as a beet (flushed skin) · hot as a hare (hyperthermia).

  • Physostigmine is the answer: an acetylcholinesterase inhibitor that lets acetylcholine build up and compete atropine off the receptors, and it crosses the blood–brain barrier, so it reverses the central signs as well.
  • Pyridostigmine is the wrong answer here — it does not cross into the central nervous system, so it misses the delirium.
  • Pralidoxime is for organophosphate poisoning, which is cholinergic excess, the opposite problem.

🎯 What Dr. Scully said he would and would not ask

He will not ask “here is an indication, name the drug” — except for bradycardia, where the answer is atropine first and then the catecholamines. His slide notes also make the broader point that treating an arrhythmia goes beyond antiarrhythmic drugs: atrial fibrillation also needs anticoagulation and cardiovascular risk reduction (Section 9).

Q: Which ion carries phase 0 in a sinoatrial nodal cell, and which carries it in a ventricular myocyte? Why does that distinction organise this whole lecture?

Answer

Calcium through L-type channels in the node; sodium through fast channels in the myocyte. Potassium repolarizes both. That is precisely why calcium channel blockers act on the node (rate control) and sodium-channel blockers act on muscle (rhythm control).

Q: A patient on quinidine reports ringing in the ears, blurred vision and confusion. Name the syndrome. Which class IA drug would have avoided the separate problem of raising digoxin levels?

Answer

Cinchonism — reduced hearing, tinnitus, blurred vision and delirium. Procainamide does not alter digoxin levels, though it brings its own problem of drug-induced lupus with chronic use.

Q: Why does lidocaine suppress ventricular ectopy arising from ischemic tissue while barely affecting healthy myocardium?

Answer

It binds sodium channels in their open and inactivated states and dissociates rapidly. Ischemic, depolarized tissue fires often and holds its channels in those bindable states, so the block accumulates there. In normal tissue the drug washes off between beats.

Q: A patient with a prior myocardial infarction and a reduced ejection fraction develops recurrent atrial fibrillation. Is flecainide a reasonable choice?

Answer

No. Class IC drugs are proarrhythmic in ischemic and structural heart disease. Flecainide is for preventing atrial fibrillation in a structurally normal heart. The hook is “IB is Best after a myocardial infarction, IC is Contraindicated.”

Q: A patient on flecainide for atrial flutter arrives with a regular wide-complex tachycardia at 250. What has happened, and how could it have been prevented?

Answer

1:1 atrial flutter with use-dependent QRS widening. Flecainide slowed the flutter circuit enough for the atrioventricular node to conduct every beat, and the faster rate produced more sodium-channel block, widening the QRS further. Prevent it by always giving an atrioventricular nodal blocker alongside a class IC drug.

Q: Which beta-blocker would you choose for a patient who already has a prolonged QT, and why? And which for hypertension in pregnancy?

Answer

Propranolol for the long QT, because it does not prolong repolarization. Labetalol in pregnancy, because its α1 blockade prevents the baroreflex vasoconstriction that would otherwise reduce placental perfusion.

Q: What distinguishes dofetilide from ibutilide, and why can dofetilide be used in a patient with a reduced ejection fraction?

Answer

Dofetilide also maintains sinus rhythm after conversion, whereas ibutilide only terminates the arrhythmia. It is usable with a low ejection fraction because it has no negative inotropic effect. Both are started in hospital because of a 1 to 3% risk of torsades.

Q: A patient on long-term amiodarone develops a dry cough, dyspnea and bilateral reticular opacities. Name the toxicity and the monitoring that should have been in place.

Answer

Amiodarone pulmonary fibrosis, the toxicity that kills. Baseline and annual pulmonary function tests, alongside thyroid function, liver function and regular eye examinations.

Q: A patient on digoxin is started on furosemide, and a week later reports yellow halos and nausea. What is the first laboratory value to check, and why?

Answer

Serum potassium. The loop diuretic has caused hypokalemia, and potassium competes with digoxin at the sodium–potassium ATPase, so low potassium increases digoxin binding and precipitates toxicity. Fixing the potassium may be all that is needed.

Q: A patient on metoprolol is given intravenous verapamil for supraventricular tachycardia and becomes profoundly hypotensive with no effective contraction. Explain.

Answer

Intravenous verapamil combined with a beta-blocker can cause irreversible electromechanical dissociation. The combination is contraindicated.

Q: A 24-year-old hockey player collapses with a rapid regular narrow-complex rhythm at 192 with no P waves. He is given a drug and the bag-valve mask becomes noticeably harder to squeeze. What was the rhythm, what was the drug, and what confirms it?

Answer

Narrow, regular, no P wave, very fast — paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia from re-entry. The drug was adenosine, acting at the P1 receptor through Gi/o to open potassium channels and hyperpolarize nodal tissue, producing transient atrioventricular block that breaks the circuit. The confirming clue is the transient bronchoconstriction — the stiff bag.

Q: After repeated doses of atropine a patient is febrile, flushed, dry, delirious and has dilated pupils. Which antidote, and why that one rather than the alternatives?

Answer

Physostigmine, because it is an acetylcholinesterase inhibitor that crosses the blood–brain barrier and so reverses the central delirium as well as the peripheral signs. Pyridostigmine does not enter the central nervous system; pralidoxime treats organophosphate poisoning, which is the opposite problem.
