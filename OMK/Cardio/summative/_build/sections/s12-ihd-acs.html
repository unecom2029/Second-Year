Stable Ischemic Heart Disease Versus Acute Coronary Syndrome

Dr. Watkins opened the chest-pain case with a warning: “If you do not consider coronary artery disease you will never diagnose it.” His second rule was “Listen to the patient and they will tell you the diagnosis.” This section follows his 72-year-old patient from eight weeks of exertional chest pressure to a non-ST-elevation infarction, and uses her to separate the stable from the acute.

Stable angina, unstable angina, NSTEMI and STEMI ★ HY

All four sit on one spectrum of oxygen supply versus demand. The difference between them is what the plaque is doing: sitting still, cracking with a partial clot, or cracking with a complete one.

  • Acute coronary syndrome means unstable angina, NSTEMI or STEMI. It is the stable-to-acute jump: acute myocardial infarction is thrombosis in a coronary artery, usually from plaque rupture.
  • As stable angina progresses toward unstable angina, duration and frequency rise and the exertional threshold falls. That trend is the warning.
  • Board add-on With high-sensitivity troponin, most patients once labelled unstable angina turn out to have a small troponin rise and are now called NSTEMI. UWorld calls unstable angina “largely obsolete” as a diagnosis, but it remains on exams as rest angina with a normal troponin.
  • MINOCA and INOCA — infarction or ischemia without obstructive coronary disease — account for about 10–15% of infarcts. The core problem is microvascular and endothelial dysfunction (diabetic small-vessel disease, genetic failure of small-vessel relaxation); associations are hypertension, aortic stenosis and anemia.
  • Chronic ischemic heart disease is progressive loss of myocytes from many small, silent, healed infarcts — common in diabetics who do not feel the pain.

Why angina appears with exertion first: coronary flow reserve

  • Resting coronary flow stays normal until a stenosis reaches about 80–90%.
  • Maximal (stress) flow begins to fall at about 50% stenosis.
  • So stable angina shows up on exertion first, and a stress test unmasks a lesion that the resting ECG misses.
  • The myocardium is perfused in diastole, so a fast heart rate shortens supply time as it raises demand (Section 1).

Chest pain: the approach from history and exam ★ HY

Always ask about quality, location, onset, duration, what relieves it and associated symptoms, and add Dr. Watkins’s question: “Can you make the chest pain occur?” Angina is predictable and inducible; random pain is not.

  • Sharp pain worse on inspiration and lying flat suggests pericarditis. Sudden ripping pain to the back suggests an acute aortic syndrome. Relief with nitroglycerin is not diagnostic of ischemia.
  • Women are persistently under-diagnosed — a lower index of suspicion before menopause, and a tendency to attribute pain elsewhere. Women, people with diabetes and the elderly more often describe left- or right-sided, stabbing, throat or abdominal discomfort. Diabetic neuropathy can make infarcts silent.
  • Coronary disease presents about 10 years later in women; smoking roughly 6× the infarct risk in women versus 3× in men, and diabetes is a more potent risk factor in women (Section 37).
  • Only about 30% of chest pain is cardiac (Dr. Scully), so keep the differential wide.

The exam and the risk factors

  • Examine for vascular disease elsewhere and for heart failure: full and equal pulses, bruits, heart sounds (listen for an S3), edema, and the skin for xanthomas.
  • Risk factors: smoking, family history (especially onset under 60), lipids, diabetes, hypertension; also stress, alcohol, drugs and adherence.
  • Tendon xanthoma and corneal arcus before 45 are highly specific for familial hypercholesterolemia; xanthelasma has low specificity (Section 30).

The ECG in each

The tracing is worked through in full in Section 7 (territories, reciprocal change, STEMI evolution, posterior infarction). The points this section needs:

  • Everyone with chest pain gets a 12-lead ECG. Look for ST elevation, LBBB, Q waves, ST depression and T-wave changes. Pathologic Q waves suggest a prior infarct.
  • Stable angina: usually a normal resting tracing — Dr. Watkins’s patient had a completely normal one.
  • NSTEMI: ST depression in about a third of patients; deep T-wave inversions (3 mm or more) are more common but less specific. The leads do not localise the infarct well.
  • STEMI: new ST elevation at the J point in two or more contiguous leads, more than 1 mm (higher thresholds in V2–V3). Board add-on STEMI equivalents: a new LBBB with a convincing story, ST elevation in aVR with diffuse depression (left main), and ST depression in V1–V3 (posterior).

Stress testing and catheterization

Step 1: estimate the pre-test probability

  • The CAD Consortium calculator uses age, sex and symptom type, then adds risk factors, and can add the coronary calcium score, which raises specificity (a calcium score of 0 makes obstructive disease much less likely).
  • His 72-year-old patient came out at about 40–50%: intermediate risk.
  • The Pooled Cohort (ASCVD) and PREVENT calculators are for prevention in people without symptoms; they are not the right tool once someone has chest pain.

Step 2: choose the test

  • Any suspicion of an acute coronary syndrome means the ACS pathway, not outpatient testing.
  • Able to exercise with an interpretable resting ECG: low-to-intermediate likelihood, a plain exercise ECG; intermediate-to-high, exercise with imaging (echo or perfusion) or CCTA.
  • Uninterpretable resting ECG (LBBB, paced rhythm, digoxin, LVH with strain): exercise with imaging.
  • Unable to exercise: pharmacologic stress echo or perfusion imaging, or CCTA.
  • His choice for her was a stress echocardiogram: more sensitive and specific than a plain treadmill test, especially in women.
  • Stress testing during active ischemia is contraindicated.

🔑 Optimal medical therapy saves lives

The four classes are antiplatelet + statin + ACE inhibitor or angiotensin receptor blocker + beta-blocker. In long-term follow-up, patients on none, one or two of them had about 20% ten-year mortality, against about 13% on three or four.

🎯 Troponin belongs in the emergency department

“I would never order a troponin in the outpatient setting. If you think about ordering a troponin, that patient should be in an emergency room.” — Dr. Qazi

Dr. Watkins made the same point: troponin is for a suspected acute coronary syndrome, measured serially, in the ED. And waiting for insurance pre-approval for an admission in an acute coronary syndrome would, in his word, be “malpractice.”

Q: A 72-year-old woman has 8 weeks of chest pressure walking up a steep hill with her dog, radiating to her jaw, lasting minutes and easing with rest. It is not getting worse. Her resting ECG is normal and she can walk on a treadmill. What is the working diagnosis, and what test comes next?

Answer

Stable ischemic heart disease at intermediate pre-test probability — typical features, new but not progressive, so not an acute coronary syndrome. Next is an exercise stress echocardiogram, which is more sensitive and specific than a plain treadmill test, especially in women. Treat her blood pressure and give as-needed sublingual nitroglycerin meanwhile.

Q: A week later the same patient tells you that over 4 days her pain has come more often and lasted up to 45 minutes, and last night she had 4 hours of pain at rest. Her ECG shows new inferolateral ST depression. Her husband offers to drive her to the hospital. What do you do?

Answer

Treat it as a non-ST-elevation acute coronary syndrome. Cancel the stress test (contraindicated in active ischemia), give aspirin, nitroglycerin, oxygen if she is hypoxic, and monitoring in the office, and send her by ambulance — no patient with ACS symptoms is ever transferred by car.

Q: An NSTEMI patient becomes hypotensive and confused in the emergency department, with cool extremities. How soon should catheterization happen?

Answer

Immediately — within 2 hours. Cardiogenic shock is a very-high-risk feature. An uncomplicated NSTEMI by troponin alone would be early invasive, within 24 hours.

Q: An 84-year-old woman with septic shock and a heart rate of 140 has a troponin rise and diffuse ST depression. Angiography a year ago showed mild fixed disease. What kind of infarction is this?

Answer

A secondary (type 2) infarction: supply–demand mismatch from sepsis, hypotension and tachycardia, not plaque rupture. Treat the sepsis; she is not managed as a thrombotic occlusion.

Q: At what degree of stenosis does maximal coronary flow start to fall, and at what degree does resting flow fall? Why does this explain the clinical picture of stable angina?

Answer

Maximal flow falls from about 50%; resting flow is preserved until about 80–90%. So a moderate stenosis causes symptoms only when demand rises, on exertion, and the resting ECG is normal — which is why you need a stress test to find it.

Q: A patient’s chest pain is sharp, worse when lying flat and on deep breaths, and better sitting forward. A second patient has sudden ripping pain through to the back and a 30 mm Hg difference in arm pressures. What does each suggest?

Answer

The first is acute pericarditis — non-ischemic, positional, pleuritic pain. The second is an acute aortic dissection, which must be excluded before any anticoagulant or thrombolytic is given.
