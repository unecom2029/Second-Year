Section 6 · The ECG & Arrhythmias

Reading the ECG: Rate, Rhythm, Waves, Intervals and Axis

Dr. Qazi reads an electrocardiogram in about half a second, but only because she has read hundreds of thousands of them with the same system. That is the real lesson of this lecture: you get there by looking at every tracing the same way every single time, so that nothing is missed. Your first solo reading will take a long time, and that is expected — even experienced emergency physicians call cardiology to ask about a tracing.

The reading order, and why you never deviate from it ★ HY

Every tracing gets the same seven steps in the same order. The payoff is that an abnormality you were not looking for still gets found, because you walked past it on the way to something else.

  1. Rate. Count the QRS complexes on the rhythm strip and multiply by six. Tachycardia is 100 or more, bradycardia is under 60.
  2. Rhythm. First ask whether it is regular. If it is regular, is it sinus, using the five criteria below? If it is irregular, which of the five irregular rhythms is it?
  3. Axis. Look at the QRS in lead I and in aVF, with lead II as a back-up. Decide left, right or normal, and then ask why.
  4. Intervals. The PR interval first (is there AV block?), then the QRS duration (is there a bundle branch block?), then the QT and corrected QT (is there torsades risk?).
  5. Chamber enlargement. The P waves in leads II and V1 tell you about the atria. Voltage plus axis tells you about the left ventricle. R-wave progression plus axis tells you about the right ventricle.
  6. Ischemia, injury and infarction. ST elevation means transmural injury, ST depression means subendocardial injury, and Q waves mean dead myocardium. Then ask which territory.
  7. Say it in one sentence. For example: “Sinus bradycardia, rate 42, normal axis, 2:1 atrioventricular block.”
🎙️ From the recording — and from the electrophysiologist too

“Look at it the same way every single time.”

The Maine Medical Center electrophysiologist who gave the second electrocardiogram lecture adds two rules to the same list:

  • An electrocardiogram is always read together with the clinical history — his “Love Island” rule is that alone, it gets voted off the island.
  • Each tracing is worth ten points, and about eight are for the clinical big picture (sinus rhythm? heart block? bundle branch block? ventricular tachycardia? normal?). Only about two are for fine detail such as left atrial enlargement.
  • The American Board of Internal Medicine agrees: report the findings that are definite and that matter for management, because reading a tracing is “not an exercise in identifying minutiae.”

The paper, the waves and the leads

The tracing is a drawing of the heart’s electricity, and each wave is one step of conduction: the SA node fires, the atria depolarize (the P wave), the impulse pauses in the AV node (the PR segment), it runs through the bundle of His and the bundle branches into the Purkinje fibres, the ventricles depolarize (the QRS), and the ventricles repolarize (the T wave). The SA node sets the pace for one reason only — it is the fastest pacemaker in the heart.

🔑 Naming the QRS, because every later criterion uses it

Q is the first negative deflection. R is the first positive deflection. S is the negative deflection that comes after an R. A second R is written R′ and said “R-prime.” Capital letters mean big waves and lower-case letters mean small ones, so “rSR′” describes a small initial positive, a deep negative, and then a second positive. You will not always see a Q or an S, and that is normal.

The leads. Ten electrodes give twelve views. Six are limb leads (I, II, III, aVR, aVL and aVF) looking at the frontal plane, and six are chest leads (V1 through V6) looking at the horizontal plane. The long strip across the bottom of the printout is the full ten-second rhythm strip, and it is almost always lead II.

Step 1 — Rate

🔑 Dr. Qazi’s method, which works whether or not the rhythm is regular

Count every QRS on the rhythm strip and multiply by six, because a standard twelve-lead tracing is ten seconds long. If you are holding a six-second monitor strip instead, multiply by ten. That is the whole method.

  • Tachycardia is 100 or more, normal is 60 to 99, and bradycardia is under 60. A sinus rhythm running at 110 must be called sinus tachycardia, not simply sinus rhythm.
  • The machine’s printed rate is correct roughly 90% of the time. Artifact and patient movement fool it, so check it yourself.
  • Board add-on For a regular rhythm you can also count big boxes between two R waves and run down the sequence 300 · 150 · 100 · 75 · 60 · 50, or divide 1500 by the number of small boxes. This shortcut only works when the rhythm is regular.

Step 2 — Rhythm: regular or irregular? ★ HY

Ask the regularity question first, because it splits the whole problem in half.

🧠 If it is regular, is it sinus? All five must be true
  1. The R–R intervals are regular. March the QRS complexes out with calipers.
  2. The P–P intervals are regular. This one matters enormously once you get to heart block.
  3. The PR interval is constant from beat to beat.
  4. There is a P wave for every QRS, which the first three criteria have effectively proven.
  5. The P wave is upright in lead II, which tells you the impulse started in the SA node. In V1 a normal P wave is biphasic, like one cycle of a sine wave.

If it is irregular, there are five things it can be

RhythmWhat you seePicture
Atrial fibrillation No P waves at all, an irregularly irregular R–R with no pattern to it, and an undulating baseline. This is the arrhythmia you will meet most often — roughly 90% of the rhythm problems in adult cardiology. Covered in Section 9.
Sinus arrhythmia Normal sinus P waves, with the rate speeding and slowing with respiration. Common in young people and entirely normal.
Second-degree atrioventricular block Dropped beats — a P wave with no QRS after it — make the rhythm look irregular, though there may be a repeating pattern. Covered in Section 8.
Wandering atrial pacemaker Several atrial foci take turns, so you see three or more different P-wave shapes, at a rate under 100.
Multifocal atrial tachycardia Exactly the same picture, but at a rate of 100 or more. Board add-on Classically a patient with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease.
🪝 Wandering atrial pacemaker and multifocal atrial tachycardia are one diagnosis with a speed limit

Both need three or more P-wave morphologies with an irregular rhythm. The only difference is the rate: under 100 makes it a wandering atrial pacemaker, 100 or over makes it multifocal atrial tachycardia. If an examiner gives you one and then changes the rate, they are asking for the other name.

Atrial flutter, which Dr. Qazi called “highly tested”

  • Look for flutter waves in a sawtooth pattern, seen best in the inferior leads II, III and aVF.
  • Conduction may be at a fixed ratio of flutter waves to QRS complexes, such as 4:1, which makes the rhythm regular, or at a variable ratio, which makes it irregular. So flutter can land in either half of your regular-versus-irregular split.
  • The circuits differ, and this is worth holding on to: typical flutter circles in the right atrium around the cavotricuspid isthmus, whereas atrial fibrillation starts around the pulmonary veins in the left atrium. Section 8 uses this to explain why each one is ablated differently.

Step 3 — Axis, in two seconds, and then “why?” ★ HY

Dr. Qazi’s practical view is that the axis matters chiefly as a criterion for other diagnoses rather than as a finding in its own right. So skip the quadrant arithmetic and simply look at whether the QRS is mostly up or mostly down in lead I and in aVF.

  • If aVF sits on the isoelectric line and you cannot call it, use lead II instead, with the same pattern: positive for normal, negative for left, positive for right. Dr. Qazi says that gets you to the right answer about 95% of the time.
  • Board add-on In degrees, a normal axis runs from about −30° to +90°.

Having named the axis, the useful work is asking what caused it. There is a short list on each side.

⬅️

Left axis — four causes

  • Left ventricular hypertrophy
  • Left bundle branch block
  • Left anterior fascicular block
  • An old inferior myocardial infarction, because the Q waves in II, III and aVF make aVF negative
➡️

Right axis — three causes

  • Right ventricular hypertrophy
  • Left posterior fascicular block
  • An old lateral myocardial infarction, with Q waves in I and aVL
  • Lung disease also shifts the axis rightward, but it does not change how you read the tracing
⚠️ The classic wrong answer on axis

Right bundle branch block has a normal axis. So if you see a right bundle branch block and right axis deviation, the right answer is not “right bundle branch block” — it is right bundle branch block plus left posterior fascicular block. The same logic runs the other way for the fascicular blocks generally, which are covered in Section 7: a fascicular block is a left or right axis with no other explanation, in a tracing whose QRS is still narrow.

Putting the first three steps together

Once you can call rate, rhythm and axis, you can already produce a usable one-line read on most tracings, and you have narrowed what the intervals and the ST segments could possibly mean. Sections 7 and 8 take over from here: Section 7 reads the tracing for structural and metabolic disease, and Section 8 reads it for arrhythmia and block.

🩺 A reminder from the second lecture: read the patient too

Before you commit to a reading, add the questions the electrophysiologist asks at the bedside:

  • Was the patient symptomatic at the time of the tracing? That question alone separates pericarditis from a heart attack.
  • Is the patient stable? That decides treatment before the diagnosis is final.
  • Is there an old tracing to compare with? Knowing that a wide QRS was already there last year changes everything.
  • If the tracing is full of noise, check the leads and repeat it — garbage in, garbage out — because the machine will still print a confident diagnosis on top of artifact.

Q: A standard twelve-lead tracing shows seven QRS complexes on the rhythm strip. What is the rate, and what do you call it?

Answer

Seven times six is 42 beats per minute, so this is bradycardia. The multiplication works because a standard twelve-lead tracing runs for ten seconds.

Q: List the five criteria that must all be met before you can call a rhythm sinus.

Answer

Regular R–R intervals, regular P–P intervals, a constant PR interval, a P wave for every QRS, and an upright P wave in lead II. The last one is the criterion that actually proves the impulse came from the sinoatrial node.

Q: An irregular narrow-complex rhythm at 118 beats per minute has three different P-wave shapes. Name it. What would you call the same tracing at 82 beats per minute?

Answer

Multifocal atrial tachycardia at 118. At 82 the identical picture is called a wandering atrial pacemaker. The rate of 100 is the only dividing line.

Q: The QRS is positive in lead I and negative in aVF. The QRS is narrow, there is no voltage evidence of left ventricular hypertrophy, and there are no Q waves in II, III or aVF. What is the diagnosis?

Answer

Left anterior fascicular block. This is a left axis with every other cause excluded: the narrow QRS rules out a left bundle branch block, the voltages rule out hypertrophy, and the absence of inferior Q waves rules out an old inferior infarction.

Q: A patient’s tracing shows a wide QRS with an rSR′ pattern in V1 and V2, and the axis is clearly rightward. Why is “right bundle branch block” an incomplete answer?

Answer

Because an isolated right bundle branch block leaves the axis normal. The right axis needs its own explanation, and the usual one here is an added left posterior fascicular block.

Q: A monitor strip six seconds long shows nine QRS complexes. What is the rate?

Answer

90 beats per minute. A six-second strip is multiplied by ten rather than six. Getting the strip length right is the only way the method can go wrong.
