Section 18 · Heart Failure & the Myocardium

Heart Failure Pharmacology

Dr. Scully’s frame for the whole lecture: the damage in heart failure with reduced ejection fraction is irreversible, so every drug either helps the patient live longer or helps them feel better. Know which is which. The four drugs that lower mortality, started together and fast, cut cardiovascular death or hospitalisation by roughly two-thirds; Dr. Segal’s summary was that patients on all four live on average about five years longer.

How each HFrEF drug works

ARNI — sacubitril/valsartan (first-line renin–angiotensin drug)

  • Sacubitril inhibits neprilysin, a neutral endopeptidase, so natriuretic peptides, bradykinin and substance P accumulate: natriuresis, diuresis and vasodilation. Valsartan blocks AT1.
  • Preferred order: ARNI over ACEi, ACEi over ARB.
  • Adverse effects: hypotension (SBP falls 5–7), orthostasis, hyperkalemia. Boxed warning: fetal toxicity — anything targeting the renin–angiotensin system is contraindicated in pregnancy.
  • It raises measured BNP, so follow NT-proBNP (Section 13).
  • Board add-on Stop an ACE inhibitor 36 hours before starting an ARNI, and never combine them: two sources of bradykinin cause angioedema.

ACE inhibitor (enalapril) or ARB

  • Less angiotensin II, so less vasoconstriction (afterload) and less aldosterone (a small diuretic effect — a little preload too).
  • Adverse effects: hyperkalemia (less aldosterone means less ENaC-driven potassium secretion), cough and angioedema with ACE inhibitors (bradykinin), hypotension, teratogenic. Never two renin–angiotensin drugs together.

Beta-blockers

  • Lower heart rate and contractility, so less oxygen demand; blunt the chronic sympathetic drive that remodels the heart.
  • Only the evidence-based three: metoprolol succinate (the once-daily form, not tartrate), carvedilol, bisoprolol.
  • Carvedilol also blocks α1, lowering afterload — more output from the contractility the patient has.
  • Start only when the patient is compensated, near euvolemic; never newly in acute decompensation. A warm patient already on one usually continues it.

MRA — spironolactone, eplerenone

  • Block the aldosterone receptor: less ENaC, less sodium reabsorption and potassium wasting; anti-fibrotic.
  • Spironolactone is not selective: it blocks androgen receptors too, giving gynecomastia, impotence and abnormal menses. Eplerenone is the selective alternative (twice daily, costlier). Finerenone is a non-steroidal MRA entering HFpEF practice.
  • Hyperkalemia is the danger with every MRA, especially with an ARNI or ACE inhibitor. Board add-on Avoid if potassium is over 5.0 or eGFR under 30.

SGLT2 inhibitors — dapagliflozin, empagliflozin

  • Block the sodium–glucose cotransporter-2 in the proximal tubule. The heart-failure benefit has little to do with glucose (they lower A1c by only about 0.5%): they are natriuretic and diuretic.
  • More sodium and chloride at the macula densa triggers tubuloglomerular feedback, constricting the afferent arteriole and lowering intraglomerular pressure — renal and cardiovascular protection. Dr. Scully adds anti-inflammatory and anti-remodelling effects “we don’t truly understand.”
  • The one class for both HFrEF and HFpEF, with or without diabetes. For decades the only class I drugs for HFpEF were loop diuretics.
  • Adverse effects: genital and urinary infections, volume depletion, back pain, renal impairment, and gangrene of the perineum — counsel every patient. Board add-on That is Fournier gangrene; also euglycemic DKA.
  • Re-check diuretic doses when starting one, to avoid hypovolemia.

Diuretics: why the loop stops working

  • The braking phenomenon (tolerance). The distal convoluted tubule hypertrophies and grabs more of the sodium that escapes the loop. Furosemide (“Lasix lasts six” hours) causes natriuresis for about 6 hours, then the kidney reabsorbs avidly for the next 18. That is why loops are not used alone for chronic hypertension.
  • The fix is sequential nephron blockade: add a thiazide.
  • Why both waste potassium: more sodium reaches the principal cell, ENaC takes it up alone, the lumen goes negative, and potassium (through ROMK) and hydrogen ion are secreted — a hypokalemic metabolic alkalosis.
  • Why loops lose calcium and magnesium: in the thick ascending limb, potassium recycling through ROMK makes a lumen-positive potential that drives calcium and magnesium between cells. Block NKCC2 and the potential disappears.
⚠️ A recording slip

The recording places the potassium-for-hydrogen swap in “beta-intercalated cells.” It is the α-intercalated cells that secrete hydrogen ion; β-intercalated cells secrete bicarbonate.

How an inotrope adds force: β1 stimulation activates Gs and adenylyl cyclase, raising cAMP. Protein kinase A then phosphorylates the L-type calcium channel and phospholamban (freeing SERCA), so more calcium is available each beat. Give an inotrope first to a hypotensive, adequately filled patient; add a vasopressor only for persistent shock, accepting that it buys perfusion “at the cost of” afterload.

🎯 The exam skill

“You’re going to have to pick PV loops. What happens a beat later if you change this? What shift happens if you give this drug?” — Dr. Scully

Know what each drug does to preload, afterload or contractility, then pick the matching shift.

The stepwise chronic regimen

First: the decompensated patient

  1. Airway, oxygen, monitoring: pulse oximetry, supplemental oxygen or ventilatory support, cardiac monitor, IV access, sit them up.
  2. Decongest — reduce preload first: IV loop diuretic (± a thiazide), strict intake and output, daily weights, sodium restriction, watch electrolytes and kidneys.
  3. Early vasodilator therapy for afterload once breathing improves.
  4. Hypotensive? Check volume, then an inotrope (dobutamine, milrinone) if SBP is under about 85 with shock signs and adequate filling; a vasopressor only as a temporising measure.

Then: getting onto all four pillars

  1. Diurese to near euvolemia.
  2. Afterload reduction, typically starting an ARNI (alternative: hydralazine plus a long-acting nitrate).
  3. Beta-blocker once compensated.
  4. SGLT2 inhibitor and MRA at low doses — start all four before up-titrating any single drug.
  5. Start in hospital (Dr. Qazi: follow-up is too unreliable to wait), teach the patient and their main support person with a nurse educator, follow up within 7–10 days, then titrate over 4–6 weeks checking kidney function, potassium and NT-proBNP.
  6. Secondary: digoxin, hydralazine plus nitrate, ivabradine, vericiguat. Diuretics as needed for symptoms throughout.

And for HFpEF

  • Everyone: a diuretic for congestion and an SGLT2 inhibitor unless contraindicated (pregnancy, type 1 diabetes, volume depletion, recurrent genitourinary infection) — “the first drug with real HFpEF benefit.”
  • Some: a GLP-1 agonist if the BMI is 30 or more; an MRA if potassium and kidney function allow.
  • Not first-line in HFpEF: ACE inhibitors, ARBs and ARNI (reserved for selected patients with an EF of 50–60%).
  • Lifestyle: DASH or Mediterranean diet, no fluid restriction unless hyponatremic, cardiac rehabilitation (30 minutes of moderate walking cuts mortality about 16%), weight loss, treat sleep apnea.
⚖️ Race and the renin–angiotensin drugs

Heart failure was one of the first conditions in which avoiding renin–angiotensin drugs in Black patients was debunked. The old recommendation has been disproven repeatedly and removed from risk calculators, and race-agnostic prescribing closes outcome disparities. Hydralazine plus nitrate (A-HeFT) is an add-on, not a replacement.

🔑 Potassium in a combined regimen

Enalapril (or an ARNI) and spironolactone both raise potassium; furosemide lowers it. The net is unpredictable, so monitor potassium and renal function, and remember all of them lower blood pressure.

Q: Which HFrEF drug classes reduce mortality, and which relieve symptoms only?

Answer

Mortality: ARNI/ACEi/ARB, beta-blockers (carvedilol, metoprolol succinate, bisoprolol), MRAs and SGLT2 inhibitors. Symptoms only: diuretics, digoxin, veno- and vasodilators, aquaretics, dobutamine and milrinone.

Q: Sacubitril inhibits which enzyme, which three substances accumulate, and what must be done before starting it in a patient on enalapril?

Answer

Neprilysin, so natriuretic peptides, bradykinin and substance P accumulate. Stop the enalapril at least 36 hours beforehand, because ACE inhibitor plus neprilysin inhibitor stacks bradykinin and causes angioedema.

Q: A man on spironolactone for HFrEF develops tender breast enlargement. Why, and what is the alternative?

Answer

Spironolactone is non-selective and blocks androgen receptors. Switch to eplerenone, the selective MRA.

Q: A congested patient needs a loop diuretic but reports an anaphylactic sulfonamide allergy. What is the board answer, and what adverse effect does it share with furosemide?

Answer

Ethacrynic acid, the non-sulfonamide loop diuretic. Like furosemide it causes dose-dependent ototoxicity.

Q: Furosemide relieves a congested patient’s dyspnea, but the fatigue and cool extremities remain. Explain using the Frank–Starling curve, and say what would help.

Answer

Preload reduction slides the patient down the same curve: the end-diastolic pressure falls, but cardiac output does not rise. Moving onto a higher curve needs afterload reduction (ACE inhibitor, ARNI, hydralazine) or an inotrope.

Q: A patient on a prolonged infusion of a drug that raises cGMP develops a rising lactate and an anion-gap metabolic acidosis, then dies of an arrhythmia. Which drug, and why does it dilate arterioles when nitroglycerin mainly dilates veins?

Answer

Sodium nitroprusside, with cyanide toxicity — cyanide sits at the core of its structure. It releases nitric oxide spontaneously, so it acts on arterioles and veins alike; nitroglycerin needs enzymatic bioactivation, which happens mainly on the venous side.

Q: A diabetic HFrEF patient on empagliflozin reports perineal pain, redness and swelling. What is the concern? And by which renal mechanism does this drug lower intraglomerular pressure?

Answer

Fournier gangrene (necrotising infection of the perineum) — see the patient immediately. The pressure effect is tubuloglomerular feedback: more sodium and chloride at the macula densa constricts the afferent arteriole.

Q: Dobutamine binds α receptors, yet acts clinically as a pure β1 agonist. Why? And why does norepinephrine raise contractility yet often slow the heart?

Answer

Dobutamine is a racemic mixture: one enantiomer is an α agonist, the other an α antagonist, so the α effects cancel. Norepinephrine’s α1 vasoconstriction (with no β2 dilation) raises blood pressure, and the baroreflex increases vagal tone: reflex bradycardia.
