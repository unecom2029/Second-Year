Parts A–H — the normal heart, the ECG and arrhythmias, atherosclerosis and ischemia, heart failure and the myocardium, infection and inflammation, valves and surgery, congenital disease, and the vessels. One card per thing you must not miss on the day.

01

Pressure opens every valve

Blood flows from high to low pressure. A valve opens when the chamber before it is higher and closes when the chamber beyond overtakes. Every event on the Wiggers diagram follows.

02

a, c, v

a is atrial contraction, c is the atrioventricular valve bulging back at the onset of systole, v is atrial filling. Atrial fibrillation removes the a wave; mitral or tricuspid regurgitation gives a tall v wave.

03

Boards draw loops, not Wiggers

Loop width is stroke volume, the ESPVR slope is contractility, the EDPVR is stiffness. More afterload: taller and narrower. More contractility: steeper ESPVR, smaller ESV.

04

Calcium binds troponin C

Never tropomyosin — half of last year’s class missed it. Cardiac muscle uses calcium-induced calcium release; myosin light-chain kinase belongs to smooth muscle.

05

S3 is full, S4 is stiff

S3 just after S2: rapid filling of a volume-overloaded ventricle. S4 just before S1: the atrial kick into a stiff ventricle — and it vanishes in atrial fibrillation.

06

Fixed split is an ASD

Wide split: pulmonic stenosis, right bundle branch block. Fixed: atrial septal defect. Paradoxical: severe aortic stenosis or left bundle branch block.

07

Systolic or diastolic, pressure or volume

Systolic murmur: aortic stenosis or mitral regurgitation. Diastolic: mitral stenosis or aortic regurgitation. A stenosis makes a gradient; a leak makes extra volume.

08

Left atrial pressure is always high in aortic stenosis

Not from the mitral valve — from a thick, stiff ventricle that will not accept a normal volume. Exercise intolerance is the first symptom.

09

Tall v wave means mitral regurgitation

The atrium fills from the pulmonary veins and the regurgitant jet at once. Prolapse adds a midsystolic click and a late systolic murmur.

10

Wide pulse pressure means aortic regurgitation

The aorta loses blood two ways in diastole. Bounding “water-hammer” pulses, a diastolic decrescendo murmur, an eccentric dilated ventricle.

11

Shrink the ventricle, louden HCM and MVP

Standing and Valsalva make hypertrophic cardiomyopathy and prolapse louder and most others softer. Handgrip louder: MR, AR, VSD; softer: AS, HCM.

12

Systolic up, diastolic flat

With exercise, systolic pressure rises about 8–12 mm Hg per MET while diastolic stays flat or falls. Stroke volume plateaus near half of VO₂max.

13

One MET is worth a drug

Each 1-MET gain lowers mortality 8–35% in coronary disease. Phase II rehab: 36 monitored sessions over 8–12 weeks; chronic heart failure is the class IIa indication.

14

Only the Mediterranean diet has trial proof

PREDIMED cut heart attack, stroke and cardiovascular death by about 30%. DASH and low sodium add; trans fats carry a class III (harm) recommendation.

15

Myocyte sodium, node calcium, both potassium

The upstroke is sodium in myocytes and calcium in the SA and AV nodes; repolarization is potassium in both. The funny current’s slope sets the heart rate.

16

Radius to the fourth power

Halve a vessel’s radius and flow falls to one-sixteenth. The capillary network, with the largest total area, has the slowest flow.

17

In heart failure the pressure systems win

The baroreflex, RAAS and ADH override the volume reflex, so salt and water are retained — and fluid can make a failing patient worse. Low serum sodium here is dilutional.

18

Read it the same way every time

Rate, rhythm, axis, intervals, chamber enlargement, ischemia, then one sentence. About eight of ten points are for the clinical big picture, not the minutiae — and the tracing is always read with the history.

19

Count the QRS complexes and multiply by six

A twelve-lead tracing is 10 seconds; a monitor strip is usually 6, so multiply by ten instead. Works whether or not the rhythm is regular.

20

An upright P in lead II is what makes it sinus

All five criteria must hold, but this is the one that proves the impulse came from the sinoatrial node. In V1 the normal P is biphasic.

21

Right bundle branch block has a normal axis

So a right bundle branch block with right axis deviation is right bundle branch block plus left posterior fascicular block. A fascicular block is an unexplained axis with a normal QRS width; a bundle block widens the QRS.

22

WiLLiaM and MaRRoW

Left bundle branch block makes a W in V1 and an M in V6. Right makes an M in V1 and a W in V6. Check the shape in those two leads before anything else.

23

aVL is specific, the precordial sum is sensitive

Left ventricular hypertrophy needs a left axis plus either R in aVL above 11 mm (most specific) or S in V1–V2 plus R in V5–V6 of 35 mm or more (most sensitive). The strain pattern it produces mimics ischemia.

24

Elevation is transmural, depression is subendocardial

ST elevation in two contiguous leads means total occlusion — open the artery now. ST depression means a 70–90% lesion with some flow — stabilise first. Q waves mean the muscle is already dead.

25

Reciprocal change confirms the STEMI

Elevation in one territory with depression in the opposite leads is an electrical mirror image. The same logic reads a posterior infarct from the front: ST depression with a tall R in V1 to V3.

26

Peaked T waves up, U waves down

High potassium gives tall narrow peaked T waves and then widens every interval. Low potassium flattens the T and brings out a U wave, which makes the QT look long and carries torsades risk.

27

In every AV block the P waves march out

P–P is constant in all four blocks — the sinus node is fine, the failure is downstream. If the P waves do not march, it is not heart block.

28

Mobitz I is benign, Mobitz II needs a pacemaker

The easiest sign of Mobitz I is that the PR after the dropped beat is shorter than the PR before it. In 2:1 block you cannot subtype it at all — let the history decide.

29

Regular, narrow, no P wave means AVNRT

The circuit goes down the slow pathway and up the fast one, so the atria and ventricles depolarize together and the P hides inside the QRS.

30

Adenosine breaks AVNRT and unmasks flutter

It stops rhythms that use the atrioventricular node and merely exposes those that do not. So the response is itself diagnostic. Avoid it in a heart transplant and in Wolff–Parkinson–White.

31

Negative sawtooth inferiorly, locked at 150

Typical flutter circles the tricuspid annulus through the cavotricuspid isthmus, so the inferior leads see it going away. Atria at 250–300 with 2:1 conduction parks the ventricle at about 150.

32

Wide and you cannot tell: treat it as VT

Nobody is marked wrong for treating a supraventricular tachycardia with aberrancy as ventricular tachycardia. Look for capture or fusion beats and AV dissociation first; negative precordial concordance is virtually diagnostic.

33

In Wolff–Parkinson–White the usual algorithm reverses

No adenosine, no calcium channel blockers, no beta-blockers, no vagal manoeuvres — they push conduction down the accessory pathway. Give procainamide, or sedate and cardiovert.

34

Flutter is anticoagulated exactly like fibrillation

Same CHA₂DS₂-VASc score, same threshold. A score of 2 or more usually means a blood thinner, and the two dangers being prevented are stroke and heart failure.

35

Node is calcium, muscle is sodium, both are potassium

That one line sorts the drug classes: calcium channel blockers and beta-blockers for rate at the node, sodium- and potassium-channel blockers for rhythm in the muscle.

36

C > A > B, and IA prolongs repolarization

Class IC slows phase 0 most, IA moderately, IB least. For repolarization it is Goldilocks: IA prolongs (so it shares class III’s torsades risk), IB shortens, IC leaves it alone.

37

IB is Best after a heart attack, IC is Contraindicated

Any prior infarct, scar, heart failure or structural disease in the vignette rules out flecainide and propafenone. And a class IC drug always needs an AV-nodal blocker alongside it.

38

Check the potassium before you reach for the antidote

Hypokalemia increases digoxin binding and causes toxicity. Yellow-green halos, anorexia and scooped “reverse check” ST depression are the clues; a loop diuretic or erythromycin is often the precipitant.

39

Amiodarone needs lungs, thyroid, liver and eyes watched

Pulmonary fibrosis is the toxicity that kills, so baseline and annual pulmonary function tests. It is 37% iodine, hence the thyroid effects, and it inhibits CYP450.

40

Atropine first for symptomatic bradycardia

Then the catecholamines. This is the one indication-to-drug pairing Dr. Scully said he would ask about. Over-atropinised patients get physostigmine, because it crosses the blood–brain barrier.

41

Mönckeberg is in the media and harmless

Calcium in the media spares the lumen, so pipe-stem arteries on an X-ray with normal pulses are Mönckeberg. Onion skin is malignant hypertension; glassy pink is benign hypertension or diabetes.

42

The plaque that kills is the thin-capped one

A thin cap over a big lipid core ruptures and thromboses: infarction and sudden death. A thick fibrocalcific plaque gives a stable stenosis and exertional angina. The whole atheroma sits in the intima.

43

Never send an acute coronary syndrome by car

Give aspirin, nitroglycerin, oxygen if hypoxic and monitoring while the ambulance comes. Never stress-test active ischemia. Troponin is an emergency-department test.

44

Ninety minutes for a STEMI

Door-to-balloon under 90 minutes. NSTE-ACS by risk: shock or refractory pain within 2 hours, a positive troponin within 24, intermediate risk within 72.

45

On sacubitril, follow NT-proBNP

Neprilysin breaks down BNP, so sacubitril raises BNP as a drug effect. NT-proBNP is inert and unaffected. Both are falsely low in obesity; NT-proBNP climbs more in kidney disease.

46

Neutrophils at 1–3 days, macrophages at 3–7, rupture in between

The wall is softest at 3–7 days: dead muscle digested, no collagen yet. Contraction band necrosis means reperfusion.

47

Match the rupture to the artery and the sound

Inferior MI, apical murmur, flash pulmonary edema: posteromedial papillary muscle. Sternal-border murmur, thrill, oxygen step-up: septum. Anterior MI, no murmur, PEA: free wall.

48

Nitrate plus sildenafil, beta-blocker plus vasospasm

The two interactions Dr. Scully flagged: a PDE5 inhibitor with a nitrate causes refractory hypotension, and a beta-blocker in vasospastic angina leaves unopposed α constriction. Ranolazine prolongs the QT.

49

Pressure goes concentric, volume goes eccentric

Pressure overload adds sarcomeres in parallel (aortic stenosis, hypertension); volume overload adds them in series (aortic or mitral regurgitation). HFrEF dilates; HFpEF is thick and stiff with a preserved EF.

50

The commonest cause of right failure is left failure

Left: dyspnea, orthopnea, PND, crackles. Right: high JVP, edema, nutmeg liver. High-output failure (AV fistula, thyrotoxicosis, beriberi) has a high cardiac output and low resistance.

51

Four pillars, all at once, in hospital

ARNI, beta-blocker (carvedilol, metoprolol succinate, bisoprolol), MRA, SGLT2 inhibitor lower mortality. Diuretics, digoxin and inotropes only relieve symptoms. Decongest first; never start a beta-blocker while decompensated.

52

Loops lose calcium and hear badly

Furosemide: low K, Na, Ca, Mg, hypokalemic alkalosis, ototoxicity. Sulfa allergy: ethacrynic acid. When it stops working (DCT braking), add a thiazide.

53

Preload reduction slides down the same curve

A diuretic relieves congestion but does not raise output. Afterload reduction or an inotrope moves the patient to a higher Frank–Starling curve.

54

Young athlete dies on court: hypertrophic cardiomyopathy

Autosomal dominant sarcomere mutation, myofibre disarray, murmur louder with Valsalva, hypertrophy appearing in adolescence. ICD, beta-blocker, no diuretics or vasodilators.

55

Myxoma: left atrium, ball valve, IL-6

Positional syncope, emboli and fevers. Resect the stalk with a cuff of septum. Children: rhabdomyoma with tuberous sclerosis. Overall most common: metastasis.

56

Injection drug use: tricuspid, S. aureus, lungs, no murmur

Talc damages the right-sided valves and skin bacteria reach them first. Septic pulmonary emboli give cough, hemoptysis and cavitating nodules; the murmur is often absent.

57

Match the organism to the patient

Prosthetic valve: S. epidermidis (biofilm). Dental work and a damaged valve: viridans. Urinary catheter: enterococcus. S. gallolyticus: colonoscopy. Farm animals: Coxiella. Cats or lice: Bartonella.

58

Three cultures before the first dose

Prior antibiotics are the commonest cause of culture-negative endocarditis. Then empiric vancomycin plus cefepime or ceftriaxone, bactericidal, for weeks.

59

Osler hurts, Janeway doesn’t

Osler nodes and Roth spots are immune complexes; Janeway lesions and splinters are emboli. Duke definite: 2 major, 1 major + 3 minor, or 5 minor.

60

Young person, viral prodrome, new heart failure: myocarditis

Parvovirus B19 and HHV-6 now; Coxsackie B on older questions. Lyme gives heart block, diphtheria toxin kills, Chagas comes decades later. Rest and support; antivirals unproven.

61

Better leaning forward, everywhere on the ECG

Pericarditis: pleuritic pain to the trapezius, a rub, diffuse ST elevation with PR depression. NSAIDs plus colchicine. Hypotension, JVD and muffled sounds mean tamponade — drain it.

62

Early calcific AS means a bicuspid valve — and look at the aorta

Bicuspid AS presents in the 40s–60s and always comes with an aortopathy: screen for an ascending aneurysm and coarctation. No drug slows AS; symptoms mean about half are dead in 2 years without replacement.

63

Organic MR is repaired, functional MR is replaced

A torn chord or myxomatous leaflet: repair. A normal valve in a dilated LV: replace. Rheumatic MS: beta-blocker, diuretic, warfarin for AF, balloon commissurotomy.

64

Mechanical means warfarin forever; TAVR needs calcium

Mechanical valves: lifelong warfarin, never a DOAC. Tissue valves: aspirin, but they wear out. TAVR anchors on a calcified annulus, so not for pure AR. New heart block after either replacement means a pacemaker.

65

Blue early is right-to-left; blue late is Eisenmenger

The 5 T’s are blue from birth. A VSD, ASD or PDA is pink until years of lung flood cause fixed pulmonary hypertension and the shunt reverses — then it is too late to close.

66

Shock as the ductus closes means PGE₁

Hypoplastic left heart, transposition with an intact septum, critical coarctation: the baby is well, then crashes on day 1–3. Reopen the ductus with prostaglandin E1; indomethacin is for closing it.

67

Feel the femorals; the boot is tetralogy

Weak femorals with arm pressure above leg pressure: coarctation (think Turner in a girl). Boot-shaped heart and tet spells relieved by squatting: tetralogy (think 22q11).

68

Classify by the higher number, measure properly

Normal under 120/80; elevated 120–129; stage 1 130–139 or 80–89; stage 2 140/90 or more. Seated, back supported, 5 minutes’ rest, right cuff, two or more readings on two or more visits. Confirm with home or ambulatory readings.

69

Young, abrupt or hypokalemic hypertension: look for a cause

Low potassium with alkalosis: aldosterone-to-renin ratio. Bruit and a creatinine jump on an ACE inhibitor: renal artery stenosis. Snoring: sleep apnea. Arm above leg: coarctation.

70

Emergency is organ damage, not a number

Hypertensive emergency: lower mean pressure by no more than about 25% in the first hour with IV drugs. Urgency: oral drugs and follow-up. Fixed narrow arterioles under-perfuse if you drop the pressure too fast.

71

ACE inhibitor plus ARB is a mistake; pregnancy rules them both out

ACE inhibitors cough and swell (bradykinin); both raise potassium and creatinine and are teratogenic. Thiazides: low K, Na, Mg, high Ca, glucose, urate. In pregnancy: labetalol, nifedipine, methyldopa.

72

Statins first, and know the muscle

High-intensity statin for clinical ASCVD, LDL 190 or more, and diabetes 40–75 with risk. Myopathy with fibrates or CYP3A4 inhibitors. Then ezetimibe, then a PCSK9 inhibitor or inclisiran.

73

Xanthomas in a child mean the LDL receptor

Familial hypercholesterolemia (autosomal dominant): tendon xanthomas, early MI. Eruptive xanthomas and pancreatitis mean triglycerides over 1,000 — fibrate.

74

Smoker, 65 or older, AAA: ultrasound once; 5.5 cm means repair

Screen men 65–75 who ever smoked. Repair at 5.5 cm (5.0 in women), growth over 0.5 cm in 6 months, or symptoms. Pulsatile mass plus pain plus hypotension is rupture: straight to theatre.

75

Tearing pain to the back: type A goes to surgery, type B to beta-blockers

Ascending (Stanford A): emergency surgery. Descending (B): IV esmolol first, then vasodilators, to heart rate under 60 and systolic 100–120. Never thrombolyse a dissection.

76

Fibrillin is Marfan, collagen III ruptures arteries

Marfan: FBN1, root aneurysm, upward lens dislocation, MVP. Vascular EDS: COL3A1, medium arteries, bowel and uterus rupture. Loeys–Dietz: TGF-β receptor, dissects small.

77

Jaw claudication over 50: steroids before the biopsy

Giant cell arteritis risks blindness; ESR high, polymyalgia. Young Asian woman with no arm pulses: Takayasu. Child with 5 days of fever: Kawasaki — echo, IVIG and aspirin. c-ANCA plus sinus, lung and kidney: GPA.

78

Who feels better walking uphill? The spine patient

Vascular claudication: fixed distance, relieved by standing still, low ABI (0.90 or less; over 1.40 means calcified). Neurogenic: relieved by sitting or leaning on a cart, normal pulses. CTA diagnoses; the angiogram is for procedures.

79

Six P’s: heparin first, and watch the compartment

Acute limb ischemia from an AF embolus: IV heparin, then lysis if the foot still moves and feels, otherwise Fogarty embolectomy. Pain on passive stretch after reperfusion: fasciotomy.

80

Exercise first; cilostazol never in heart failure

Claudication: supervised walking 30–45 minutes, 3 times a week, 12 weeks; quit smoking; high-intensity statin; aspirin or clopidogrel, or aspirin plus rivaroxaban 2.5 mg twice daily. Cilostazol is a PDE3 inhibitor with a boxed HF warning.

81

Atrial fibrillation plus pain out of proportion: SMA embolus

CT angiogram, embolectomy, lifelong anticoagulation; when in doubt, operate. Pain after meals with weight loss is chronic mesenteric ischemia: revascularise.
