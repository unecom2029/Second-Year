Master High-Yield Checklist

The facts that show up in more than one objective, or that the source lectures flagged explicitly. If you can produce all of these cold, you own the covered half of the exam.

Abbreviations used in this section CBC complete blood count · MCV mean corpuscular volume (average red cell size, in femtolitres) · TIBC total iron binding capacity · LDH lactate dehydrogenase · ABO the major blood group system · RhD the D antigen of the Rhesus blood group · INR international normalized ratio · ART antiretroviral therapy · CD4 the surface marker defining helper T cells · SOFA Sequential Organ Failure Assessment
01

One test splits the anemia tree

The reticulocyte count. Low means a production problem (iron, B12, folate, inflammation, kidney). High means loss or destruction (bleeding, hemolysis).

02

Read the MCV before anything else

Under 80 fL microcytic, 80–100 normocytic, over 100 macrocytic. A normal MCV can be two diseases averaging out — check the red cell distribution width and the smear.

03

Ferritin and TIBC move opposite ways

Iron deficiency: ferritin LOW, TIBC HIGH. Anemia of inflammation: ferritin HIGH, TIBC LOW. Serum iron is low in both and discriminates nothing.

04

Iron deficiency is a symptom, not a diagnosis

In an adult male or a postmenopausal woman it is gastrointestinal blood loss until proven otherwise — that is a colonoscopy, not a prescription.

05

Methylmalonic acid separates B12 from folate

Elevated in B12, normal in folate. Homocysteine rises in both, so it cannot discriminate. B12 has the brain; folate does not.

06

Never give folate alone for a macrocytosis

It corrects the blood count while subacute combined degeneration of the dorsal and lateral columns keeps progressing toward irreversible.

07

Three microcytic anemias, three treatments

Iron deficiency → give iron. Anemia of inflammation → treat the disease. Thalassemia trait → give nothing, and specifically not iron.

08

Pink plasma, dark urine, absent haptoglobin

The intravascular hemolysis triad. Free heme is nephrotoxic — this is why intravascular hemolysis is the dangerous kind and why ABO-incompatible transfusion kills.

09

Match the cell to the culprit

Ball → spherocytosis. Bite → G6PD. Bull's-eye → thalassemia. Crescent → sickle. Fragment → microangiopathy. Howell-Jolly body → no spleen.

10

Sudden anemia + LOW reticulocytes in a chronic hemolytic

Aplastic crisis from parvovirus B19 until proven otherwise. A maximally compensating marrow cannot afford a week off.

11

The direct Coombs test settles the spherocyte

Negative → hereditary spherocytosis. Positive → immune-mediated hemolysis. Same smear, opposite disease.

12

Restrictive transfusion won the trial

TRICC: transfuse a stable euvolemic patient below 7 g/dL, one unit at a time. The liberal (10 g/dL) arm did worse.

13

Ten, twenty, twenty-five

Platelets: 10,000 for hypoproliferative thrombocytopenia, 20,000 before a lumbar puncture, 25,000 for a preterm neonate. Consumptive thrombocytopenia is not treated by the number.

14

The ocean and the teacup

You need only 30% factor activity to clot. Plasma for an INR of 1.1 is pure risk for no benefit.

15

TRALI versus TACO

Both hypoxic with a white-out. TRALI: hypotensive, normal jugular venous pressure, donor anti-HLA antibodies, diuresis does not help. TACO: hypertensive, raised jugular venous pressure, diuresis helps.

16

Irradiate to prevent graft-versus-host disease

The only defence. Required for the severely immunocompromised and for related donors — partial HLA matching is what makes directed donation dangerous.

17

White clot versus red clot

Arterial, platelet-rich → antiplatelet. Venous, fibrin-rich → anticoagulant. This one distinction answers a surprising number of “which drug” questions.

18

1972, plus protein C and S

Warfarin blocks vitamin K–dependent factors II, VII, IX, X — and protein C and S, which fall first. That is why it starts by making you clot and why you bridge.

19

Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia is a clotting emergency

Platelets fall 5–10 days in, but the danger is thrombosis. Stop all heparin, start argatroban, bivalirudin or fondaparinux. Do not start warfarin alone.

20

Eleven is the ESA ceiling

Every erythropoiesis-stimulating agent boxed warning turns on hemoglobin above 11 g/dL = harm with no benefit. And cut the dose if it rises more than 1 g/dL in 2 weeks.

21

Sepsis is the response, not the organism

Cultures are negative in 30–50%. SOFA ≥ 2 ≈ 10% mortality. qSOFA: respiratory rate ≥ 22, systolic pressure ≤ 100, altered mentation.

22

Two different CD4 numbers

Under 200/µL defines AIDS. Under 50/µL is where opportunistic infection risk is greatest. They are not the same threshold.

23

CD4 stages, viral load transmits

The CD4 count tells you the damage and who needs prophylaxis. The viral load tells you the activity and drives U = U.

24

One point mutation kills a whole ART class

NNRTIs — all members bind the same allosteric pocket. Protease inhibitors sit at the other extreme: resistance is uncommon unless previously treated with one.

🪝 The one framing that ties the anemia half together

Every anemia lands in one of three buckets, and the lecture returned to this slide before every new topic:

  • Blood loss — acute (trauma) or chronic (gastrointestinal, gynecologic).
  • Impaired production — the factory is not building. Reticulocytes low. Iron, B12, folate, inflammation. Sections 3–6.
  • Increased destruction — the factory works and the cells are destroyed. Reticulocytes high. Sections 7–10.

Patients do not always fall neatly into one bucket — but you need the buckets before you can notice when someone straddles two.

🪝 And the one that ties the pharmacology half together

Two machines, not eighty drugs. Machine 1, the erythron, builds red cells: iron in, vitamins in, erythropoietin signal on, hemoglobin out. Every anemia drug answers which ingredient is missing? Machine 2, hemostasis, builds and dissolves a clot: platelets stick, the cascade fires, fibrin sets, plasmin dissolves. Every clotting drug answers which step am I cutting? Name the missing ingredient or the cut step and the drug names itself.

Section 1 · Normal hematopoiesis

The Stem Cell Hierarchy, the Two Lineages, and Where Blood Is Made

One cell type at the top, two great branches beneath it, and every blood cell you will ever name at the bottom. Learn the hierarchy once and the rest of hematology has somewhere to live.

Abbreviations used in this section HSC hematopoietic stem cell — the self-renewing cell every blood cell descends from · RBC red blood cell, also called an erythrocyte · WBC white blood cell, also called a leukocyte · EPO erythropoietin — the kidney hormone that orders red cell production · TPO thrombopoietin — the platelet equivalent · G-CSF granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (drug form: filgrastim) · GM-CSF granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (drug form: sargramostim) · IGF insulin-like growth factor
🔑 Definition, and the two properties that make a stem cell a stem cell

Hematopoiesis is the production and development of blood cells from hematopoietic stem cells. It begins in embryonic life and continues throughout life inside specialized microenvironments.

A hematopoietic stem cell needs both of: self-renewal (it makes more copies of itself, so the supply never runs out) and differentiation (it makes committed daughter cells). It is multipotent — one cell can produce red cells, white cells and platelets.

The pathologic corollary, stated plainly in lecture: a stem cell that keeps dividing for your whole life is normal; cancer is a cell that keeps dividing and is not supposed to. When hematopoietic stem cells make too many of themselves, that is leukemia.

Sites of hematopoiesis across a lifetime ★ HY

  1. Embryo — the yolk sac. The first, primitive wave of blood formation happens outside the embryo proper.
  2. Fetus — liver and spleen. The fetal liver becomes the dominant site mid-gestation, with a contribution from the spleen.
  3. Late fetus onward — bone marrow. Marrow takes over before birth and remains the site for the rest of life.
  4. Adult — the axial skeleton. Hematopoiesis occurs primarily in the marrow of the vertebrae, ribs, sternum and pelvis. The long bones convert to fatty (yellow) marrow.
🪝 Memory hook — “Yolk → liver → marrow, then the marrow retreats to the core”

Blood formation moves inward and upward over a lifetime: outside the embryo, then into the belly, then into bone — and in adults it retreats out of the arms and legs into the axial skeleton. That last fact is why a marrow biopsy is taken from the pelvis and not the femur, and why the sternum and iliac crest are the sites you will hear named on the wards.

🩺 When the body reopens sites it abandoned

Under extreme marrow demand — severe chronic hemolysis, thalassemia major — the body reactivates fetal sites. Extramedullary hematopoiesis in liver and spleen, plus expansion of the marrow cavities of the skull and face, produces the frontal bossing and maxillary prominence of the thalassemia case in Section 9.

The two lineages ★ HY

🧱

The myeloid branch — does the work

  • Erythrocyte — proerythroblast to mature red cell. Loses its nucleus on the way out of the marrow. A biconcave disc, extremely deformable, roughly 120-day lifespan.
  • Megakaryocyte — a giant marrow cell that stays put and blebs off fragments of its own membrane. Those anucleate fragments are platelets.
  • Neutrophil — the first responder to bacterial infection; multilobed nucleus, normally three to five lobes.
  • Monocyte — circulates, then leaves the blood to become a macrophage or dendritic cell.
  • Eosinophil and basophil — brightly granulated; allergy and parasitic defence.
🧠

The lymphoid branch — keeps the memory

  • T cell — educated in the thymus; trained to tolerate self and destroy non-self. That one sentence also explains transfusion reactions and graft-versus-host disease in Section 13.
  • B cell — matures in the bone marrow; recognizes antigen and on activation gives rise to plasma cells.
  • Plasma cell — the terminally differentiated B cell; the antibody factory of the humoral response.
  • NK (natural killer) cell — kills without prior sensitization; part of innate immunity.
🪝 Memory hook — “Myeloid does the work; lymphoid keeps the memory”

The myeloid line handles physical jobs — carry the oxygen, plug the hole, eat the bacteria. The lymphoid line handles the intelligence job — remember what it has seen. That is exactly what a vaccine exploits: show the lymphoid line a pathogen it does not have to survive, and it remembers.

🧠 Erythropoietin — the oxygen sensor loop

The kidney continuously samples how much oxygen is arriving. When tissue oxygen delivery falls — from anemia, hypoxia or altitude — it releases erythropoietin, which drives division and differentiation of committed erythroid progenitors and pushes reticulocytes into the blood. One loop explains three clinical facts:

  • Chronic kidney disease causes anemia — the sensor and the foreman are the same organ, and it has failed.
  • Erythropoietin can be given as a drug (epoetin, darbepoetin) — you supply the missing order.
  • Inflammation blunts erythropoietin release — one of the four mechanisms behind anemia of inflammation in Section 6.

And the corollary that runs through the pharmacology: an erythropoiesis-stimulating agent is useless without iron. You can shout the order all you like; the factory still needs bricks.

🩺 The reticulocyte count — the single most useful add-on test

A reticulocyte is a newly released red cell still carrying residual RNA. The count answers exactly one question: is the marrow responding?

  • Low reticulocytes with anemia = a production problem. Everything in Sections 3–6.
  • High reticulocytes with anemia = loss or destruction. Bleeding, or the hemolytic anemias of Sections 7–9.

Q: Which mature blood cell is not really a cell at all, and what does that fact explain about aspirin?

Answer

The platelet. It is a membrane fragment blebbed off a megakaryocyte and has no nucleus. Because it cannot transcribe new protein, an irreversible inhibitor such as aspirin disables it for its entire 7–10 day lifespan — the platelet cannot make replacement enzyme. That is why aspirin is stopped about a week before major surgery (Section 15).

Q: A 68-year-old on dialysis has a hemoglobin of 8.4 g/dL, a normal MCV, normal iron studies and a low reticulocyte count. What is the mechanism, and what single laboratory value would you predict?

Answer

Anemia of chronic kidney disease. The failing kidney no longer makes enough erythropoietin, so the marrow is never told to build — hence a low reticulocyte count despite plenty of raw material. You would predict an inappropriately low erythropoietin level for that degree of anemia. Treatment is an erythropoiesis-stimulating agent, with iron stores repleted first.

Q: A patient's marrow is packed with immature cells of a single myeloid line, crowding out everything else. What has gone wrong at the top of the hierarchy?

Answer

A stem or progenitor cell has begun self-renewing without differentiating — proliferation uncoupled from maturation. In the blood system that is leukemia. The marrow looks hypercellular, and the other lineages fail because they have been crowded out.

Section 2 · Normal hematopoiesis

The Marrow Niche, the Biopsy, and Reading the CBC

Knowing when not to order a marrow biopsy is as much of the objective as knowing how it is done.

Components of the niche

Stem cells do not float free. They live in a structured neighbourhood that decides whether they rest or divide — and that neighbourhood is itself a therapeutic target.

1

Stromal cells

Fibroblasts, osteoblasts along the bone surface, adipocytes and endothelial cells. They anchor stem cells and secrete the signals that hold them in place.

2

Extracellular matrix

The structural mesh providing adhesion sites. Losing adhesion is how a stem cell is mobilized into the blood — the principle behind harvesting stem cells for transplant.

3

Blood vessels

The sinusoids — nutrients in, and the exit door. A reticulocyte squeezes through a sinusoidal wall to enter the circulation.

4

Signaling molecules

Cytokines and growth factors that set the balance between quiescence (rest) and activation (divide).

🧠 Quiescence is the point

The niche's real job is restraint. Most hematopoietic stem cells are quiescent most of the time — that is what protects the pool from exhaustion and from accumulating replication errors across a lifetime. The niche activates only as many stem cells as demand requires.

Three therapies live here: stem cell transplantation (empty the niche with conditioning chemotherapy so the graft has somewhere to land), gene therapy (Casgevy and Lyfgenia for sickle cell disease, which still require chemotherapy conditioning), and ordinary cancer chemotherapy, which hits the niche hardest of all — the reason counts crash and hair falls out.

🪝 Memory hook — the niche is a hotel, not a warehouse

A warehouse stores things. A hotel has a front desk deciding who stays asleep in their room (quiescence), who is called down to work (activation), and who checks out into the bloodstream. Disrupt the hotel and it does not matter how good your stem cells are — which is why every transplant begins by emptying the building.

Aspirate and core biopsy

  1. Position and site. The patient lies prone; the operator palpates the posterior superior iliac spine and preps and drapes sterilely.
  2. Anesthesia. Local anesthetic to skin and periosteum, plus at least mild sedation. It takes real physical force, and is usually performed by a hematologist-oncologist — not in the emergency department.
  3. Aspirate. A trocar is advanced through the cortex and marrow fluid is drawn into a syringe, then squirted onto slides. Gives cytology: individual cell morphology, blast percentage, iron stain.
  4. Core biopsy. A cylindrical core of bone. Preserves architecture — trabeculae, fat-to-cell ratio, fibrosis, infiltration pattern — which an aspirate cannot show.
🪝 Memory hook — aspirate = the players; core = the stadium

The aspirate is a smear of loose cells: you see each player's face clearly but have no idea how they were arranged. The core keeps the seating plan — how full the stadium is, where the fibrosis is, whether an infiltrate is patchy or diffuse. Cellularity is a core-biopsy word, not an aspirate word.

Cellularity — the first read ★ HY

Cellularity is the ratio of hematopoietic cells to fat. On the slide, bright pink struts are bone trabeculae; the clear round holes are fat; the dense purple material is maturing blood cells with stem cells scattered among them.

⚠️ Do not order a marrow biopsy to work up a straightforward anemia

Most anemia is diagnosed from blood drawn out of the arm — a complete blood count, red cell indices, iron studies, B12 and folate, a reticulocyte count, and a look at the smear. A biopsy is reserved for suspected primary marrow disease: unexplained pancytopenia, suspected leukemia or myelodysplasia, staging a lymphoma, or confirming remission.

🩺 The one exception worth knowing — the iron stain

You do not need a biopsy to diagnose iron deficiency. But if one is done for another reason, the Prussian blue stain is the historical gold standard for marrow iron: blue granules present means stores exist; their complete absence is diagnostic of iron deficiency. It is also how you catch iron deficiency hiding underneath another marrow disorder.

The CBC — what each number actually measures ★ HY

🧪

Hemoglobin — a direct chemical measurement

  • Measured by spectrophotometry — hemoglobin absorbs light at a characteristic wavelength.
  • Reported in grams per decilitre (g/dL).
  • Because hemoglobin is confined to red cells, it is an indirect measure of red cell mass.
  • Each molecule has four subunits — two alpha and two beta — each carrying one oxygen molecule.
  • The measurement favoured in the United Kingdom.
📏

Hematocrit — a physical measurement

  • Spin whole blood; measure the height of the red column as a percentage of total.
  • Also called packed cell volume.
  • Normal ranges differ by sex: roughly 37–47% in females, 42–52% in males.
  • An elevated hematocrit is polycythemia, not anemia.
  • The measurement favoured in the United States.

Menstruation — a recurring monthly iron and blood loss across a large part of adult life. That single fact also drives the first anemia case in Section 4.

⚠️ Acute blood loss is a different clinical animal

With acute hemorrhage you lose whole blood — plasma volume and red cells together. Early on the hemoglobin can look deceptively normal, because the concentration has not changed; it falls only once the intravascular volume is replaced, over hours. The immediate threat in acute bleeding is volume, not the number on the CBC.

Q: A 7-year-old finished induction chemotherapy for acute lymphoblastic leukemia three weeks ago. A marrow biopsy shows a markedly hypocellular marrow with rare hematopoietic elements. Is this a treatment failure?

Answer

No — this is the expected and intended result. High-dose chemotherapy is given specifically to ablate the marrow and eliminate leukemic cells, so a hypocellular marrow at this point reflects the treatment working. The questions that matter are whether residual blasts remain and whether normal hematopoiesis subsequently recovers. Hypocellular is not a diagnosis on its own — it must be read against the clinical context.

Q: A 34-year-old is brought in after a motor vehicle collision with obvious hemorrhage. Her hemoglobin on arrival is 13.8 g/dL. Does this rule out significant blood loss?

Answer

No. In acute hemorrhage the patient loses plasma and red cells in the same proportion, so the concentration — which is what hemoglobin and hematocrit measure — is initially unchanged. The value falls only after fluid shifts into the intravascular space or resuscitation fluid is given. Manage the hemodynamics and the bleeding source, not the initial hemoglobin.

Q: A centrifuged tube shows a red column occupying 27% of the total height. Name the measurement and estimate the hemoglobin.

Answer

The hematocrit — 27%. Using the rule that hematocrit is roughly three times the hemoglobin, the hemoglobin is about 9 g/dL. The factor of three is a useful approximation, not a law.

Section 3 · Anemia I

Defining, Measuring and Classifying Anemia

Two definitions, two independent classification systems — and you use both at once. Mechanism tells you why; the red cell indices tell you which one.

🔑 The two definitions — know both wordings
  • Physiologic definition — a reduction in the oxygen-carrying capacity of the blood.
  • Laboratory definition — a reduction below normal limits of the total circulating red cell mass, as measured by hemoglobin concentration or hematocrit.

What anemia does to the patient

  • Pallor — best appreciated in conjunctivae, palmar creases and nail beds.
  • Weakness and fatigue.
  • Dyspnea on exertion — the classic history is getting short of breath climbing stairs that used to be easy.
  • If severe: myocardial and central nervous system hypoxia. The brain does not tolerate significant anemia.

Reduce the tree to one question. Factory problem (impaired production): not enough cells are built; reticulocytes low. System 2 — classification by red cell indices ★ HY

🧠 Why a red cell looks like a doughnut

A normal red cell is a biconcave disc — thick at the rim, thin in the middle. On a smear that thin middle reads as a pale circle: the central pallor, normally about one third of the cell's diameter. The shape maximizes surface area for gas exchange and makes the cell extraordinarily deformable, so it can fold through capillaries narrower than itself.

Two things go wrong with it, and each has its own section:

  • Not enough hemoglobin to fill it → the pale centre grows → hypochromia (iron deficiency, thalassemia).
  • The cytoskeleton fails and it balls up → the pale centre disappears → a spherocyte (Section 8). It stops being a doughnut and becomes a baseball.

The practical algorithm — the one to reproduce under pressure

  1. Confirm the anemia. Hemoglobin or hematocrit below the sex-specific reference range.
  2. Read the MCV first. Microcytic, normocytic or macrocytic — this is where the differential splits.
  3. Get a reticulocyte count. Low points to a production problem; high points to loss or destruction.
  4. Order the targeted studies. Microcytic → iron studies. Macrocytic → B12, folate and methylmalonic acid. Normocytic with low reticulocytes → inflammation, kidney disease or early marrow disease.
  5. Look at the smear. Hypersegmented neutrophils, target cells, spherocytes, bite cells and sickled cells are each nearly diagnostic on sight.

Q: A patient has a hemoglobin of 10.1 g/dL with an MCV of 89 fL. A colleague says the normal MCV rules out a nutritional deficiency. Where is the flaw?

Answer

A normal MCV is an average. A patient with both iron deficiency (microcytic) and B12 or folate deficiency (macrocytic) can average out to a perfectly normocytic value — real and common in malnutrition, malabsorption, bariatric surgery and the elderly. Two clues expose it: a raised red cell distribution width, and a smear showing a dimorphic population of both small pale cells and large oval ones. Patients do not always have exactly one diagnosis.

Iron Metabolism and Iron Deficiency Anemia

The most common nutritional deficiency in the world. It looks like it should be simple and is not — because there are four separate places along the pathway where it can break, and the laboratory pattern tells you which.

Four stations, four ways to fail ★ HY

  1. Duodenum — absorption. Dietary iron is absorbed in the duodenum and only as Fe²⁺; roughly 1–2 mg per day. Fails in celiac disease, chronic diarrhea, gastrectomy or bypass, and acid suppression.
  2. Plasma — transport on transferrin. Transferrin carries about 3 mg of Fe³⁺ at any moment. Picture a molecule with little cups that may be full or empty — that image is the whole basis of the TIBC.
  3. Cells — utilization. Iron is delivered to marrow for erythropoiesis (~2500 mg sits in circulating erythrocytes) and to muscle for myoglobin (~500 mg). Fails in anemia of chronic disease, where iron exists but cannot be released.
  4. Liver and macrophages — storage. Roughly 1000 mg as ferritin and hemosiderin; senescent red cells are recycled here. The first pool to empty in chronic loss.
🪝 Memory hook — ferritin is the fuel gauge; transferrin saturation is the fuel line

Ferritin tells you what is in the tank — the earliest thing to fall, before the hemoglobin ever moves. Transferrin saturation tells you what is reaching the engine right now. You can have a full tank and a blocked line — that is anemia of chronic disease, Section 6.

How people become iron deficient

1

Dietary lack

Rare where food is abundant, but real in the elderly, and in infants and toddlers fed largely on milk, which contains very little bioavailable iron.

2

Impaired absorption

Celiac disease, chronic diarrhea, gastrectomy and bariatric surgery — anything that removes or bypasses the duodenum, or removes gastric acid.

3

Increased requirements

Growing infants and children, and pregnancy.

4

Chronic blood loss

The most important cause in the Western world. Gastrointestinal: ulcers, hemorrhoids, cancer. Female genital tract: menorrhagia, uterine cancer.

⚠️ The single highest-yield sentence in the anemia lectures

Iron deficiency in an adult male or a postmenopausal woman should be considered to be caused by gastrointestinal blood loss until proven otherwise.

The reason: gastrointestinal cancer — adenocarcinoma of the colon in particular — can present with iron deficiency anemia and nothing else. The patient is bleeding into the stool, does not know it, and the loss is chronic and insidious. That is why iron deficiency in this group triggers a fecal occult blood test and endoscopic evaluation, not just a prescription for iron. Iron deficiency is a symptom, not a final diagnosis — always ask where the iron went.

🧠 Why iron deficiency makes cells small as well as pale

An erythroid precursor divides while it accumulates hemoglobin, and stops dividing once it reaches a target hemoglobin concentration. Without iron it cannot reach that concentration on schedule, so it undergoes an extra division or two before maturing. Each division halves the cell. One mechanism, two findings: microcytic and hypochromic.

Contrast this with B12 and folate deficiency, where the cytoplasm matures normally but the nucleus cannot divide — so the cell ends up too big. Same organ, opposite direction, and the reason the MCV splits the differential so cleanly.

🪝 Memory hook — empty cups, high capacity

The TIBC is the one students reliably get backwards. Hold the picture: transferrin carries cups. When you are iron deficient the cups float around empty — so the capacity to bind more iron is high. Empty cups = high TIBC = low saturation.

Now flip it for Section 6: in anemia of chronic disease the body hides iron and makes less transferrin. Fewer cups on the shelf = low TIBC. That single difference separates the two most commonly confused anemias in the hospital.

⚠️ Ferritin is an acute phase reactant

Ferritin rises in inflammation, infection, liver disease and malignancy regardless of iron stores. So a genuinely iron-deficient patient who is also acutely sick can have a normal or even elevated ferritin. Diagnosing anemia is never one test — interpret ferritin alongside transferrin saturation, TIBC, C-reactive protein and the smear.

🩺 Treatment — and why it fails
  • Oral iron plus dietary change is first line, together with correcting the underlying cause.
  • Adherence is the real obstacle. Oral iron causes nausea, constipation, metallic taste and black stools — and patients quit. Full pharmacology, including when to give up on the oral route, is in Section 16.
  • Diet alone will not correct an established deficiency. Once someone is genuinely deficient you must replete stores.
  • Expect a reticulocyte rise within about a week if the treatment is working.

Q: A 58-year-old man has a hemoglobin of 10.8 g/dL, MCV 73 fL, ferritin 8 ng/mL and transferrin saturation 9%. He has no symptoms other than fatigue. What is the diagnosis, and what is the next step?

Answer

Iron deficiency anemia — microcytic, with low ferritin and low transferrin saturation. The next step is not to prescribe iron and stop there. In an adult male, iron deficiency is presumed to be gastrointestinal blood loss until proven otherwise, so he needs fecal occult blood testing and, importantly, endoscopic evaluation to exclude colorectal carcinoma. Treat the iron and find the leak.

Q: Explain why the total iron binding capacity is HIGH in iron deficiency but LOW in anemia of chronic disease.

Answer

TIBC measures unsaturated transferrin binding sites. In iron deficiency there is little iron to occupy those sites and the liver increases transferrin production, so unbound capacity is high. In anemia of chronic disease, inflammation both reduces transferrin synthesis (it is a negative acute phase reactant) and sequesters iron inside macrophages, so there are fewer binding sites overall and the TIBC is low to normal even though serum iron is also low.

Q (case): A 25-year-old woman has fatigue, dyspnea, pallor, a craving for chewing ice, heavy menstrual bleeding and a vegetarian diet. Hemoglobin 7 g/dL, MCV 74 fL, ferritin low. Diagnosis, the value that settles it, and management?

Answer

Iron deficiency anemia from menstrual blood loss plus dietary insufficiency. Microcytic anemia with a low ferritin is iron deficiency until proven otherwise, and the ferritin settles it. Pica — craving non-nutritive substances, classically ice (pagophagia) — is distinctive and highly suggestive. She has both mechanisms at once: chronic loss through menorrhagia and reduced intake of bioavailable iron.

Note the discrimination: in a premenopausal woman with obvious menorrhagia you do not automatically pursue a gastrointestinal source — that rule applies to adult males and postmenopausal women. Management: oral iron and dietary change, warning her up front about the gastrointestinal side effects, and continuing iron after the hemoglobin normalizes to refill stores.

Megaloblastic Anemia — Vitamin B12, Pernicious Anemia and Folate

Iron deficiency breaks hemoglobin synthesis and gives you small cells. B12 and folate deficiency break DNA synthesis and give you big ones. Same organ, opposite direction — and one of the two also destroys the spinal cord.

Abbreviations used in this section B12 vitamin B12, also called cobalamin · IF intrinsic factor — the protein from gastric parietal cells that B12 must bind to be absorbed · MMA methylmalonic acid — a metabolite that accumulates when B12 is deficient · THF tetrahydrofolate — the active form of folate · PA pernicious anemia — autoimmune loss of intrinsic factor · SCD here, subacute combined degeneration — degeneration of the dorsal and lateral spinal columns · LDH lactate dehydrogenase · NTD neural tube defect
🔑 Vitamin B12 has two jobs; folate has one
  • DNA synthesis — shared with folate. Failure here produces the megaloblastic anemia.
  • Maintenance of the myelin surrounding neurons — B12 only. Failure here produces the neurologic complications, which eventually become irreversible.

Folate has only the first job. That single asymmetry is the most testable fact in this section.

Megaloblastic morphology ★ HY

  • Erythrocytes larger than normal and oval — macro-ovalocytes.
  • Neutrophils larger and hypersegmented, with five to six or more nuclear lobes. A normal neutrophil has three to five.
  • The marrow is hypercellular, with large red cell precursors containing fine, open chromatin — the megaloblasts — plus enlarged granulocyte precursors (giant bands and metamyelocytes).
🧠 Nuclear–cytoplasmic asynchrony — one idea behind every finding

Hemoglobin synthesis in the cytoplasm proceeds normally, because it needs iron, not DNA. Nuclear maturation and cell division stall, because they need thymidine, which needs folate, which needs B12 to be regenerated. The cytoplasm outruns the nucleus. Every finding drops out of that sentence:

  • The cell keeps growing but cannot divide → macrocytic.
  • The nucleus stays immature → fine, open chromatin in marrow precursors.
  • The defect hits every dividing cell → hypersegmented neutrophils and giant bands.
  • Many precursors die in the marrow before release → ineffective erythropoiesis: hypercellular marrow with a low reticulocyte count and an elevated LDH.
🔑 Ineffective hematopoiesis — the definition, and where it appears ★ HY

Ineffective hematopoiesis means precursors are produced in large numbers but destroyed within the marrow before release. The signature pair is a hypercellular marrow with a low reticulocyte count, plus a raised LDH and indirect bilirubin from intramedullary cell death.

Diseases it is seen in: megaloblastic anemia (B12 and folate deficiency), thalassemia (unpaired globin chains kill precursors — Section 9), myelodysplastic syndrome, sideroblastic anemias, and severe iron deficiency.

🪝 Memory hook — the hypersegmented neutrophil is the giveaway

Macrocytosis has a long differential — alcohol, liver disease, hypothyroidism, drugs, reticulocytosis. But a hypersegmented neutrophil says the problem is specifically DNA synthesis, which narrows you immediately to B12 or folate. Count the lobes: six or more, think megaloblastic.

Vitamin B12 metabolism — a long road with many exits

  1. Diet. The major source is animal products. Plants contain very little cobalamin — which is why strict vegans are at genuine risk.
  2. Stomach — liberation. Gastric pepsin and acid liberate B12 from dietary binding proteins. Salivary haptocorrin then carries it onward.
  3. Stomach — intrinsic factor. Parietal cells of the gastric mucosa secrete intrinsic factor. These are the same cells that make gastric acid — remember that pairing.
  4. Duodenum — the handoff. Pancreatic proteases split the haptocorrin–B12 complex, freeing B12 to bind intrinsic factor.
  5. Terminal ileum — absorption. The complex is absorbed via the cubilin receptor. This is the only place it happens.
  6. Blood — transport. B12 binds transcobalamin II, which delivers it to the cells of the body.

🔑 It is an autoimmune disease, not a diet problem

⚠️ Treating pernicious anemia means treating three problems

You are not just giving more B12. You are managing an autoimmune gastritis with cancer risk, a hematologic deficiency, and a neurologic degeneration. And B12 must be given by a route that does not require intrinsic factor — intramuscular injection, or high-dose oral therapy relying on passive diffusion.

🩺 The Schilling test — historical, but it teaches the anatomy

No longer performed, but it localizes the lesion better than any single modern test. Saturate the body with injected non-radioactive B12 so any absorbed oral dose spills into the urine. Stage I: oral radiolabelled B12 alone; no radiolabel in the urine means it was not absorbed. Stage II: repeat with exogenous intrinsic factor — if the radiolabel now appears, the missing ingredient was intrinsic factor, i.e. pernicious anemia. Still absent? The defect is at the terminal ileum — ileitis, ileal resection or bacterial overgrowth.

🔑 Why folic acid is required in pregnancy ★ HY

Folate is the key nutrient for neural tube closure. Supplementation prevents the neural tube defects spina bifida and anencephaly, which is why every prenatal vitamin contains more folic acid than a general multivitamin.

The timing is the whole point: the neural tube closes at about 4 weeks — often before a pregnancy is even recognized. So the recommendation is to supplement before conception, in all women of childbearing potential, not after a positive test. Pregnancy is also a state of increased folate requirement in its own right.

🧠 Why chemotherapy targets folate

Chemotherapy is broadly a poison aimed at fast-dividing cells. What are fast-dividing cells busy doing? DNA synthesis. So shut down DNA synthesis — and the folate pathway is the obvious lever. That is also why the side effects are predictable: the fastest-dividing normal tissues suffer first. The marrow empties out, the gut mucosa ulcerates, and the hair falls out.

🪝 Memory hook — “MMA rises with B12 — the M's go together”

Methylmalonic acid is elevated in B12 deficiency and normal in folate deficiency. Homocysteine goes up in both, so it only tells you one of the two is missing. If you can order one test to separate them, order the methylmalonic acid. And the clinical version: B12 has the Brain. Folate does not.

⚠️ Never give folate alone to a macrocytic anemia

The anemia of B12 deficiency will improve on folate — but folate has no effect on the neurologic manifestations. You correct the blood count, everyone relaxes, the patient looks better, and the subacute combined degeneration keeps progressing toward irreversibility. Always check a B12 level before treating a megaloblastic anemia with folate.

Q: A 62-year-old woman has hemoglobin 9.4 g/dL, MCV 112 fL, hypersegmented neutrophils, elevated homocysteine and a normal methylmalonic acid. What is the deficiency, and what must you check before treating?

Answer

Folate deficiency. Homocysteine is raised in both deficiencies, but the normal methylmalonic acid excludes B12. Before starting folic acid you must still check a B12 level, because a coexisting B12 deficiency would be masked hematologically by folate replacement while the neurologic degeneration continued unopposed.

Q: Why is the reticulocyte count LOW in megaloblastic anemia even though the bone marrow is HYPERCELLULAR?

Answer

Ineffective erythropoiesis. Precursors are produced in large numbers — hence the hypercellular marrow — but they cannot complete nuclear division and are destroyed within the marrow before release. Few mature cells reach the blood, so the reticulocyte count is low, and the intramedullary cell death releases lactate dehydrogenase and indirect bilirubin.

Q (case): A 61-year-old man with fatigue, mild confusion, pallor and paresthesias has hemoglobin 6.3 g/dL, MCV 115 fL, B12 140 pg/mL, elevated LDH, low reticulocytes and hypersegmented neutrophils on smear. Diagnosis, the two supporting findings worth reading properly, and management?

Answer

Megaloblastic anemia from B12 deficiency, most likely pernicious anemia. Macrocytosis with hypersegmented neutrophils means defective DNA synthesis; the low B12 names which one. The paresthesias and confusion are the tell that this is B12 and not folate.

Two supporting findings: the elevated LDH with a low reticulocyte count is the signature of ineffective erythropoiesis; and a prior gastric history points at loss of intrinsic factor. Management: intramuscular B12, which bypasses the need for intrinsic factor; confirm with anti-parietal cell and anti-intrinsic factor antibodies; and remember the associated gastric carcinoma risk requires ongoing surveillance.

Section 6 · Anemia I · impaired production

Anemia of Chronic Disease (Anemia of Inflammation)

The most common cause of anemia among hospitalized patients in the United States — and the one that most often gets mistaken for iron deficiency and treated with iron that does nothing.

🔑 What it is
  • Impaired red cell production associated with chronic disease. Also called anemia of inflammation — the newer term, covering both anemia of chronic disease and anemia of critical illness.
  • The most common cause of anemia among hospitalized patients in the United States.
  • Associated with defective iron utilization, and therefore mimics iron deficiency.
  • A diagnosis of exclusion. Can appear as early as 1–2 months after disease onset.
CategoryClassic examples
Chronic infectionsOsteomyelitis, lung abscess, endocarditis

🧠 What every entry on that list has in common

One word: inflammation. Not the infection, not the tumour, not the joint — the cytokine response to all three. That is why a single mechanism covers such a scattered list of diseases.

Four mechanisms, one stimulus ★ HY

A

Hepcidin goes up

Interleukin-6 drives hepatic hepcidin synthesis. Hepcidin inhibits iron release from macrophages and enterocytes, so recycled and dietary iron is trapped. Serum iron falls even though total body iron is normal or high.

B

Erythropoietin release is inhibited

Cytokines blunt erythropoietin release from the kidney — the order is never given loudly enough.

C

Erythroid proliferation is inhibited

Cytokines act directly on erythroid precursors to suppress proliferation, independent of iron and erythropoietin.

D

Hemophagocytosis is augmented

Activated macrophages of the reticuloendothelial system begin consuming red cells — something they should not be doing.

🪝 Memory hook — hepcidin is the doorman who locks the exits

Ferroportin is the only door iron has out of a gut cell or a macrophage. Hepcidin destroys ferroportin. High hepcidin means the doors are locked — the iron is in the building, it just cannot get to the marrow.

That one image explains the whole laboratory picture and the treatment failure: ferritin high (iron is stored, and ferritin is also an acute phase reactant), serum iron low (none is circulating), and oral iron does not work — you are shoving more iron at a locked door. It is also why intravenous iron does work: it enters past the locked door.

⚠️ The two tests that actually separate them

Serum iron is low in both, so it discriminates nothing. The pair that does the work is ferritin and TIBC, and they move in opposite directions:

  • Iron deficiency: ferritin LOW, TIBC HIGH. Empty tank, extra empty cups.
  • Anemia of chronic disease: ferritin HIGH, TIBC LOW. Full tank, locked doors, fewer cups.

And the confounder that makes this hard in real patients: ferritin is an acute phase reactant, so a genuinely iron-deficient patient who is also inflamed can show a normal ferritin. When the picture is mixed, the transferrin saturation and a marrow iron stain settle it.

🩺 Treatment — mostly, treat something else
  • Treat the underlying disease. Control the rheumatoid arthritis, drain the abscess, treat the cancer. This is the whole management strategy, and it is worth saying plainly because the instinct to “treat the number” is strong.
  • Erythropoiesis-stimulating agents may be considered if the patient is symptomatic — but note they are not FDA-approved for this indication, and there are real safety concerns (Section 16).
  • Optimize the diet, especially in older patients with concurrent nutritional deficiencies.
  • Oral iron generally does not help, because the problem is not iron supply — it is iron release.

Q: A 71-year-old with long-standing rheumatoid arthritis has hemoglobin 9.6 g/dL, MCV 86 fL, low serum iron, ferritin 340 ng/mL, low TIBC and a low reticulocyte count. Would oral iron help? Why or why not?

Answer

No. This is anemia of chronic disease. Inflammatory cytokines — chiefly interleukin-6 — raise hepcidin, which degrades ferroportin and blocks iron export from macrophages and enterocytes. Total body iron is adequate (high ferritin, low TIBC, increased marrow macrophage iron); it simply cannot be released to erythroid precursors. Adding oral iron does not open that door. The treatment is control of the underlying rheumatoid arthritis, with an erythropoiesis-stimulating agent considered only if she is symptomatic.

Hemolysis — Intravascular versus Extravascular

One question decides the entire laboratory workup: where was the red cell destroyed? Inside the blood vessel, or outside it. The two produce genuinely different laboratory signatures, and one of them can kill a kidney.

Abbreviations used in this section RES reticuloendothelial system — the macrophage network of spleen, liver and marrow · LDH lactate dehydrogenase — an intracellular enzyme released when cells are destroyed · DAT direct antiglobulin test, the direct Coombs test · Haptoglobin the plasma protein that binds free hemoglobin · Hemopexin a second scavenger, which binds free heme rather than intact hemoglobin
🔑 All hemolytic anemias share three features
  1. Shortening of red cell lifespan. The defining event — cells destroyed before their 120 days are up.
  2. Accumulation of the products of hemoglobin catabolism. Chiefly unconjugated (indirect) bilirubin, because it has not yet reached the liver. This is what makes patients jaundiced.
  3. Compensatory increase in erythropoiesis. The marrow senses the loss and turns the factory up. This is why the reticulocyte count is HIGH and the marrow hypercellular — the single cleanest split from everything in Sections 3–6.
🩸

Intravascular — destroyed inside the vessel

The cell ruptures in the circulation, spilling free hemoglobin directly into plasma.

  • Schistocytes (sometimes) — fragmented red cells
  • Hemoglobinemia — spun plasma looks pink or red instead of yellow
  • Hemoglobinuria — dark or tea-coloured urine
  • Jaundice from unconjugated hyperbilirubinemia
  • Decreased or ABSENT haptoglobin — the most sensitive marker
🫀

Extravascular — destroyed outside the vessel

The cell is removed intact by a splenic macrophage, which dismantles the hemoglobin internally. Nothing free is released into plasma.

  • Spherocytes — from partial membrane removal
  • Haptoglobin decreased, but only mildly
  • Jaundice from unconjugated hyperbilirubinemia
  • Splenomegaly — the spleen enlarges from the work
  • No hemoglobinemia or hemoglobinuria
🧠 Haptoglobin — nature's mop

Haptoglobin is a plasma protein whose only job is to bind free hemoglobin released after intravascular hemolysis. The complex is cleared by the reticuloendothelial system, and because haptoglobin is consumed in the process, the serum level falls.

Free heme in plasma is so dangerous that evolution built a dedicated scavenger for it. A low or absent haptoglobin means the mop has been used. It is a consumption marker, not a production marker — which is why it falls further in intravascular hemolysis, where there is far more free hemoglobin to mop up.

⚠️ Why intravascular hemolysis is the more dangerous one

Hemoglobin is essential — inside a red cell. Free in the plasma it is toxic, and specifically toxic to the kidney: filtered free heme causes tubular injury and acute kidney injury. This is the mechanism behind deaths from ABO-incompatible transfusion in Section 13. Extravascular hemolysis is bad; intravascular hemolysis is worse.

🪝 Memory hook — pink plasma, dark urine, no haptoglobin

The intravascular triad, callable at the bench. Spin a tube: yellow plasma normal, pink plasma = free hemoglobin. Look at the urine: dark or tea-coloured = hemoglobinuria. Check the haptoglobin: it will be absent, not merely low. Extravascular hemolysis gives you none of those three — it gives you spherocytes and a big spleen instead.

🧠 The one organ that appears in every disease in Sections 8–10

The spleen is the cleaner. It patrols for red cells that are the wrong shape, the wrong stiffness, or coated in something they should not be, and it removes them. It is doing its job correctly in every disease below — the problem is that the body keeps producing defective cells for it to find.

  • Spherocytosis — the cell is a ball instead of a disc → removed.
  • G6PD deficiency — oxidized hemoglobin precipitates as a Heinz body → the spleen bites the inclusion out.
  • Thalassemia — unpaired globin chains precipitate → removed.
  • Sickle cell disease — the cell is rigid and misshapen → removed, until the spleen itself infarcts.

Three consequences follow everywhere: splenomegaly, jaundice, and pigmented gallstones from the chronic bilirubin load.

Q: A patient's spun plasma is pink, the urine dipstick is positive for blood but microscopy shows no red cells, and haptoglobin is undetectable. What kind of hemolysis is this, and what organ is at immediate risk?

Answer

Intravascular hemolysis. Pink plasma is hemoglobinemia; a dipstick positive for blood with no red cells on microscopy is hemoglobinuria — the dipstick detects heme, not intact cells. Undetectable haptoglobin means the scavenger has been completely consumed. The organ at immediate risk is the kidney: filtered free heme causes tubular injury and acute kidney injury.

Q: Why is the reticulocyte count HIGH in hemolytic anemia but LOW in iron deficiency, B12 deficiency and anemia of chronic disease?

Answer

Because the marrow is intact in hemolysis. Cells are destroyed peripherally, the marrow detects the loss and works harder, so it releases more immature cells. In the impaired-production anemias the marrow itself is the problem — missing raw material, or suppressed by inflammation — so it cannot respond and the reticulocyte count stays low. One cheap test splits the classification tree in half.

Hereditary Spherocytosis and G6PD Deficiency

The first two inherited red cell defects. One breaks the container; the other breaks the defences.

Abbreviations used in this section HS hereditary spherocytosis · MCHC mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration — hemoglobin per unit cell volume · G6PD glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase — a red cell enzyme protecting against oxidant damage · NADPH nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate, reduced form — the reducing power G6PD generates · Coombs / DAT the direct antiglobulin test · WHO World Health Organization

Hereditary spherocytosis — a broken cytoskeleton

🔑 The genetics and the defect
  • Autosomal dominant.
  • An intrinsic defect in the red cell membrane that renders erythrocytes spheroidal rather than biconcave.
  • The proteins are cytoskeletal: ankyrin, band 3, β-spectrin, α-spectrin or protein 4.2.
  • Genes on testing: ANK1, SPTB, SLC4A1.
  • Almost always diagnosed in childhood; these patients keep a hematologist for life.
🧠 Why losing the scaffold costs the cell its life

The biconcave disc is held by an internal protein lattice clipped to the inner membrane. When one of those proteins is defective the lattice cannot maintain the shape and the cell rounds up into a sphere — it stops being a doughnut and becomes a baseball.

The consequence is mechanical. A sphere has the smallest possible surface area for its volume, so it is far less deformable. A normal red cell folds through capillaries and through the narrow slits of the splenic cords; a spherocyte cannot. It gets stuck, the spleen identifies it as abnormal, and removes it. This is extravascular hemolysis by definition.

🪝 Memory hook — Anemia · Jaundice · Big spleen · Gallstones

The chronic extravascular quartet — the signature of any long-standing extravascular hemolysis, not just spherocytosis. A young patient with lifelong intermittent jaundice, an enlarged spleen and pigmented gallstones at an unusually young age is being shown chronic hemolysis and asked to name the mechanism. A teenager with pigmented gallstones almost never has ordinary gallbladder disease.

The two crises

🔥

Hemolytic crisis

Often elicited by infection — destruction accelerates past what the marrow can match.

  • Significant hemolysis
  • Fever
  • Abdominal pain
  • Worsening jaundice
  • Shock in the worst events

Reticulocytes HIGH — the marrow is driven harder.

🛑

Aplastic crisis

Suppression of red cell production, usually triggered by parvovirus B19, which infects erythroid precursors and shuts erythropoiesis down.

A healthy person tolerates a brief pause in red cell production. Someone whose survival depends on a marrow running at maximum does not — the foundation is cut out from under the compensatory drive.

Reticulocytes LOW — the discriminating value.

⚠️ Parvovirus B19 appears twice in this block ★ HY

In hereditary spherocytosis, and again in sickle cell disease. Same mechanism each time: it infects red cell precursors and stops erythropoiesis. A sudden crash in hemoglobin with a LOW reticulocyte count in a chronic hemolytic patient is an aplastic crisis until proven otherwise.

🩺 The osmotic fragility test — old-fashioned, but the logic is the disease

Red cells are exposed to hypotonic saline; water enters and the cell swells. A normal biconcave disc has spare membrane relative to its volume and can swell substantially into a sphere before bursting. A spherocyte is already a sphere — it has no spare surface area at all, so a small amount of swelling ruptures it. Result: increased osmotic fragility.

⚠️ Spherocytes are not specific — always send a Coombs test ★ HY

Spherocytes appear in two quite different diseases: hereditary spherocytosis and warm autoimmune hemolytic anemia, where antibody-coated cells have membrane bitten away by splenic macrophages. The direct Coombs test separates them — negative in hereditary spherocytosis, positive in autoimmune hemolysis. (The indirect Coombs test, its photo negative, is the antibody screen of Section 12.)

G6PD deficiency — when the antioxidant defence runs out

🔑 The defect is instability, not absence

The enzyme is synthesized in normal amounts. But the genetic variant makes it unstable, with a short half-life, so activity decays as the cell ages. The consequence: marked deficiency in OLDER red cells while younger cells are still protected.

That one fact explains the entire behaviour of the disease — why hemolysis is episodic, why it is self-limited, and why the diagnostic test can be falsely normal. Inheritance is X-linked, so it is expressed most fully in males.

  1. G6PD makes NADPH. In a red cell, this is the only meaningful source of NADPH.
  2. NADPH regenerates glutathione — it powers glutathione reductase, converting oxidized glutathione back to the reduced form.
  3. Reduced glutathione neutralizes peroxide — converting hydrogen peroxide to water. The actual detoxifying step.
  4. Without the chain, peroxide accumulates and oxidizes hemoglobin, which denatures and precipitates inside the cell as a Heinz body.
🪝 Memory hook — a red cell has no nucleus, no mitochondria, and one antioxidant plan

Every other cell has alternative routes to NADPH and can make replacement protein when an enzyme wears out. A red cell can do neither — it ejected its nucleus and mitochondria on the way out of the marrow. G6PD is the whole plan. No backup and no way to build more, which is why the enzyme's instability over the cell's lifetime is the disease.

What the spleen does about it

  1. Hemoglobin is oxidized and precipitates as an inclusion — a Heinz body — stuck to the inner membrane. Heinz bodies need a supravital stain (crystal violet); they do not show on a routine smear.
  2. The spleen finds it. A splenic macrophage detects the abnormal inclusion and takes a bite out of it.
  3. Bite cells and blister cells result. The macrophage removes the inclusion plus the overlying membrane, leaving a bite cell — a semicircular chunk missing. A blister cell is the same event with the membrane still stretched over the defect.
  4. Less damaged cells become spherocytes — which is why spherocytes are not unique to hereditary spherocytosis.

Viral hepatitis, pneumonia, typhoid fever. In practice this is the commonest trigger of all — and it is why a hemolytic episode is so often mistaken for the infection worsening.

They generate oxidants when metabolized — favism. Fava beans are endemic in the Mediterranean, the Middle East and parts of Africa, exactly where the deficiency is common.

🧬 The malaria hypothesis — and the pattern to notice

G6PD deficiency is the world's most common enzyme deficiency, affecting over 400 million people, concentrated in the tropical and subtropical Eastern Hemisphere. The distribution tracks populations historically exposed to endemic malaria — the deficiency provides a protective advantage against severe Plasmodium falciparum, though it is not fully protective.

The same bargain recurs with sickle cell trait in Section 9: a mutation advantageous in the heterozygous state under malaria pressure becomes a disease when you inherit too much of it. Note the caution — the geography reflects where genes are common, not a reason to reason about individual patients by ancestry.

⚠️ The enzyme assay can be falsely NORMAL during an acute crisis ★ HY

This follows directly from the mechanism. During hemolysis the oldest, most enzyme-depleted cells are exactly the ones being destroyed, and the marrow floods the blood with reticulocytes, which have the highest enzyme levels of any red cell. Measure the average at that moment and it can look normal.

Repeat the assay two to three months after the episode, once the age distribution has returned to baseline. A genetic test is unaffected by timing.

Q: A 9-year-old with known hereditary spherocytosis presents pale and lethargic five days into a viral illness. Hemoglobin has fallen from her baseline 10.5 to 4.2 g/dL. Reticulocyte count 0.2%. What has happened?

Answer

An aplastic crisis, almost certainly from parvovirus B19. The discriminating value is the low reticulocyte count — in a hemolytic crisis the marrow would be driven harder and reticulocytes would be high; here erythropoiesis has been shut off at the precursor level. Because her survival depends on a permanently maximal marrow, losing that compensation for a few days causes a precipitous drop. Management is transfusion and supportive care until marrow function recovers.

Q: A 6-year-old boy of Greek ancestry develops jaundice, dark urine and pallor two days after eating fava beans. Hemoglobin 6.8 g/dL. A G6PD assay sent during the admission returns normal. Diagnosis, and why was the test normal?

Answer

G6PD deficiency — almost certainly the Mediterranean variant (WHO class II), given his ancestry and the severity. The assay was falsely normal because it was drawn during the crisis: the old, enzyme-depleted cells had already been destroyed, and the compensatory reticulocytosis flooded the sample with young cells carrying the highest enzyme levels. Repeat it two to three months later, or send a genetic test. The smear during the episode would show bite cells and blister cells.

Q (case): A 13-year-old girl has six months of weakness and jaundice, lifelong episodic jaundice, and a father who was jaundiced as a teenager. Spleen 6 cm below the costal margin. Hemoglobin 9, reticulocytes 11.45%, MCHC 40%, spherocytes on smear, osmotic fragility markedly increased, Coombs negative, indirect bilirubin 3.4. Diagnosis, the value that excludes the alternative, and management?

Answer

Hereditary spherocytosis. The picture is chronic extravascular hemolysis: spherocytes, unconjugated hyperbilirubinemia, splenomegaly, high reticulocytes and marrow erythroid hyperplasia. The autosomal dominant inheritance shows in the father's adolescent jaundice. Increased osmotic fragility confirms it, and the high MCHC fits.

The value that excludes the alternative is the NEGATIVE Coombs test — warm autoimmune hemolytic anemia also produces spherocytes, jaundice and splenomegaly, but its cells are antibody-coated and the Coombs would be positive.

Management: folic acid to support the high-turnover marrow, and splenectomy — removing the site of destruction normalizes red cell lifespan and removes the rupture risk of a spleen that size. Vaccinate against the encapsulated organisms first (Section 10).

Thalassemia and Sickle Cell Disease — the Globin Disorders

Thalassemia is a quantity problem: structurally normal globin, not enough of one kind. Sickle cell disease is a quality problem: the right amount of beta globin, and one amino acid in it is wrong.

Abbreviations used in this section HbA normal adult hemoglobin, α₂β₂ · HbF fetal hemoglobin, α₂γ₂ · HbS sickle hemoglobin · HbH the β₄ tetramer of severe alpha thalassemia · Hb Barts the γ₄ tetramer of hydrops fetalis · HbA₂ α₂δ₂ · HPLC high performance liquid chromatography — separates hemoglobins for identification · HU hydroxyurea · CRISPR/Cas9 a genome-editing technology · BCL11A the gene that switches fetal hemoglobin off after birth

Count the genes — the whole thalassemia framework ★ HY

🔑 The asymmetry that drives everything
  • Adult hemoglobin (HbA) is α₂β₂ — two alpha chains, two beta chains.
  • You have FOUR alpha globin genes (two on each copy of chromosome 16).
  • You have TWO beta globin genes (one on each copy of chromosome 11).

Which is easier to be severely affected by? You only have two betas — lose one and you are in trouble. You have four alphas — losing one barely registers. That single asymmetry is why alpha thalassemia has four clinical grades and beta thalassemia essentially two.

🧠 The globin switch — why a fetus needs a different hemoglobin

A fetus gets its oxygen from its mother across the placenta, and the mother has ordinary adult hemoglobin. For the fetus to pull oxygen off maternal hemoglobin, fetal hemoglobin must have a higher affinity for oxygen than adult hemoglobin.

  • Prenatal: gamma (γ) chains pair with alpha to make HbF = α₂γ₂ — F for fetus.
  • After birth: the globin switch — gamma production falls off and beta takes over, giving HbA = α₂β₂.

This matters three separate times: it explains why beta thalassemia major does not present at birth, it explains the two abnormal tetramers below, and it explains why hydroxyurea works in sickle cell disease by turning HbF back on.

The mechanism of damage

  1. Not enough hemoglobin → hypochromia. Reduced globin synthesis means low intracellular hemoglobin, showing as hypochromia and microcytosis — the same appearance as iron deficiency, from a completely different cause.
  2. Relative excess of the unpartnered chain → destruction. If beta chains are deficient you have a relative excess of normal alpha chains with nothing to pair with. They aggregate into insoluble complexes inside the cell and its precursors.
  3. Precursors die in the marrow. Those aggregates destroy developing cells before release — ineffective hematopoiesis. This is why the marrow is hypercellular while the patient is profoundly anemic.
  4. Mature cells are destroyed in the spleen. The ones that make it out carry inclusions the spleen finds and removes — hemolysis. Hence the classification under increased destruction.
🪝 Memory hook — the unpaired partner is the poison

The intuitive assumption is that thalassemia hurts because you make too little hemoglobin. That is only the milder half. The severity tracks the chain you have too MUCH of. Unpartnered globin chains precipitate, damage membranes and kill cells in the marrow and the spleen.

This is also why beta thalassemia is worse than alpha thalassemia of comparable gene loss: free alpha chains are far more unstable and damaging than free beta chains, which can at least assemble into HbH.

A fetus and newborn run on HbF (α₂γ₂), which does not require beta chains at all. So a baby with beta thalassemia major is born completely well. Only as gamma production falls off and beta is supposed to take over — around 3 to 6 months — does the anemia appear.

⚠️ Iron overload — treating the treatment

Transfused blood carries iron, and the human body has no physiologic mechanism to excrete iron. A patient transfused every two to four weeks for life accumulates it relentlessly, damaging heart, liver and endocrine organs. So they must take iron chelation therapy — pills which treat you for the treatment. Chelator pharmacology is in Section 16.

Note the irony that closes the loop with Section 4: this is the one anemia where you must remove iron rather than give it.

Sickle cell disease — one amino acid ★ HY

🔑 The mutation
  • A point mutation in the beta globin chain: a single adenine-to-thymine substitution changing the 6th codon of the mature protein.
  • The result is valine substituted for glutamic acid at that position.
  • The product is hemoglobin S (HbS), with abnormal physicochemical properties.
  • The gene is HBB — the same gene as beta thalassemia, a different kind of mutation.
🧠 Why one hydrophobic residue does this much damage

Glutamic acid is charged and water-loving; valine is hydrophobic. Putting a hydrophobic patch on the outside of a hemoglobin molecule gives it a sticky spot, and hydrophobic patches find each other.

  1. Oxygenated, nothing happens. With oxygen bound, the conformation keeps the sticky patch tucked away.
  2. Deoxygenated, it polymerizes. On releasing oxygen the conformation shifts, the valine is exposed, and HbS molecules polymerize into long rigid fibers.
  3. The fibers deform the cell into the sickle shape and, critically, make it inflexible.
  4. Inflexible cells get stuck in the microvasculature → ischemia, tissue damage and pain.

Pathophysiology — one polymerization, three arms

1

Vaso-occlusion

Sickled cells lodge in the microcirculation → hypoxia, infarction and necrosis of vital organs plus severe pain. Produces acute pain, hyposplenism, osteonecrosis, nephropathy and acute chest syndrome.

2

Vasculopathy and endothelial dysfunction

The disturbed vessel becomes inflamed and the blood becomes hypercoagulable — little clogs, inflammation, and a clotting system that no longer knows what it is doing.

3

Hemolysis

Cells are torn apart under arterial pressure. Free hemoglobin inactivates nitric oxide and increases reactive oxygen species → pulmonary hypertension, leg ulcers, cerebrovascular disease. Raised bilirubin → gallstones and jaundice.

The three crises ★ HY

⚡

Vaso-occlusive (painful) crisis

Repeated hypoxic injury and infarction, often with infection, dehydration or acidosis. Extreme pain in the abdomen, joints or chest. In infants and toddlers it presents as dactylitis — painful swelling of hands and feet.

Hemoglobin often near baseline.

🛑

Aplastic crisis

Parvovirus B19 infecting erythroid precursors → cessation of erythropoiesis. Same mechanism as in hereditary spherocytosis.

Reticulocytes LOW.

🫃

Sequestration crisis

Massive sequestration of deformed red cells in the spleen of children with splenomegaly. The spleen abruptly swallows a large fraction of the circulating volume → rapid fall in hemoglobin and hypovolemic shock.

Reticulocytes HIGH, spleen suddenly ENORMOUS.

🪝 Memory hook — three crises, three reticulocyte counts

All three look similar at the bedside — a sickle cell patient suddenly much sicker and much more anemic. The reticulocyte count separates them fastest. Aplastic → LOW (production stopped, think parvovirus). Sequestration → HIGH plus a huge spleen (the cells are alive, just not in the circulation). Vaso-occlusive → hemoglobin near baseline; the problem is pain and ischemia, not red cell mass.

Three variables control how much sickling happens

  1. The amount of HbS, and what it can partner with. HbS polymerizes well with itself and poorly with HbF and HbA. This is the single most important therapeutic lever — dilute the HbS with anything else and polymerization falls off sharply.
  2. Hemoglobin concentration. The higher the intracellular HbS concentration, the more likely polymerization. Dehydration concentrates the cell and makes sickling worse.
  3. pH — acidosis. Acidosis reduces hemoglobin's oxygen affinity (the Bohr effect), enhancing deoxygenation — and deoxygenated HbS is the form that polymerizes. Acidosis increases sickling.
🧠 Why every treatment is really the same idea

Nearly all of them attack the same variable — the proportion and concentration of HbS:

  • Hydroxyurea raises HbF, and HbS polymerizes poorly with HbF. You are diluting the polymer.
  • Transfusion adds HbA — the same idea from the other direction.
  • Intravenous fluids lower hemoglobin concentration inside the cell.
  • Oxygen keeps hemoglobin in the oxygenated conformation, which does not polymerize.
  • Gene therapy either switches HbF back on (Casgevy) or inserts a working beta gene (Lyfgenia).

The number that anchors it: randomized trial evidence shows that keeping HbS below 30% significantly decreases the risk of stroke.

🩺 Exchange transfusion — the arithmetic problem

If a patient's HbS is 80% and you want it under 30%, you cannot simply transfuse more blood — you would need an impossible volume and would make them dangerously polycythemic. So you perform an exchange transfusion: remove some of the patient's blood while giving donor blood, which nets a lower HbS percentage without raising total blood volume. Effective, and a lifelong commitment.

Q: A 2-year-old has a microcytic anemia with an MCV of 65 fL. Ferritin 180 ng/mL, transferrin saturation normal, red cell count high for the degree of anemia. What should you NOT do, and what test do you order?

Answer

Do not give iron. Completely normal iron studies with a disproportionately small MCV and a high red cell count point to thalassemia, not iron deficiency — and giving iron achieves nothing except iron overload, the very thing you spend the rest of their care chelating out. Order hemoglobin electrophoresis or HPLC, which will show an enlarged HbF band and raised HbA₂ in beta thalassemia.

Q: Explain why hydrops fetalis is incompatible with life while hemoglobin H disease is survivable, even though both are alpha thalassemias.

Answer

It is about which tetramer forms and how it behaves. In hemoglobin H disease (one alpha gene) excess beta chains form HbH (β₄), which is unstable and precipitates — a moderately severe hemolytic anemia, but the patient still has some normal HbA and can deliver oxygen. In hydrops fetalis (no alpha genes) no alpha-containing hemoglobin can be made at all; excess gamma chains form Hb Barts (γ₄), which has extremely high oxygen affinity — it binds oxygen and will not release it to tissue. The fetus is profoundly anoxic despite being full of hemoglobin.

Q: Why does hydroxyurea help in sickle cell disease, and what is its principal toxicity?

Answer

It increases fetal hemoglobin (HbF). Because HbS polymerizes well with itself but poorly with HbF, raising the HbF fraction dilutes the polymer and reduces sickling, crises and organ damage. The mechanism is thought to involve a cytotoxic hit to the marrow that triggers stress erythropoiesis favouring HbF — and that same cytotoxicity is the principal toxicity: myelosuppression. It is also carcinogenic. Those are its two boxed warnings.

Q: A hemoglobin electrophoresis shows bands at both the HbA and HbS positions, roughly 55% and 43%. Does this patient have sickle cell disease?

Answer

No — this is sickle cell trait. In sickle cell disease the patient is homozygous, so 90–100% of hemoglobin is HbS and there is no HbA band at all. In trait, HbS makes up 40–45% with the remainder HbA, exactly as shown. Trait is generally asymptomatic — and confers up to a 30% reduction in malaria mortality, which is why the mutation persists.

Q (case): A 2-year-old boy has progressive pallor and failure to thrive. Born full-term and well; pallor began around 6 months. Transfusion-dependent every 4–6 weeks since 12 months. Frontal bossing and maxillary prominence; liver 3 cm and spleen 6 cm below the costal margin. Diagnosis, and explain the age of onset and the facial findings.

Answer

Beta thalassemia major.

Age of onset: a fetus and newborn run on HbF (α₂γ₂), which does not require beta chains, so he was genuinely well. Only as the globin switch turned gamma off and beta was supposed to take over — at 3 to 6 months — did the deficiency appear. The electrophoresis shows the consequence: reduced HbA with elevated HbF and HbA₂, because he never successfully made the switch.

Facial findings: frontal bossing and maxillary prominence come from massive expansion of the marrow cavities in the skull and face, as ineffective erythropoiesis drives the marrow to expand into any available space. Failure to thrive follows from chronic anemia plus the enormous metabolic cost of that drive.

Management: regular packed red cell transfusion every 3–4 weeks; iron chelation with deferasirox — essential, because there is no physiologic route to excrete transfusional iron; folic acid; genetic counseling (each pregnancy carries a 25% risk); and evaluation for stem cell transplantation or gene therapy.

Q (case): A 1-year-old boy has 3 days of fever to 38.9 °C, poor feeding, six months of intermittent joint swelling and abdominal pain, and two prior admissions for anemia and pneumonia. Sickle cell disease. HbS 85% with HbA 0% means he is homozygous — this is disease, not trait (trait would show roughly 40–45% HbS alongside HbA). Section 10 · Anemia II · the spleen

Asplenia, Post-Splenectomy Sepsis, and Hypersplenism

Two opposite problems with one organ: a spleen that works too hard, and no spleen at all.

Post-splenectomy sepsis — the complication and its prevention ★ HY

🔑 Why an absent spleen is dangerous

The spleen is the body's principal filter for encapsulated organisms. Without it, opsonized encapsulated bacteria are not cleared, and a bloodstream infection can progress to overwhelming sepsis within hours rather than days. The organisms that matter:

  • Streptococcus pneumoniae — the dominant risk, by a wide margin.
  • Neisseria meningitidis.
  • Haemophilus influenzae type b.
🩺 Prevention — the three-part answer
  1. Vaccinate, and vaccinate before the splenectomy where possible. Pneumococcal, meningococcal and Haemophilus influenzae type b. In a planned splenectomy this is done in advance; when a spleen is removed emergently it is done afterward.
  2. Antibiotic prophylaxis in children. Daily penicillin — the standard of care in pediatric sickle cell disease, where the spleen is functionally gone by the first years of life.
  3. Educate the patient and the family. Any fever is an emergency requiring immediate evaluation and empiric antibiotics without waiting for cultures. This is the intervention that actually saves lives, because the window is measured in hours.
🧠 Functional asplenia is the same problem without the surgery

You do not need a surgeon to be asplenic. In sickle cell disease, repeated splenic infarction destroys the organ over the first years of life — autosplenectomy — and the infection risk is identical. The smear tells you it has happened: Howell-Jolly bodies, retained nuclear DNA fragments that a working spleen would have removed. Their presence means the spleen is absent or non-functional, which is why they and the penicillin prophylaxis go together.

🩺 When splenectomy is nonetheless the right answer

In hereditary spherocytosis the spleen is the site of destruction, not the site of the defect. The cells remain spherocytic afterward — you can still see them on the smear — but without the splenic cords to trap and remove them, their lifespan approaches normal and the anemia resolves. It also removes the risk of splenic rupture from chronic enlargement, which can be fatal because an enlarged spleen is essentially a bag of blood. The permanent cost is the lifelong susceptibility above.

Splenectomy is also used in sickle cell disease for recurrent sequestration crises or massive splenomegaly.

Hypersplenism — the opposite problem

🧠 Secondary hypersplenism as a cause of hemolysis

Hypersplenism sits on the classification tree of Section 3 as an extrinsic cause of increased red cell destruction — alongside antibody-mediated destruction and mechanical trauma. The cells themselves are normal; an enlarged, overactive spleen simply removes too many of them.

Because the spleen sequesters all three lineages, the clinical presentation is splenomegaly with cytopenias — anemia, leukopenia and thrombocytopenia in some combination — alongside whatever caused the splenomegaly in the first place. The chronic hemolytic anemias in this section are themselves a cause: a spleen working constantly against defective cells enlarges, and an enlarged spleen then removes cells more indiscriminately.

⚠️ Scope note

The full etiologic list for secondary hypersplenism — portal hypertension, infiltrative and storage diseases, hematologic malignancy — belongs to lectures we do not have notes for. What is here is the hemolysis mechanism and the asplenia consequences. Study guide objective 88 is marked partial in the coverage map for that reason.

The Four Blood Products, Their Indications, and the Trials That Moved Every Threshold Down

Blood as a drug — the one drug that cannot be manufactured. Everything in a blood bank refrigerator started as one unit of whole blood from one volunteer.

🔑 The split that organizes the whole field

The red product is given to carry oxygen. Every yellow product is given for hemostasis. That single distinction sorts the indications — and it is why a bleeding patient needs more than red cells: when you bleed you lose whole blood, so you must replace the coagulation factors and platelets too.

What is actually in the bag

Citrate anticoagulant

in every bag

Additive solution

“red cell food”

Adenine, mannitol and dextrose — fuel for the red cell's ATP pumps. This is what extends storage to 42 days.

Breathable plastic

the bag itself

The cells in the bag are alive and need oxygen, so the plastic is formulated to allow gas exchange.

a filter, before bagging

White cells are the main source of the cytokines causing febrile reactions and of the HLA exposure causing alloimmunization. Standard of care on essentially every US product.

Pathogen reduction

platelets

Inactivates residual bacteria, viruses and parasites — implemented specifically to reduce the risk from bacterially contaminated platelets.

Prevents donor lymphocytes proliferating in the recipient — the only defence against transfusion-associated graft-versus-host disease.

⚠️ Platelets are the exception to everything

Every other product is kept cold. Platelets cannot be — cold activates them and they are cleared rapidly after transfusion. So they are stored at room temperature on a continuously agitating rotator.

Room temperature storage has a direct consequence: any bacteria introduced during collection can grow. That is why the most common infectious risk of transfusion is a bacterially contaminated platelet, and why the shelf life is only 5–7 days rather than 42.

🧠 Cryoprecipitate — the Thanksgiving turkey

You buy a turkey frozen and thaw it in the refrigerator, not on the counter. Do the same with a unit of frozen plasma — thaw it at refrigerated temperature (1–6 °C) rather than at room temperature — and certain proteins fall out of solution as a precipitate.

That cold-insoluble precipitate is enriched in fibrinogen, and also carries factor VIII, von Willebrand factor, factor XIII and fibronectin. Why fibrinogen matters: the intrinsic and extrinsic pathways converge on the common pathway, and at the bottom is fibrinogen becoming fibrin — the protein that actually cross-links the clot.

🔑 Plasma-derived medicines — the category most students miss

There are drugs you will prescribe routinely that are made from human plasma donors and arrive from the pharmacy, not the blood bank: albumin, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), and coagulation factor concentrates including 3-factor and 4-factor prothrombin complex concentrates.

The difference from blood-bank plasma is the processing: manufacturers pool plasma from many donors, then heat and solvent-detergent treat it to inactivate pathogens, before purifying the protein of interest. So albumin has had a pathogen-inactivation step that a bag of thawed plasma has not.

Platelets — why the threshold is 10,000 ★ HY

🧠 Why platelets matter — the first phase of hemostasis

When a vessel wall is cut, the subendothelial matrix is exposed. The collagen in it is the stimulus that starts hemostasis, and the first cells to arrive are platelets. They adhere and spread over the exposed collagen — primary hemostasis. The coagulation factors then build a fibrin mesh over the top to hold it — secondary hemostasis. Platelets first, mesh second. That order is why platelet count and coagulation tests are treated as separate problems with separate products.

Treatment is directed at the immune process: corticosteroids, IVIG, thrombopoietin receptor agonists — with platelet transfusion reserved for serious active bleeding. Same logic as dengue. Plasma and cryoprecipitate — the ocean and the teacup ★ HY

🧠 The single number that governs plasma transfusion

You only need about 30% coagulation factor activity to form a clot. Above that line hemostasis is adequate; below it, it is not.

Now the metaphor: your circulating plasma is an ocean; a unit of transfused plasma is a teacup. If your ocean already has 50–60% factor activity, pouring in a teacup changes essentially nothing — and it did not need changing, because you could already clot. If your ocean is down at 10–15%, that same teacup actually moves the needle.

Translated into the number you will see: an INR of 1.1 is well above the threshold. A PT around 2 to 2.2 times normal is roughly where you reach 30%.

🔑 Indications for plasma — the whole list
  • A documented factor deficiency AND active bleeding. Both halves are required.
  • A markedly prolonged coagulation test in a patient about to undergo a procedure.
  • Warfarin reversal — but also give vitamin K, and consider prothrombin complex concentrate first (Section 14).
  • NOT for single factor deficiencies. If a patient is factor VIII deficient (hemophilia A), you give factor VIII, not plasma.
⚠️ The commonest plasma error

Someone sees an INR of 1.1, or 1.3, and orders plasma to “normalize” it. At that INR the patient is far above the 30% threshold and can already form a clot. The transfusion achieves nothing measurable while exposing them to a transfusion reaction, an infectious risk and a volume load. You are not treating the patient; you are treating the number.

Q: Why is citrate added to a unit of red cells, and what electrolyte problem can it cause during a massive transfusion?

Answer

Citrate is the anticoagulant. It works by chelating calcium, and calcium is the trigger for the coagulation cascade — without free calcium the unit cannot clot in the bag. During massive transfusion the recipient receives a large citrate load faster than the liver can metabolize it, so the citrate chelates the patient's calcium too, producing hypocalcemia — paresthesias, tetany, QT prolongation and impaired hemostasis. Monitor ionized calcium and replace it.

Q: A hemodynamically stable postoperative patient has hemoglobin 8.1 g/dL and is asymptomatic. The surgical resident orders two units “to help her heal and get her walking sooner”. Give two reasons this is wrong.

Answer

First, the indication is invalid. Red cells are given to increase oxygen-carrying capacity — explicitly not for wound healing or to shorten time to ambulation. Both were once believed and neither is supported.

Second, the threshold is wrong. TRICC randomized 838 euvolemic intensive care patients to restrictive (<7) versus liberal (<10) and found no mortality difference, with the liberal arm doing worse. At 8.1 g/dL in a stable, asymptomatic patient the correct action is no transfusion — and if one were indicated, one unit, then reassess, not two reflexively.

Q: Why was it important that the patients in the TRICC trial were euvolemic?

Answer

Because the trial was asking about anemia, not hemorrhage. An actively bleeding patient is losing whole blood — plasma volume, coagulation factors and platelets along with red cells — and needs volume resuscitation and the full range of products regardless of the hemoglobin, which in early bleeding can look deceptively normal. Restricting the trial to euvolemic patients isolated how anemic is too anemic from the separate question of how do you resuscitate someone who is bleeding out. The restrictive 7 g/dL threshold does not apply to active hemorrhage.

Q: A patient with disseminated intravascular coagulation after obstetric hemorrhage has a fibrinogen of 80 mg/dL. Why is plasma alone an inadequate answer?

Answer

Because plasma is not a concentrated source of fibrinogen. It contains fibrinogen at physiologic concentration, so correcting a profound deficiency would require a volume the patient cannot tolerate. Cryoprecipitate is the cold-insoluble fraction of thawed plasma, enriched in fibrinogen (along with factor VIII, von Willebrand factor and factor XIII), and delivers the same fibrinogen in a fraction of the volume. Since fibrinogen becoming fibrin is the step that actually cross-links the clot, replacing it is what allows the rest of the cascade to produce a stable clot.

Q: A patient needs plasma urgently. Your colleague suggests albumin instead, since both are “plasma products”. What is wrong with that?

Answer

They do completely different jobs. Plasma contains the coagulation, fibrinolytic and complement proteins and is given to correct a coagulation factor deficiency. Albumin is a single purified protein, manufactured from pooled plasma and pathogen-inactivated; it is a colloid volume expander and contains no coagulation factors at all. Giving albumin to a coagulopathic bleeding patient replaces volume while doing nothing for hemostasis.

If you are group A, you are an ocean of anti-B antibody. Give that person group B blood and the reaction is immediate.

🪝 Memory hook — front type and back type must agree

The blood bank types you twice, in opposite directions, as a built-in cross-check. Front type — test the patient's red cells for A and B antigen; this gives the phenotype. Back type — test the patient's plasma for anti-A and anti-B; this must be the mirror image.

Group A front-types as A and back-types with anti-B. If the two do not agree, something is wrong — a mislabeled sample, a recent transfusion, or an unusual subgroup — and the blood bank stops and investigates before releasing anything.

Two ways an antibody destroys transfused red cells ★ HY

💥

ABO incompatibility → INTRAVASCULAR hemolysis

  • ABO antibodies are IgM, and IgM is very good at activating complement.
  • IgM cross-links the incompatible cells and fixes complement.
  • Complement runs to completion and the cells are lysed inside the vessel.
  • Free heme spills into plasma and reaches the kidney — the mechanism of acute kidney injury and death.
  • Needs no prior exposure — the antibodies are naturally occurring and present in everyone.
🫧

Alloantibody (e.g. anti-D) → EXTRAVASCULAR hemolysis

  • Antibodies formed after exposure to foreign red cells are IgG.
  • IgG coats (opsonizes) the cell rather than lysing it in the vessel.
  • Tagged cells are cleared in the spleen.
  • Slower, less explosive — but still causes hemolysis and transfusion failure.
  • Requires prior exposure, so it can only follow transfusion or pregnancy.

⚠️ Why sickle cell disease patients become so hard to transfuse

Look at that last row. In a normal population, alloantibody formation is 1–3%. In sickle cell disease it is 20–60%.

The reason is simple exposure: these patients are transfused repeatedly, for life. We always match ABO — but they are being exposed over and over to minor antigens (Duffy, Kidd, Kell) that differ between them and the donor pool. Each exposure is a chance to form a new antibody, and each new antibody makes finding compatible units harder. This is why many centres now provide extended antigen-matched blood for chronically transfused patients rather than matching ABO and RhD alone.

RhD, alloimmunization, and hemolytic disease of the fetus and newborn

  1. A D-negative person receives D-positive blood. They lack the D antigen, so it is foreign. They may form anti-D.
  2. Nothing happens — this time. Forming the antibody alone causes no harm. This is called red cell sensitization, or alloimmunization.
  3. The next exposure is the problem. Give D-positive blood again and the anti-D now causes hemolysis. This is why the blood bank must detect existing antibodies before every transfusion.
  4. And in pregnancy it is worse. If a D-negative woman with anti-D carries a D-positive fetus, the IgG anti-D crosses the placenta and destroys fetal red cells — hemolytic disease of the fetus and newborn.
🔑 The rationing rule, and the arithmetic behind it

Only about 15% of the population is RhD negative, so RhD-negative units are relatively scarce. The blood bank therefore rations them:

  • Save RhD-negative units for females of childbearing potential. If a girl or woman forms anti-D, a future pregnancy with a D-positive fetus can be seriously harmed.
  • For male trauma patients, give RhD-positive when supply is tight. They will never carry a pregnancy, so the consequence of alloimmunization is far smaller.
  • Group O RhD-negative is only about 6% of people — roughly 1 in 15 — and it is the emergency-release product.

And the emergency reality: an unidentified trauma patient is given group O immediately, because they may die while you type them — O positive if male, saving the O negative.

  3. Add Coombs reagent — anti-IgG. IgG bound to a cell is too small to see; the anti-IgG reagent cross-links the coated cells, building clumps large enough to be visible.

That names the antibody.

Q: A group A patient is mistakenly transfused with group B red cells. Describe the immunology, and name the organ most at risk.

Answer

A group A person has naturally occurring anti-B, formed in infancy from gut bacterial colonization — no prior transfusion needed. That antibody is IgM, highly efficient at fixing complement. It cross-links the transfused B cells and activates complement to completion, lysing them inside the blood vessels — an acute intravascular hemolytic transfusion reaction.

The organ most at risk is the kidney: free heme released into the plasma is nephrotoxic, causing tubular injury and acute kidney injury. This reaction can be fatal and is classified as a never event.

Q: Why is a woman who is RhD negative treated differently from a man who is RhD negative when blood is scarce?

Answer

Because alloimmunization has a second, larger consequence in someone who may become pregnant. If a D-negative female of childbearing potential forms anti-D, that antibody is IgG and therefore crosses the placenta. In a future pregnancy with a D-positive fetus it will destroy fetal red cells — hemolytic disease of the fetus and newborn. A man cannot carry a pregnancy, so his risk is limited to difficulty with future transfusions. Since only 15% of the population is RhD negative, blood banks preserve scarce D-negative units for girls and women.

Q: A patient with a known anti-Kell antibody needs two units of red cells. Why does this take longer than usual, and what must the units be?

Answer

The units must be Kell antigen negative as well as ABO and RhD compatible, and antigen-negative units must be identified — often from a wider inventory or a reference laboratory — then individually crossmatched. That takes time.

The antibody was formed by previous transfusion or pregnancy, the only two routes of exposure to foreign red cells. It is IgG, so re-exposure would cause extravascular hemolysis with splenic clearance of the transfused cells. The requirement is lifelong: the titre can fall below detection but will rise rapidly on re-exposure.

Q: A patient develops anemia and jaundice. The direct antiglobulin test is positive. What does this tell you, and what does it NOT tell you?

Answer

It tells you the patient's red cells are coated with antibody — so the hemolysis is immune-mediated rather than due to an intrinsic membrane, enzyme or hemoglobin defect. That distinguishes it immediately from hereditary spherocytosis, G6PD deficiency, thalassemia and sickle cell disease.

It does not tell you why. A positive DAT is compatible with autoimmune hemolytic anemia (warm or cold), a delayed or acute hemolytic transfusion reaction, hemolytic disease of the fetus and newborn, or drug-induced immune hemolysis. It also does not tell you the antibody's specificity — that needs further work-up.

Reading each one properly

🤒

Febrile non-hemolytic — the common one

The product is living cells in a bag, and while they survive they are not entirely happy — they release cytokines. Give those cytokines to a patient and they can produce a fever. Self-limited, and as the name says, no hemolysis.

White cells are the main cytokine source, which is why leukocyte reduction drops the rate sharply. It is now standard of care for essentially every US product.

🦠

Septic transfusion reaction — the dangerous one

Bacterial contamination. The usual route is startling: when the donor's arm is accessed, the bore of the needle punches out a tiny plug of skin, and everyone's skin carries flora. That plug lands in the bag and, because platelets are stored at room temperature, the bacteria grow.

Three countermeasures: a diversion pouch that captures and discards the first ~100 mL of the donation, scrupulous arm preparation, and pathogen reduction.

⚠️ Acute hemolytic transfusion reaction — a “never event” ★ HY

The full intravascular hemolysis signature: hemoglobinemia (pink plasma), hemoglobinuria, and a positive direct Coombs test, with characteristic back and flank pain. Naturally occurring IgM ABO antibody fixes complement and lyses the transfused cells inside the vessels; free heme threatens the kidneys.

It is called a never event because the incompatibility is entirely predictable and entirely preventable.

How it actually happens: “wrong blood in tube” (WBIT). The classic story is a double room with two patients cared for by one nurse holding pre-printed labels for both. They draw the blood bank tube from one patient and apply the other patient's label. The blood bank then types the sample perfectly correctly — it is just the wrong person's blood. Everything downstream is flawlessly wrong. This is why hospitals are hyper-vigilant about bedside sample labelling, and why the clerical check is step 3 in the reaction algorithm.

🧠 The allergic spectrum — two mechanisms
  • The strawberry model. The donor ate strawberries. A soluble allergen from the donor's diet is present in the plasma, the recipient has pre-formed anti-strawberry IgE, and they react. A hypersensitivity reaction — usually just rash and itching, treated with diphenhydramine, and the transfusion can often be resumed cautiously.
  • IgA deficiency. The mechanism behind true anaphylaxis. Every plasma-containing product contains IgA. A patient who is IgA deficient and has formed anti-IgA can react anaphylactically to a tiny volume — the classic case reacts after 5 mL. These patients need washed or IgA-deficient products. Stop immediately, give epinephrine, support the airway.

TRALI — a mechanism worth knowing, and a public health success

  1. A woman becomes pregnant. During pregnancy she is exposed to fetal cells carrying paternal tissue antigens.
  2. She makes anti-HLA antibodies. They do not harm her at all — she simply has them. 14–20% of women who have been pregnant do.
  3. More pregnancies, more antibody. Each pregnancy adds to the repertoire.
  4. She becomes a blood donor. Her plasma now carries a load of anti-HLA antibody into the product.
  5. The recipient's white cells are the target. Those antibodies react with recipient leukocytes, which agglutinate in the pulmonary capillaries — leukoagglutination in the lungs — producing acute lung injury.
🩺 Why you almost never see it now

Radiographically it is ARDS: bilateral pulmonary opacities with a hypoxic patient, and it can cause death. Blood centres now test female donors for anti-HLA antibodies and avoid making high-plasma-volume products from donors who have them. A generation ago this killed people; now it is genuinely rare.

🪝 Memory hook — TRALI versus TACO ★ HY

Both present as a hypoxic patient with a white-out on chest imaging, and they are the two most confused entries on the list.

  • TRALI — an immune injury to the pulmonary capillaries from donor anti-HLA antibodies. Blood pressure tends to be low, jugular venous pressure normal, and diuresis does not help.
  • TACO — too much volume, too fast. Blood pressure tends to be high, jugular venous pressure raised, and diuresis does help.

TACO = Too much volume (like eating too many). TRALI = the donor's antibodies attacked the Lung.

Transfusion-associated graft-versus-host disease

⚠️ The graft here is the blood product itself

Residual donor lymphocytes in the unit engraft and proliferate in the patient, then attack the recipient's tissues — including the bone marrow, which is why every cell line collapses into pancytopenia. Treatment is largely futile; the only rescue is a stem cell transplant, and it is very hard to get one in time.

Two groups at risk:

  • Patients with a severely dysfunctional immune system, who cannot reject the donor lymphocytes.
  • Patients whose donor partially matches their HLA type — which is why directed donation from relatives is discouraged. If a mother is HLA A2/A3 and her child inherited A3 from her and A3 from the father, the child's immune system tolerates the mother's cells as sufficiently self, while the mother's donor lymphocytes recognize the child's paternal antigen as foreign and attack. One-way recognition.

Prevention: irradiate the product. Irradiation damages the DNA of residual white cells so they cannot proliferate in the recipient; red cells, having no nucleus, still function. It is the only defence.

Every unit is tested for all of these diseases, and if anything is positive the unit is not given — it is discarded.

Q: A patient becomes acutely dyspneic and hypoxic one hour into a transfusion, with bilateral pulmonary infiltrates. Blood pressure 88/50; jugular venous pressure not elevated. Diagnosis and mechanism?

Answer

TRALI — transfusion-related acute lung injury. The discriminating features against TACO are the hypotension and the normal jugular venous pressure; circulatory overload would produce hypertension with a raised JVP and would respond to diuresis, which TRALI does not.

Mechanism: the donor — classically a woman sensitized during pregnancy, since 14–20% of parous women carry anti-HLA antibodies — passed anti-HLA antibodies in the plasma. These react with the recipient's leukocytes, causing leukoagglutination in the pulmonary capillaries and acute lung injury with an ARDS picture. Management is supportive.

Q: Why is irradiation of blood products required for a severely immunocompromised child, and what does irradiation actually do?

Answer

To prevent transfusion-associated graft-versus-host disease. Blood products contain residual donor lymphocytes. A patient with a competent immune system rejects them; a severely immunocompromised patient cannot, so they engraft, proliferate, and attack the recipient's tissues including the marrow — producing pancytopenia, rash, diarrhea and liver dysfunction, usually fatally.

Irradiation damages the DNA of the residual white cells so they cannot proliferate in the recipient. Red cells, which have no nucleus, still function. Irradiation is also required for products from related donors, because partial HLA matching allows the donor lymphocytes to escape recognition while still recognizing the recipient as foreign.

Answer

Q (case): An older man had a gastrointestinal bleed, received 4 units of red cells and 3 units of plasma, ultimately underwent liver transplantation, and was discharged. He returns with anemia, and now every cell line is down. What happened, who is at risk, and how is it prevented?

Q: A patient refuses a needed transfusion because she is afraid of contracting HIV. How do you counsel her?

Answer

Acknowledge that the fear is historically grounded — thousands of people did acquire HIV and hepatitis from blood products, and hemophiliacs were devastated. Then give her the actual numbers: the residual risk of transfusion-transmitted HIV is now roughly comparable to being struck by lightning.

Explain the two layers of protection: every unit is tested for a panel of transmissible diseases and any positive unit is discarded, and the supply comes exclusively from unpaid volunteer donors who complete a detailed health questionnaire — a system built specifically in response to the 1980s. Indirect thrombin inhibitors — the heparins

🧠 None of them touches a clotting factor directly

They bind antithrombin and change its shape, turning a slow natural brake into a fast one. What differs between them is which factors the accelerated antithrombin can still reach — and that single difference generates every clinical distinction:

  • Unfractionated heparin is long enough to wrap around both thrombin (IIa) and factor Xa → inhibits both, roughly equally.
  • LMWH is too short to bridge to thrombin → mostly anti-Xa.
  • Fondaparinux is the minimal 5-sugar sequence → pure anti-Xa, nothing else.

Heparin (unfractionated)

prototype · UFH / HMWH

Monitor: aPTT (or anti-Xa level). Reversal: protamine sulfate — full. Short half-life and a real antidote make it the choice when you may need to stop fast, and in severe renal impairment. Enoxaparin · Dalteparin

low-molecular-weight heparin

Predictable dosing → no routine monitoring (check anti-Xa only in pregnancy, obesity or renal impairment). Subcutaneous, outpatient-friendly. Renally cleared — dose-reduce when CrCl < 30. Protamine only partial (~60%). HIT risk lower than UFH but not zero.

Fondaparinux

synthetic indirect factor Xa inhibitor

A synthetic pentasaccharide — pure anti-Xa, no thrombin effect. Does not bind platelet factor 4, so it does not cause HIT and is used to treat it. No effective reversal agent — protamine does not work. Contraindicated when CrCl < 30.

🪝 Memory hook — chain length decides everything

Picture antithrombin as a hand and heparin as the rope tying it to a target. Long rope (UFH) reaches around to grab thrombin AND Xa. Short rope (LMWH) only reaches Xa. Shortest rope (fondaparinux) reaches Xa only — and nothing can untie it. Rope length also predicts protamine's grip: full → partial → none.

⚠️ Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia — a clotting emergency, not a bleeding one ★ HY

Antibodies form against the heparin–platelet factor 4 complex. Those antibodies then activate platelets. So despite the falling platelet count, the danger is thrombosis, not hemorrhage — limb-threatening and life-threatening clots.

  • Timing: platelets fall 5–10 days after exposure (sooner with recent prior exposure), typically a >50% drop from baseline.
  • Management: stop all heparin — including flushes and coated catheters — and start a non-heparin anticoagulant: argatroban, bivalirudin or fondaparinux.
  • Do not start warfarin alone in acute HIT. It drops protein C first and can precipitate venous limb gangrene. Bridge until the platelet count recovers.

Warfarin — 70 years old and still tested hardest ★ HY

🔑 Mechanism, monitoring, onset, reversal, pregnancy
  • Mechanism: inhibits vitamin K epoxide reductase (VKORC1) → no recycled vitamin K → the liver cannot finish factors II, VII, IX and X, or the natural anticoagulants protein C and protein S.
  • Monitoring: INR. Usual target 2.0–3.0; higher (2.5–3.5) for a mechanical mitral valve.
  • Onset: delayed by days — the existing factors must decay first.
  • Reversal: vitamin K (slow, hours) plus 4-factor prothrombin complex concentrate (immediate) for serious bleeding; fresh frozen plasma if PCC is unavailable.
  • Pregnancy: teratogenic — contraindicated (fetal warfarin syndrome). Use a heparin instead.
🧠 Why the onset is slow — and why you must bridge ★ HY

Warfarin does not touch factors that are already circulating. It only stops the liver making new functional ones, so anticoagulation arrives at the pace at which the existing factors decay.

Factor VII has the shortest half-life (~6 hours), and the INR is dominated by factor VII. So the INR moves before the patient is actually anticoagulated — factor II (prothrombin), with a half-life of days, is still fully present. A therapeutic-looking INR on day 2 is a lie.

Worse, protein C has a shorter half-life than factors II, IX and X. So in the first days the body loses its anticoagulant before it loses its procoagulants — a transient hypercoagulable window. In patients with underlying protein C deficiency this causes warfarin-induced skin necrosis over fatty areas.

Both problems have the same fix: bridge with a heparin (unfractionated heparin or enoxaparin) until the INR has been therapeutic for at least 24 hours, typically ≥ 5 days of overlap.

Direct oral factor Xa inhibitors — the “-xabans”

🔑 Class blueprint

Q: A patient starts warfarin for a new deep vein thrombosis. On day 2 the INR is 2.3. The intern wants to stop the enoxaparin. What is wrong with that?

Answer

The INR is not yet telling you the patient is anticoagulated. The INR is dominated by factor VII, which has the shortest half-life (~6 hours) and therefore falls first. Factor II (prothrombin), with a half-life of days, is still fully present — so a therapeutic-looking INR on day 2 reflects factor VII depletion, not true antithrombotic effect.

On top of that, protein C also has a short half-life, so the first days of warfarin are a transient hypercoagulable window. Continue the enoxaparin bridge for at least 5 days and until the INR has been therapeutic for 24 hours.

Q: On day 7 of unfractionated heparin, a patient's platelet count falls from 240,000 to 90,000 and their leg becomes cold and pulseless. Diagnosis, first move, and what you must NOT do?

Answer

Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia. Antibodies to the heparin–platelet factor 4 complex activate platelets, so despite the falling count the danger is thrombosis, not bleeding — here a limb-threatening arterial event. The timing (5–10 days) and the >50% drop fit.

First move: stop all heparin, including flushes and heparin-coated catheters, and start a non-heparin anticoagulant — argatroban, bivalirudin or fondaparinux.

What you must NOT do: start warfarin alone. It depletes protein C first and can precipitate venous limb gangrene. Bridge until the platelet count recovers. Also do not transfuse platelets reflexively — this is a clotting emergency.

Q: A 76-year-old on warfarin for atrial fibrillation presents with a severe headache and altered mental status. PT 30 s, INR 2.7. Concern is intracranial hemorrhage. Is plasma the best option?

Answer

He needs urgent reversal, but plasma is probably not the best way to do it. His INR of 2.7 is genuinely low enough to matter (unlike an INR of 1.1). Warfarin inhibits the vitamin K-dependent factors II, VII, IX and X, so he has a multiple factor deficiency and a possible intracranial bleed.

Prothrombin complex concentrates — 3-factor and 4-factor — are more efficacious than plasma for warfarin reversal. They work faster, in a far smaller volume (important in a 76-year-old with atrial fibrillation who cannot tolerate volume overload), and achieve a more reliable INR correction.

And whichever product you choose, also give vitamin K. The concentrate corrects the INR now; vitamin K restores his own ability to make the factors, so the INR does not rebound as the concentrate clears.

Q: A patient stable on warfarin is started on trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole for a urinary tract infection and simultaneously begins a new high-kale diet. Predict the net effect on the INR and explain both mechanisms.

Answer

The two push in opposite directions, so the net effect is unpredictable — which is exactly why you check the INR rather than reason it out.

Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole raises the INR by two mechanisms at once: it is a CYP2C9 inhibitor (less warfarin cleared — a pharmacokinetic interaction), and as an antibiotic it kills the gut flora that produce vitamin K (less substrate — a pharmacodynamic one). It is one of the highest-risk antibiotic interactions with warfarin.

The kale lowers the INR — a drug–food interaction. Leafy greens are rich in vitamin K, which outcompetes warfarin at VKORC1. Note the counselling point: patients are told to keep vitamin K intake consistent, not to avoid greens.

Antiplatelet Drugs and Fibrinolytics

Six antiplatelet mechanisms, one destination. A platelet can be activated through several doors — thromboxane, adenosine diphosphate, thrombin — but every path ends at GP IIb/IIIa opening up so fibrinogen can bridge one platelet to the next.

🪝 Memory hook — white clot versus red clot ★ HY
  • Arterial clots are platelet-rich (“white clots”) — high shear, ruptured plaque. Treated with antiplatelets: myocardial infarction, ischemic stroke, peripheral arterial disease, stents.
  • Venous clots are fibrin-and-red-cell-rich (“red clots”) — stasis. Treated with anticoagulants: deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, and the clot in atrial fibrillation.

White = Wall of the artery = platelets. Red = Resting vein = fibrin. Fast blood makes white clots; slow blood makes red ones. This single distinction answers a surprising number of “which drug” questions — and explains why aspirin is not the answer for a deep vein thrombosis.

Aspirin — the COX inhibitor prototype

🔑 Mechanism, and why “irreversible” matters
  • Mechanism: irreversibly acetylates cyclooxygenase-1 → no thromboxane A2 → the platelet cannot recruit its neighbours.
  • Why irreversible matters: a platelet has no nucleus and cannot make new enzyme, so the effect lasts the platelet's entire 7–10 day lifespan. That is why you stop aspirin ~7 days before major surgery, and why a single dose commits you for a week.
  • The dose paradox: low dose is the antiplatelet dose. Higher doses also inhibit COX-2 in the endothelium, which makes prostacyclin — the platelet inhibitor — so pushing the dose can work against you.
  • Harms: gastrointestinal ulceration and bleeding; tinnitus at high dose; Reye syndrome in children with viral illness.

Fibrinolytic (thrombolytic) drugs ★ HY

🔑 Every other drug in this block prevents a clot. These destroy one that already exists.

Mechanism: they convert plasminogen → plasmin; plasmin degrades the fibrin mesh holding the clot together. Fibrin degradation products (measured as the D-dimer) are the debris.

They are relatively fibrin-selective — they work best on plasminogen already bound to a clot — but “relatively” is doing a lot of work, which is why systemic bleeding is the defining risk.

Q: A patient on clopidogrel after a coronary stent is started on omeprazole for reflux. What is the problem, and what would you do instead?

Answer

Clopidogrel is a prodrug that requires CYP2C19 for activation, and omeprazole inhibits CYP2C19. The result is reduced conversion to the active metabolite and blunted antiplatelet effect — a real concern in a freshly stented patient, where inadequate platelet inhibition risks stent thrombosis.

Use pantoprazole instead, which does not meaningfully inhibit CYP2C19. Alternatively switch to ticagrelor, which is not a prodrug and has no CYP2C19 dependency — keeping in mind its own quirks (dyspnea, and aspirin must stay ≤ 100 mg/day). Note also that genetic CYP2C19 poor metabolizers get little benefit from clopidogrel regardless of co-medication.

Q: A patient with atrial fibrillation asks why they cannot just take aspirin instead of apixaban. Answer them mechanistically.

Answer

Because the two drugs target different kinds of clot. The clot that forms in a fibrillating left atrial appendage is a stasis clot — fibrin-and-red-cell-rich, a “red clot”. Aspirin blocks thromboxane-mediated platelet aggregation, which is what drives arterial, high-shear, plaque-rupture “white clots” — myocardial infarction, ischemic stroke from plaque, peripheral arterial disease.

To prevent a fibrin-rich stasis clot you must interrupt the coagulation cascade, which is what apixaban does at factor Xa. Aspirin is substantially inferior for stroke prevention in atrial fibrillation. Same logic as why aspirin is not the answer for a deep vein thrombosis.

Q: Two hours into alteplase for an acute ischemic stroke, a patient develops a new severe headache, vomiting and a falling level of consciousness. What has happened, and what do you give?

Answer

Intracranial hemorrhage — the feared complication of thrombolysis, and the reason the contraindication list exists.

Stop the infusion immediately. Get emergent non-contrast head imaging. Then give cryoprecipitate to replace the fibrinogen that plasmin has consumed, and an antifibrinolytic — tranexamic acid or aminocaproic acid — which blocks the conversion of plasminogen to plasmin and stops the demolition. Support blood pressure and involve neurosurgery.

Oral iron — the cheapest drug on the list and the one patients quit

🔑 One fact drives every rule

Only ferrous (Fe²⁺) iron crosses the gut wall, and only in an acidic duodenum. Everything that raises stomach pH or holds iron in the wrong oxidation state is a failure mode.

  • Dosing: classic adult target 150–200 mg elemental iron daily. Newer data favour alternate-day dosing — each dose spikes hepcidin for roughly a day, so a second dose runs into a closed door.
  • Timing: at least 1 hour before meals; but giving it with food is an acceptable trade when that is the only way the patient will take it. An absorbed smaller dose beats a refused larger one.
  • Formulations to avoid: enteric-coated and sustained-release — they release iron distal to the duodenum, past the absorption window.
  • Best dietary sources: meat, fish and poultry (heme iron). Milk and tea reduce absorption.

⚠️ Adverse effects of oral iron — the real reason therapy fails ★ HY

Gastrointestinal effects are extremely common: metallic taste, nausea, flatulence, constipation, diarrhea, epigastric distress, vomiting. Patients are also bothered by itching and by black/green or tarry stools that stain clothing and cause alarm about bleeding. The result is poor adherence — the single most common reason a “treatment failure” is not a treatment failure at all.

Interactions: antacids, H2 receptor blockers and proton pump inhibitors all raise gastric pH and impair absorption — separate them by iron 2 hours before, or 4 hours after, an antacid.

🩺 When to give up on the oral route ★ HY

Switch to parenteral iron for: iron malabsorption · intolerance of oral iron · nonadherence · severe or ongoing blood loss (losing it faster than the gut can absorb) · gastric surgery (less acid, bypassed duodenum) · malabsorption syndromes (celiac, Whipple, bacterial overgrowth).

Knowing it worked: a reticulocyte bump at 7–10 days and a hemoglobin rise of roughly 1 g/dL every 2–3 weeks. Then keep going — continue iron for 3–6 months after the hemoglobin normalizes to refill ferritin. Stopping when the hemoglobin looks pretty is the classic under-treatment.

Intravenous iron — and the acute toxicity question

🔑 Every IV iron product, same three rules
  • Administer slowly at first and observe for infusion reactions.
  • Non-allergic infusion reactions are common and self-limiting: urticaria, palpitations, dizziness, and neck and back spasm. This is not anaphylaxis — recognizing the difference prevents needlessly labelling a patient iron-allergic for life.
  • True adverse effects: allergic reactions including life-threatening anaphylaxis (exceedingly rare), hypotension, dizziness, dyspnea, headache, lower back pain, arthralgia, syncope. Many are minimized by decreasing the dose or the rate.
  • Premedication for asthma or more than one drug allergy: methylprednisolone 125 mg IV.

Counterintuitive and heavily testable. Diphenhydramine does not prevent the reaction and causes its own: hypotension, somnolence, flushing, dizziness, irritability, nasal congestion, wheezing and supraventricular tachycardia — a list that looks exactly like the infusion reaction you were trying to prevent, which then gets misattributed to the iron. Vitamin B12 and folate as drugs

Cyanocobalamin

vitamin B12 replacement

Available OTC (multivitamins, B-complex) and by prescription as oral, intranasal and injectable (IM and subcutaneous). The injectable and intranasal routes bypass a broken gut entirely — essential in pernicious anemia. Adverse effects are rare; the two uncommon ones both come from suddenly restarting a stalled marrow: hypokalemia (potassium pulled into new cells) and hyperuricemia (nucleic acid turnover).

Folic acid

vitamin B9 replacement

1 mg daily is standard replacement; 1–5 mg in malabsorption; 500 micrograms for anticonvulsant-induced megaloblastic anemia, so the anticonvulsant need not be stopped. Nontoxic even at high doses — rapidly excreted in urine. Long-term dosing in chronic hemolysis, pregnancy and dialysis.

Folinic acid (leucovorin)

the rescue agent

Bypasses the DHFR step and is directly converted to active folate forms. Methotrexate inhibits DHFR, so in a methotrexate-poisoned patient giving folic acid is useless — the enzyme that would activate it is exactly the one blocked. Folinic acid is already past that step.

“FoliNic is fiNished” — N for “No enzyme needed”. Folic acid still needs DHFR, which methotrexate has already broken. “MetForMin and the PPI Pinch B12” — metformin pinches it at the ileum (calcium-dependent uptake at the end of the tube), the proton pump inhibitor pinches it at the stomach (no acid, no release from food). Erythropoiesis-stimulating agents ★ HY

🔑 The class, fully specified — this objective asks for all of it
  • The agents: recombinant epoetin alfa and darbepoetin alfa. Same mechanism; darbepoetin has a longer half-life and is dosed less frequently. Methoxy polyethylene glycol-epoetin beta is the longest-acting.
  • Mechanism of action: stimulate the division and differentiation of committed erythroid progenitor cells, and induce release of reticulocytes from the marrow into the blood, where they mature. There is a dose–response relationship: the reticulocyte count rises first, then hematocrit and hemoglobin.
  • Route: all may be given IV or subcutaneously. Subcutaneous epoetin has a prolonged absorption phase → extended half-life → target hemoglobin maintained at doses 15–30% lower than IV.
  • Approved indications: anemia of chronic kidney disease and anemia due to chemotherapy in cancer patients. Sometimes used in anemia of inflammation, but not FDA-approved for it.
  • When to initiate: only when the anemia is symptomatic and the goal is avoiding transfusion — and only after iron stores are checked and repleted. Not for acute correction of severe anemia; erythropoiesis is delayed in onset and an ESA is not a substitute for emergency transfusion.
  • Pregnancy: use only when clearly needed; safety is not established, and the thrombotic risk of the class is a concern.
⚠️ The four US boxed warnings — they share one sentence ★ HY

Every one of them ends in “use the lowest dose needed to avoid transfusion.”

  1. Cardiovascular events. Increased serious cardiovascular events, myocardial infarction, stroke, venous thromboembolism, vascular access thrombosis and death when targeting hemoglobin above 11 g/dL — with no additional benefit there.
  2. Cancer patients. Shortened overall survival and/or increased tumour progression or recurrence in breast, cervical, head and neck, lymphoid and non-small cell lung cancer. Use in cancer only for anemia from concurrent myelosuppressive chemotherapy, discontinue when the chemotherapy course finishes, and do not use when the anticipated outcome is curative.
  3. Chronic kidney disease patients. Increased death, serious cardiovascular events and stroke when targeting hemoglobin above 11 g/dL.
  4. Perisurgery patients. Increased deep venous thrombosis in orthopaedic surgery. DVT prophylaxis is strongly recommended whenever ESAs are used to reduce transfusions in surgical patients.

🩺 Iron first, signal second

An ESA drives the marrow to build red cells, and building red cells consumes iron fast. Give an ESA to an iron-deplete patient and you create functional iron deficiency: the order is shouted, the factory has no raw material, and the hemoglobin does not move. Check and replete iron before blaming the ESA for not working — this is the most common reason for apparent ESA resistance.

🧠 The newer class — HIF prolyl hydroxylase inhibitors

Vadadustat (daprodustat was withdrawn from the US market in late 2024 for commercial, not safety, reasons). Mechanism: HIF is a key regulator of the erythropoietin gene; normally prolyl hydroxylases tag HIF for destruction whenever oxygen is plentiful. Inhibiting them raises intracellular HIF, activates the oxygen-sensing pathway and stimulates erythropoiesis — it convinces the cell it is at altitude. Oral, for anemia of chronic kidney disease in adults on dialysis ≥ 3 months; the oral route is the entire selling point against injected ESAs.

The first oral pill for alpha- and beta-thalassemia anemia; also approved for pyruvate kinase deficiency. Increases cellular energy, keeping red cells alive longer. Filgrastim and the long-acting G-CSFs

prevention of febrile neutropenia

Filgrastim stimulates neutrophil production to shorten the duration of neutropenia — daily injections. Pegfilgrastim and eflapegrastim use pegylation to block renal clearance (once per chemotherapy cycle); efbemalenograstim alfa uses an IgG2 Fc fusion instead, avoiding PEG allergy. Signature side effect: bone pain — expected, not harmful. Do not give within ~24 hours of cytotoxic chemotherapy.

⚠️ Complement inhibitors and meningococcal disease

In paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria — a deficiency of the complement regulators CD55 and CD59 causing intravascular hemolysis, and a highly prothrombotic state where thrombosis, not anemia, is what kills patients — the drugs are complement inhibitors: eculizumab and ravulizumab (C5), pegcetacoplan (C3), and iptacopan, the first oral agent, which inhibits complement factor B.

Blocking terminal complement removes the exact defence the body uses against encapsulated organisms. All complement inhibitors carry a boxed warning for serious, life-threatening meningococcal infection, and patients must be vaccinated against Neisseria meningitidis before starting. Note the parallel with asplenia in Section 10 — same organisms, different missing defence.

Memory hook: “C3 catches more than C5.” Blocking higher in the cascade stops more hemolysis — C5 blockers stop only the intravascular half, leaving C3b-coated cells to be eaten in the spleen; C3 and factor B blockers stop both.

🧠 Hemophilia A and B — four strategies, and the mechanism each one reveals

Hemophilia A is insufficient factor VIII (1 : 5,000 live births); hemophilia B is insufficient factor IX (1 : 25,000). Both are X-linked. The mechanism they share: factors IXa and VIIIa together activate factor X, and that is the step both diseases break.

  1. Replace it. Intravenous recombinant or plasma-derived factor concentrate. Simple and effective — but requires frequent venous access, and some patients develop inhibitors (antibodies against replacement factor) that make it stop working.
  2. Mimic it — emicizumab. A bispecific antibody: one arm binds factor IX, the other binds factor X, holding them together. That is all factor VIII ever did — it is a clamp, not an enzyme. Subcutaneous, and because it is not factor VIII, anti-factor-VIII inhibitors do not neutralize it.
  3. Insert the gene. Adeno-associated virus vectors delivering a functional factor gene to liver cells — fidanacogene elparvovec and etranacogene dezaparvovec (hemophilia B), valoctocogene roxaparvovec (hemophilia A).
  4. Rebalance — works in A and B. If you cannot raise the procoagulant side, lower the anticoagulant side. Anti-TFPI antibodies marstacimab (without inhibitors) and concizumab (with inhibitors) block tissue factor pathway inhibitor; the siRNA fitusiran degrades antithrombin mRNA. The predictable adverse event for all of them is thrombosis — you are deliberately removing a natural anticoagulant.

Memory hooks: emicizumab is a molecular clothespin (bispecific = two arms). And “with or without inhibitors” is the tie-breaker: Concizumab = Complicated patient (WITH inhibitors); marstacimab and fitusiran, without.

Q: A 34-year-old with celiac disease and iron deficiency anemia has failed three months of ferrous sulfate. Her ferritin is still 6 ng/mL. What went wrong, and what do you do?

Answer

Iron malabsorption — celiac disease damages the duodenum, which is the only site where iron is absorbed. No oral dose will work reliably. (Check adherence too, since gastrointestinal side effects are the commonest reason for apparent failure — but here the pathology explains it.)

Switch to intravenous iron, which bypasses the duodenum, the acid requirement and the hepcidin door entirely. Give it slowly with observation for infusion reactions; if she has asthma or multiple drug allergies, premedicate with methylprednisolone 125 mg IV — not diphenhydramine. If she is receiving repeat courses of ferric carboxymaltose, check a phosphate level.

Q: A patient with chronic kidney disease on epoetin alfa has a hemoglobin that has risen from 9.2 to 10.9 g/dL over two weeks. The nephrology fellow wants to increase the dose to reach 12. What do you say?

Answer

Both parts of that plan violate the boxed warnings.

The target is wrong. Targeting hemoglobin above 11 g/dL increases death, serious cardiovascular events and stroke in chronic kidney disease, with no additional benefit. The goal is the lowest dose needed to avoid transfusion, not a normal hemoglobin.

The rate is also wrong. The hemoglobin has risen 1.7 g/dL in 2 weeks. The rule is to decrease the dose if it rises more than 1 g/dL in any 2-week period, because an excessive rate causes or worsens hypertension — the most common adverse effect, occurring in up to a third of treated patients. Reduce the dose, check the blood pressure, and confirm iron stores are adequate.

Q: A patient is admitted with methotrexate toxicity. Someone has written for high-dose folic acid. Why will that not work, and what should be given?

Answer

Methotrexate inhibits dihydrofolate reductase (DHFR) — and DHFR is exactly the enzyme required to convert folic acid into its biologically active form. Giving folic acid hands the cell a substrate it cannot activate.

Give folinic acid (leucovorin). It is already past the DHFR step and is converted directly to active folate forms, restoring the folate pool without needing the blocked enzyme. That is what “leucovorin rescue” means — you are not reversing methotrexate; you are handing the cell an activated folate it can still use.

Q: Why would you choose luspatercept rather than an erythropoiesis-stimulating agent in transfusion-dependent beta thalassemia?

Answer

Because thalassemia's problem is ineffective erythropoiesis, not an absent signal. The marrow has plenty of precursors — it is hypercellular — and they are dying before release because unpaired globin chains destroy them. An ESA amplifies the build order, which would only shout louder at an assembly line already jammed at the end.

Luspatercept is an erythroid maturation agent: it helps late-stage red cells complete development, unblocking the actual bottleneck. Different lever, matched to the mechanism.

Sepsis — Signs, Scores, Microbial Triggers and Management

The body's extreme response to an infection, resulting in life-threatening organ dysfunction from a dysregulated host response. Rather than dealing with the infection, the immune system attacks “self”.

🔑 The one sentence that organizes everything

Because the damage comes from the host response rather than the pathogen directly: no single organism defines sepsis, cultures are negative in 30–50% of cases, and — as the outline states twice — no assessment or diagnostic criterion has sufficient sensitivity or specificity. That is why there are five competing scoring systems rather than one test.

The three terms, in order of severity

  1. Early sepsis. Infection and bacteremia are risk factors — the setup, not the diagnosis itself.
  2. Sepsis. Life-threatening, time-dependent organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection. The phrase time-dependent is what justifies the treatment bundle.
  3. Septic shock. A subset of sepsis in which underlying circulatory AND cellular/metabolic abnormalities are profound enough to substantially increase mortality. Can lead to multi-organ dysfunction (MODS, an older term).
🧠 How to keep them straight

Infection/bacteremia → risk factor. Add organ dysfunction → sepsis. Add profound circulatory and cellular/metabolic abnormality → septic shock. Each step is a strict subset of the one before, and each adds one requirement. Note that septic shock is not simply hypotension — the cellular/metabolic derangement is part of the definition, which is why lactate sits in the treatment bundle.

Both are early-warning scores built from vital signs. NEWS2 has 6 parameters (respiration rate, oxygen saturation, systolic blood pressure, pulse, consciousness level, temperature). The sepsis paradigm — mechanism

  1. The patient is injured by some means — the initiating insult.
  2. Host defences activate — release of inflammatory cytokines, plus activation of other agents that exert control over bodily systems.
  3. Persistent inflammation → the cascade to organ failure. Endothelial dysfunction and ischemic tissue injury → sequential organ damage/failure → activation of cell death pathways: necrosis, necroptosis, pyroptosis, autophagy.
🧠 Why “sequential” matters

Organ failure in sepsis is sequential, not simultaneous — which is precisely what the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment is built to track. The mechanism and the scoring system share the same word for the same reason.

Q: A septic patient has a normal white blood cell count. Does this argue against the diagnosis?

Answer

No. The laboratory criterion is satisfied by leukocytosis (> 12,000/µL), leukopenia (< 4,000/µL), or a normal white count with > 10% immature forms — a left shift with a normal total. Q: Which sepsis assessment is described as more specific with better predictive validity than SIRS, and what does a score of ≥ 2 indicate?

Answer

SOFA — the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment. A score ≥ 2 indicates a mortality risk of approximately 10%. It assesses neurologic, liver, kidney and blood/hemodynamic systems. Q: Name the four cell death pathways activated by persistent inflammation in sepsis, and the two upstream events that precede them.

Answer

Cell death pathways: necrosis, necroptosis, pyroptosis, autophagy.

The two upstream events are endothelial dysfunction and ischemic tissue injury, which together produce sequential organ damage and failure. The chain to reproduce: persistent inflammation → endothelial dysfunction + ischemic tissue injury → sequential organ failure → cell death pathways.

Bacteroides fragilis — the abdominal site is the one explicitly paired with B. fragilis and mixed anaerobes, alongside E. coli and Candida. 20% of sepsis cases are polymicrobial, and abdominal sources are a major reason why.

Q: Which HIV protein is easiest to detect in serum, which gene encodes it, and why does that matter for testing?

Answer

p24, the capsid protein, encoded by the gag gene — cleaved from the p55 precursor by viral protease.

It matters because p24 antigen becomes detectable before antibodies do. Pairing p24 antigen detection with antibody detection in a combination immunoassay is exactly what lets 4th-generation testing catch acute infection, weeks before an antibody-only test would turn positive.

Q: A patient's virus develops a single point mutation in a conserved domain of reverse transcriptase. Which drug class is most likely to be rendered entirely ineffective, and why does the other reverse-transcriptase class fare better?

Answer

The NNRTIs (efavirenz, doravirine). A single point mutation on a conserved RT domain can inactivate all members of the class, because they all bind the same allosteric pocket non-competitively.

NRTIs fare better because their resistance mechanism is different: point mutations reduce the affinity of NRTI binding rather than abolishing it, and NRTIs bind competitively at the active site as nucleoside analogues — so they generally require accumulated mutations rather than one.

Three cases span the arc of the disease: acute retroviral syndrome, a newly diagnosed outpatient, and advanced AIDS with an opportunistic infection.

Acute retroviral syndrome — the diagnosis that gets missed

🔑 Why it matters
  • After acquisition, more than 50% develop a transient symptomatic illness — which then resolves on its own.
  • Features are nonspecific and it is most often confused with mononucleosis.
  • Transmission risk is at its highest here — very high HIV RNA plus the person does not know their status.
  • Keep it on the differential for any teenager or 20-something with non-exudative pharyngitis + rash + splenomegaly + atypical lymphocytosis.

The key discriminator from Group A streptococcal pharyngitis: acute retroviral syndrome gives NON-exudative pharyngitis. Frequencies from the lecture: fever ~75–80%, fatigue ~68–78%, myalgia ~48%, rash ~48–51%, headache ~45–54%, pharyngitis ~40%.

🩺 Two clinical pearls that predate modern testing
  • Hypergammaglobulinemia with a reversed albumin:globulin ratio was the historical tip-off for HIV when serology took 6 weeks to 3 months — especially alongside atypical lymphocytosis. It is still a useful clue.
  • New-onset or unusually aggressive seborrheic dermatitis — classically at the glabella (between the eyebrows) and nasolabial folds — should make you think about HIV and screen for it.

The three periods — why the window exists ★ HY

Seroconversion is the interval between acquiring HIV and the first detection of anti-HIV antibodies — the window period. How long it is depends on the sensitivity of the assay, which is why modern practice uses 4th- or 5th-generation assays that also detect p24 antigen, catching infection weeks before antibodies appear.

The CDC testing algorithm — three tiers ★ HY

  1. HIV-1/2 antigen/antibody immunoassay, with HIV-1 p24 antigen. Non-reactive → stop. But if acute infection is clinically suspected, go straight to an HIV-1 NAT, because of the eclipse period.
  2. HIV-1/HIV-2 antibody differentiation immunoassay — performed as a reflex when step 1 is reactive. Resolves to HIV-1 positive, HIV-2 positive, untypeable, or indeterminate/negative. If antibodies are detected → manage as HIV infection.
  3. HIV-1 nucleic acid test (NAT / RNA PCR) — reflexed when the differentiation assay is negative or indeterminate. RNA detected here = acute HIV-1 infection; antibodies have not yet developed. RNA not detected → negative; offer prevention services including PrEP and PEP.
⚠️ “Why not just do the NAT first?”

The nucleic acid test does detect HIV earlier. But it is roughly 3× the cost, far more complex for laboratories to run, and carries a real false-positive potential — particularly in low-risk individuals. That is why it sits at tier 3, not tier 1.

Note also: the Western blot is obsolete. It detects antibodies only, has a longer detection window, inferior sensitivity, and cannot differentiate HIV-1 from HIV-2. It was the standard until 2014.

🩺 False positives are rare

The false-positive rate for HIV testing with reflex is 0.22%. Practically: when a modern 4th/5th-generation assay with reflex is positive, the patient has HIV. Patients will sometimes not believe the result — these tests are very good.

CD4 count versus viral load — the single most testable distinction ★ HY

🛡️

CD4 T-cell count

Degree of immune system impairment. Determines the risk of opportunistic infections and who needs prophylaxis. It is the staging variable.

🦠

HIV RNA viral load

Active viral replication. Determines drug selection and monitors treatment response. It is the transmission variable — the basis of U = U.

🔑 Two thresholds that are NOT the same number
  • CD4 < 200 cells/µL (or < 14%) defines AIDS — CDC stage 3. So does the presence of an AIDS-defining opportunistic infection, at any CD4 count.
  • CD4 < 50 cells/µL is where opportunistic infection risk is greatest.

Careful: community-acquired pneumonia by itself, without evidence of a compromised immune system, is not an opportunistic infection. In a patient with an impaired immune system, the same pneumonia does count — and two or more episodes of bacterial pneumonia within 12 months is itself AIDS-defining.

Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia ★ HY

🧠 Why it is an “atypical” fungus — and why that dictates the drug
  • Classified as a fungus on RNA, cell wall and key enzymes — but it does not grow in fungal culture.
  • Its cell wall contains cholesterol, not ergosterol — which is precisely why the standard antifungals that target ergosterol synthesis do not work, and why the treatment is TMP/SMX.
  • The cell wall is rich in β-1,3-D-glucan — the basis of the serum β-D-glucan test.
  • Main risk factor is immunosuppression of any cause, not just HIV: kidney transplant, chemotherapy, chronic steroids.
  • Airborne transmission; exists in a latent state within alveolar macrophages.

🩺 PJP management
  • Treat empirically when suspicion is high — CD4 < 200, hypoxemia with an increased A–a gradient, classic radiographic features. Do not wait for confirmation.
  • TMP/SMX is the preferred regimen — oral for mild–moderate, intravenous for severe. Dose by weight on the trimethoprim component.
  • Add corticosteroids in moderate–severe disease — they reduce respiratory failure and mortality. Indications: worsening respiratory symptoms with PaO₂ < 70 mmHg or an A–a gradient ≥ 35 mmHg. Prednisone for 21 days.
  • Expect worsening for the first 2–3 days after starting treatment — killing the organism releases inflammatory material. This early worsening is NOT IRIS; it is too soon.
  • Start ART within 2 weeks of beginning PJP treatment, to restore immune function.
  • Prophylaxis: TMP/SMX if CD4 < 200 and not on ART, continued until the immune system reconstitutes.

Opportunistic infection versus IRIS ★ HY

🧠 Definition

Immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome is an exaggerated immune response to a variety of pathogens, occurring in response to ART-mediated recovery of the immune system. The immune system comes back online and overreacts to things that were sitting latent.

📉

Paradoxical IRIS

The opportunistic infection is already known. You are treating it, you start ART, and symptoms worsen. Example: a PJP patient improves past the expected 2–3 day dip, then at 1–2 weeks develops worsening hypoxemia and infiltrates and requires intubation.

🎭

Unmasking (ART-associated) IRIS

The opportunistic infection was undiagnosed until ART was started. No known OI; you start ART; the recovering immune system produces inflammation that reveals the infection.

⚠️ The answer is not to delay ART

Delaying costs more than it saves. The correct approach is to anticipate IRIS, counsel the patient that worsening may occur, and keep it on your differential when the patient deteriorates on day 10 instead of improving.

The management plan

  1. The conversation is the intervention, not the preamble. Shared decision-making and motivational interviewing. Identify structural barriers — housing, transport, cost, prior authorization, stigma. Adherence is the whole disease.
  2. Start ART as soon as possible — ideally within 7 days, and “same day” is acceptable and increasingly preferred. Exclude pregnancy in anyone of childbearing potential, and screen for sexually transmitted infections.
  3. Define the goal: U = U. Plasma HIV RNA below the level of detection — the CDC threshold is < 200 copies/mL. Undetectable = Untransmittable. Check viral load every 3–4 months; small temporary rises (“blips”) are not immediately alarming — follow the trend.
  4. Prophylax by CD4 count. See the table below.
  5. Immunize — and know the live vaccine rule.
  6. Prevent onward transmission — PrEP and PEP for partners and contacts.
🔑 The evidence behind U = U — the PARTNER trial

Observed male–female and male–male couples (~3,000 individuals) across more than 74,000 condomless sex episodes. Zero HIV transmissions to HIV-negative partners while the partner with HIV maintained a durable undetectable viral load for ≥ 6 months.

⚠️ The live vaccine rule ★ HY

The three live vaccines — varicella, yellow fever, and MMR (measles, mumps, rubella) — may be given only when the CD4 count is ≥ 200 cells/µL. Two reasons: risk of vaccine-strain disease, and below that threshold the patient cannot mount an immunologic response, so you are wasting the dose.

Zoster: the old live attenuated vaccine is contraindicated in immunocompromised patients and in HIV with CD4 < 200. The recombinant vaccine (2 doses) is the one to use in immunocompromised patients, including all transplant recipients.

Give regardless of CD4 count: mpox, Tdap, COVID-19, influenza, hepatitis A, hepatitis B, and recombinant zoster.

⚠️ The drug interaction you will be tested on ★ HY

Boosters — ritonavir and cobicistat — are added to raise antiretroviral levels by inhibiting cytochrome P450 3A4 (CYP3A4).

Combine a booster with an over-the-counter nasal corticosteroid and the steroid accumulates → iatrogenic Cushing syndrome and adrenal suppression. Nasal steroids are sold over the counter, so if you do not warn the patient, no one will. The same CYP3A4 pathway governs several statins. Always ask about over-the-counter drugs, supplements and herbal preparations at every visit — patients do not volunteer them.

🩺 Three pearls before starting PrEP, and one worked example
  • Always exclude pregnancy and consider a sexually transmitted infection screen.
  • Check renal function and lipids. Both tenofovir formulations are toxic to the proximal tubule — worsening kidney function and Fanconi syndrome. TAF is the less nephrotoxic of the two, which is why it was developed. Both can drive lipids up substantially.
  • Screen for hepatitis B. Emtricitabine and tenofovir both treat active hepatitis B — so if a patient with active hepatitis B stops them, hepatitis B can rebound.

Needlestick on a surgical rotation: notify the surgeon immediately, notify human resources and health services, get tested, and consider starting PEP while results are pending. Do not wait for the source patient's results — the 72-hour window is what matters.

HIV as a disease of aging — chronic complications

🧠 “Inflammaging”

People with HIV now live close to normal lifespans, so the management plan has to extend past viral suppression into decades of cardiometabolic, bone, cognitive and cancer risk. Age-associated inflammation — “inflammaging” — is the major risk factor tying all the comorbidities together. It impacts both innate and adaptive immunity, increases cardiovascular disease, contributes to neurocognitive impairment, and accelerates functional limitation and frailty.

Q: A 32-year-old presents after notification of a positive HIV 4th-generation antigen/antibody test. What is the next test?

A. HIV-1 Western blot · B. HIV-1 RNA PCR (NAT) · C. p24 antigen via ELISA · D. Genotype resistance testing · E. HIV-1/HIV-2 antibody differentiation immunoassay

Answer — E

E. The HIV-1/HIV-2 antibody differentiation immunoassay — it tells you which form of HIV you are dealing with, or whether the strain is untypeable.

A — no: the Western blot detects antibodies only, has a longer detection window, inferior sensitivity, and cannot differentiate HIV-1 from HIV-2. It was the standard until 2014. B — no: that is the third-tier confirmatory test. C — no: p24 is already included in the combination assay. D — no: genotype guides ART selection; it is not part of the diagnostic algorithm.

Q: Which lab value best predicts risk of opportunistic infections in HIV?

A. HIV RNA viral load · B. CD4 T-cell count · C. p24 antigen · D. HIV genotype · E. Absolute lymphocyte count

Answer — B

B. The CD4 T-cell count — the best measure of the degree of immune system impairment, and therefore of opportunistic infection risk and the need for prophylaxis.

A — viral load reflects active replication and drives drug selection; it is the transmission variable (U = U). C — p24 detects acute HIV-1 infection. D — genotype detects resistance mutations. E — useful as a surrogate (< 1,000/mm³ predicts CD4 < 200), but not the best predictor.

Q: A patient with AIDS (CD4 90 cells/µL) is being treated for PJP with TMP/SMX and prednisone. ART is started on day 10. On day 21 she develops worsening hypoxemia and new pulmonary infiltrates. Her HIV RNA has dropped from 1.5 million to 12,000 copies/mL and her CD4 has risen to 180. Most likely explanation?

Answer

Paradoxical IRIS. The tell-tale combination: a known, already-treated opportunistic infection, a short interval between OI treatment and ART start, and a robust virologic and immunologic response (falling viral load, rising CD4) occurring alongside clinical worsening.

The improving laboratory numbers are the clue that separate this from treatment failure or a second infection — in true OI progression the virology would not be improving. Timing also fits: IRIS occurs within the first few months of ART, median 48 days. Note that the expected 2–3 day post-treatment worsening of PJP happens far earlier and is a different phenomenon.

Q: A 25-year-old with multiple sexual partners is considering PrEP. Besides HIV testing, what additional evaluation is recommended before starting?

A. HLA-B*5701 genotype · B. Tropism assay · C. Bone density scan · D. HIV genotype · E. Baseline renal function + pregnancy testing

Answer — E

E. Baseline renal function and pregnancy testing — tenofovir affects kidney function (proximal tubule toxicity, Fanconi syndrome), and pregnancy can be a contraindication.

A — HLA-B*5701 predicts abacavir hypersensitivity, and abacavir is not used in PrEP. B — a tropism assay establishes eligibility for a CCR5 antagonist (maraviroc). C — a DEXA scan documents baseline bone density but is not the required pre-PrEP evaluation. D — genotype detects resistance mutations. Also screen for hepatitis B, since stopping tenofovir/emtricitabine can let it rebound.

Glossary and Abbreviations

If a short form appears anywhere in this file, its long form is here. Each section also carries its own abbreviation strip at the top.

Cells, counts and morphology

CBC — complete blood count
The standard automated panel: hemoglobin, hematocrit, red cell indices, white cell count with differential, and platelet count.
Hgb — hemoglobin
The oxygen-carrying protein, measured by spectrophotometry in grams per decilitre (g/dL). Four subunits, two alpha and two beta, each carrying one oxygen molecule.
Hct — hematocrit
The percentage of whole blood volume that is red cells, measured by centrifugation. Also called packed cell volume. Roughly three times the hemoglobin.
MCV — mean corpuscular volume
Average red cell size in femtolitres (fL). Under 80 microcytic, 80–100 normocytic, over 100 macrocytic.
MCH — mean corpuscular hemoglobin
Average hemoglobin mass per red cell, in picograms. Underlies normochromic and hypochromic.
MCHC
Mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration — hemoglobin per unit red cell volume. Characteristically high in hereditary spherocytosis.
RDW
Red cell distribution width — variability in cell size. Raised when two red cell populations coexist.
LDH — lactate dehydrogenase
An intracellular enzyme released when cells are destroyed. Elevated in hemolysis and in ineffective erythropoiesis.
Reticulocyte
A newly released, immature red cell still containing residual RNA. The count tells you whether the marrow is responding.
Spherocyte
A round, dense red cell with no central pallor. Hereditary spherocytosis and autoimmune hemolytic anemia.
Bite cell / blister cell
A red cell with a semicircular defect where a splenic macrophage removed a Heinz body plus membrane. G6PD deficiency.
Heinz body
An intracellular inclusion of precipitated, oxidized hemoglobin. Requires a supravital stain (crystal violet).
Target cell (codocyte)
A bull's-eye red cell, from too much membrane relative to hemoglobin content. Thalassemia, sickle cell disease, liver disease, iron deficiency.
Schistocyte
An angular red cell fragment, from mechanical shearing.
Howell-Jolly body
A retained nuclear DNA fragment in a mature red cell. Its presence means the spleen is absent or non-functional.
Macro-ovalocyte
A large, oval red cell — characteristic of megaloblastic anemia.
Hypersegmented neutrophil
A neutrophil with six or more nuclear lobes. Near-diagnostic of B12 or folate deficiency.
Anisocytosis / poikilocytosis
Variation in red cell size / variation in red cell shape. Both together is anisopoikilocytosis.
Cellularity
The proportion of marrow occupied by hematopoietic cells rather than fat: normocellular, hypercellular, hypocellular. A core-biopsy word.

Iron, vitamins and the erythron

Fe²⁺ / Fe³⁺
Ferrous / ferric iron. Only the ferrous form is absorbed from the gut.
Transferrin
The plasma protein that transports iron. Picture it carrying cups that may be full or empty.
TIBC — total iron binding capacity
A measure of transferrin binding sites not saturated with iron. Rises in iron deficiency, falls in inflammation.
TSAT — transferrin saturation
The percentage of transferrin binding sites occupied by iron. Under 15% suggests iron deficiency.
Ferritin
The intracellular iron storage protein; the serum level reflects total body stores. Also an acute phase reactant, so it rises in inflammation regardless of stores.
Hepcidin

Ferroportin

Prussian blue stain
A histochemical stain for marrow iron. Blue granules present means stores exist; absence is diagnostic of iron deficiency.

FOBT — fecal occult blood test

IF — intrinsic factor
Secreted by gastric parietal cells; B12 must bind it to be absorbed in the terminal ileum.
MMA — methylmalonic acid
Accumulates in B12 deficiency and stays normal in folate deficiency. The key discriminator.
Homocysteine
Elevated in both B12 and folate deficiency, so it cannot separate them.
THF — tetrahydrofolate
The active form of folate, required for thymidine and therefore DNA synthesis.
DHFR — dihydrofolate reductase
The enzyme that activates folic acid — and the enzyme methotrexate blocks.
Pernicious anemia
Autoimmune destruction of gastric mucosa causing loss of parietal cells and intrinsic factor.
Subacute combined degeneration
Degeneration of the dorsal and lateral spinal columns from B12 deficiency. Eventually irreversible.
EPO — erythropoietin
The kidney hormone that stimulates red cell production. Available as a drug (epoetin, darbepoetin).

Hemolysis and hemoglobinopathies

The plasma protein that binds free hemoglobin after intravascular hemolysis. Consumed in the process, so serum levels fall.
Hemopexin
A second scavenger, which binds free heme rather than intact hemoglobin.
Hemoglobinemia / hemoglobinuria
Free hemoglobin in plasma (pink plasma) / in urine (dark, tea-coloured).

NADPH

HbA / HbF / HbA₂
Adult hemoglobin α₂β₂ / fetal hemoglobin α₂γ₂ / α₂δ₂, raised in beta thalassemia.
HbS
Sickle hemoglobin — valine for glutamic acid at the 6th codon of beta globin.
HbH / Hb Barts
β₄ (one alpha gene remaining) / γ₄ (no alpha genes; hydrops fetalis). Both are unpartnered-chain tetramers.
HPLC
High performance liquid chromatography — separates hemoglobins for identification; now more common than gel electrophoresis.
BCL11A
The gene that switches fetal hemoglobin off after birth; the target of Casgevy.
Autosplenectomy
Destruction of the spleen by repeated infarction, as in sickle cell disease. Produces functional asplenia.
PNH
Paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria — deficiency of the complement regulators CD55 and CD59, causing intravascular hemolysis and a prothrombotic state.

Transfusion medicine

FFP — fresh frozen plasma
The plasma fraction, frozen. Contains all coagulation, fibrinolytic and complement proteins.
Cryoprecipitate
The cold-insoluble precipitate formed when frozen plasma is thawed at refrigerated temperature. Rich in fibrinogen, factor VIII, von Willebrand factor and factor XIII.
Apheresis
Drawing blood from a donor, separating one component in line, and returning the rest.
Citrate
The anticoagulant in every blood bag. Chelates calcium. A large load during massive transfusion causes hypocalcemia.
Leukocyte reduction
Filtering white cells out of a product. Reduces febrile reactions and HLA alloimmunization.
Irradiation
Damaging the DNA of residual donor lymphocytes so they cannot proliferate. The only prevention for transfusion-associated graft-versus-host disease.
PCC — prothrombin complex concentrate
A concentrated plasma-derived product containing vitamin K-dependent factors. 3-factor and 4-factor forms. Preferred over plasma for urgent warfarin reversal.
IVIG
Intravenous immunoglobulin — pooled, purified human antibody, dispensed from pharmacy.
MTP — massive transfusion protocol
A single activation sending a standard box of all products to the bedside.
ABO / RhD
The major blood group systems. RhD positive means the D antigen is present; about 15% of people are D negative.
HLA — human leukocyte antigen
The tissue type system. Anti-HLA antibodies from parous donors cause TRALI.
Alloimmunization
Forming an antibody against a foreign red cell antigen. Only two routes of exposure: transfusion and pregnancy.
Type and screen / type and cross
Screen = ready (type + antibody screen, no units held). Cross = reserved (units crossmatched and held for that patient).
TRALI
Transfusion-related acute lung injury. Hypotensive, normal JVP, donor anti-HLA antibodies, within 6 hours.
TACO
Transfusion-associated circulatory overload. Hypertensive, raised JVP, responds to diuresis.
GVHD
Graft-versus-host disease. Transfusion-associated form presents 1–6 weeks later with pancytopenia; prevented only by irradiation.
WBIT — wrong blood in tube
The labelling error that causes fatal ABO-incompatible transfusion.
DIC
Disseminated intravascular coagulation — simultaneous widespread clotting and clot breakdown; consumes fibrinogen.

Hemostasis pharmacology

UFH / LMWH
Unfractionated / low-molecular-weight heparin. Both act through antithrombin; chain length determines which factors are reached.
aPTT / PT / INR
Activated partial thromboplastin time (monitors heparin) / prothrombin time / international normalized ratio (monitors warfarin).
HIT
Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia. Antibodies to the heparin–platelet factor 4 complex activate platelets; the danger is thrombosis.
PF4
Platelet factor 4 — the protein heparin binds to form the antigenic complex in HIT.
DOAC
Direct oral anticoagulant — dabigatran and the “-xabans”.
VKORC1
Vitamin K epoxide reductase complex 1 — warfarin's target; genetic variants alter dose requirement.
CYP2C9 / CYP3A4 / CYP2C19
Cytochrome P450 enzymes. 2C9 metabolizes warfarin; 3A4 governs the -xabans and the HIV boosters; 2C19 activates clopidogrel.
P-gp
P-glycoprotein — a drug-transport pump; all three -xabans are substrates.
CrCl
Creatinine clearance — a measure of kidney function used to dose-adjust anticoagulants.
COX / TXA₂
Cyclooxygenase / thromboxane A2 — aspirin irreversibly acetylates COX-1, abolishing thromboxane for the platelet's lifespan.
P2Y12
The platelet ADP receptor blocked by clopidogrel, prasugrel, ticagrelor and cangrelor.
GP IIb/IIIa
Glycoprotein IIb/IIIa — the fibrinogen receptor and the final common step of platelet aggregation.
PAR-1
Protease-activated receptor 1 — the thrombin receptor on the platelet, blocked by vorapaxar.
PDE / cAMP
Phosphodiesterase / cyclic adenosine monophosphate — dipyridamole and cilostazol raise cAMP to keep platelets calm.
tPA
Tissue plasminogen activator — alteplase. Converts plasminogen to plasmin, which degrades fibrin.
TXA
Tranexamic acid — an antifibrinolytic, the mirror image of the “-plase” drugs.
TFPI
Tissue factor pathway inhibitor — a natural anticoagulant blocked by marstacimab and concizumab in hemophilia.
G-CSF / GM-CSF
Granulocyte, and granulocyte-macrophage, colony-stimulating factor. Given as filgrastim and sargramostim.

Sepsis and HIV

SIRS
Systemic Inflammatory Response Syndrome. No longer defining for sepsis but still used by the US Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services.
SOFA / qSOFA
Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (score ≥ 2 ≈ 10% mortality) / quick SOFA (respiratory rate ≥ 22, systolic pressure ≤ 100, altered mentation).
NEWS2 / MEWS
National Early Warning Score, 6 parameters including oxygen saturation / Modified Early Warning Score, 5 parameters, more sensitive.
MODS
Multi-Organ Dysfunction Syndrome — the older term for the end state of septic shock.
PAMPs / DAMPs
Pathogen-Associated / Damage-Associated Molecular Patterns. Viral sepsis works through DAMPs, not PAMPs.
LPS
Lipopolysaccharide — the most prominent gram-negative trigger.
Procalcitonin
The one sepsis marker that points to a cause — elevation indicates a bacterial etiology.
PSS
Post-sepsis syndrome, affecting 50% of survivors.
ART
Antiretroviral therapy. Called HAART in the 1990s.
NRTI / NNRTI / INSTI / PI
Nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitor (competitive, chain-terminating) / non-nucleoside (allosteric) / integrase strand transfer inhibitor / protease inhibitor.
gp120 / gp41
The env products — the binding protein and the fusion protein.
p24
The gag capsid antigen; easiest HIV protein to detect in serum, and the basis of 4th-generation testing.
PIC / RTC
Pre-integration complex / reverse transcription complex — chaperoned into the nucleus by capsid material.
NAT
Nucleic acid test (HIV-1 RNA PCR) — the third tier of the CDC algorithm; a positive result there means acute infection.
ARS
Acute retroviral syndrome — primary HIV infection. Non-exudative pharyngitis is the discriminator from strep.
IRIS
Immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome — paradoxical or unmasking; median 48 days after starting ART.
PJP
Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia. Cholesterol not ergosterol in the cell wall, which is why TMP/SMX and not an azole.
TMP/SMX
Trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole — treatment and prophylaxis for PJP, and prophylaxis for toxoplasmosis.
TDF / TAF
Tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (more nephrotoxic, more bone loss) / tenofovir alafenamide (less).
PrEP / PEP
Pre-exposure prophylaxis (~99% effective for sexual transmission) / post-exposure prophylaxis (start within 72 hours, take for a month).
U = U
Undetectable equals untransmittable — CDC threshold < 200 copies/mL. Evidence: the PARTNER trial.

Hematopoiesis · LO 1 · 76 · 87 · 92 · 93

Sites & hierarchy

  • Yolk sac → fetal liver & spleen → bone marrow. Adult: vertebrae, ribs, sternum, pelvis (axial).
  • HSC = self-renewal + differentiation; multipotent. Over-self-renewal = leukemia.
  • Myeloid: RBC, megakaryocyte→platelet, neutrophil, mono→macrophage, eos, baso. Lymphoid: T (thymus), B (marrow)→plasma cell, NK.
  • Platelet = anucleate membrane fragment → aspirin lasts its 7–10 day life.
  • Extramedullary hematopoiesis + skull/face marrow expansion → frontal bossing in thalassemia.

Hormones → drugs

  • EPO (kidney) → RBC · TPO → platelets · G-CSF/GM-CSF → granulocytes · GH/IGF → lymphocytes.
  • Retic low = production problem. Retic high = loss/destruction.

Marrow biopsy & CBC

  • Prone, posterior superior iliac spine. Aspirate = cytology; core = architecture/cellularity.
  • Hypercellular → leukemia or compensatory drive. Hypocellular → aplastic anemia, MDS, post-chemo.
  • Do NOT biopsy for routine anemia.
  • Hct ≈ 3 × Hgb. Female 37–47%, male 42–52%. RDW ↑ = two populations.
  • Acute hemorrhage: initial Hgb can be normal — concentration unchanged.
Anemia I · LO 7 · 11 · 23 · 25 · 26 · 37 · 50 · 66 · 80

The 3 buckets + the algorithm

  • Blood loss · impaired production · increased destruction.
  • Confirm → read MCV first → retic count → targeted study → smear.
  • <80 micro · 80–100 normo · >100 macro.

Iron deficiency

  • Fe ↓, ferritin ↓, TIBC ↑, TSAT <15%, MCV ↓, marrow iron absent.
  • Stations: duodenum (Fe²⁺ only) → transferrin → cells → ferritin.
  • Adult male or postmenopausal female = GI blood loss until proven otherwise → colonoscopy.
  • Ferritin is an acute phase reactant — can be falsely normal.
  • Small AND pale: extra divisions before maturation.

B12 · pernicious · folate

  • B12 = DNA synthesis + myelin. Folate = DNA only. B12 has the Brain.
  • Megaloblastic: macro-ovalocytes, hypersegmented neutrophils (≥6 lobes), hypercellular marrow, retic low, LDH high = ineffective erythropoiesis.
  • MMA elevated in B12, normal in folate. Homocysteine ↑ in both.
  • PA = autoimmune loss of parietal cells → no IF. Anti-parietal 85–90% (sensitive), anti-IF 75% (specific). Triad: anemia + gastric atrophy (cancer risk) + subacute combined degeneration.
  • Path: pepsin → haptocorrin → pancreatic proteases → IF → terminal ileum → transcobalamin II.
  • Drug causes: metformin (ileum), PPI (stomach).
  • Never folate alone — corrects the count while the cord degenerates.
  • Folate in pregnancy: neural tube closes at ~4 weeks — supplement before conception.

Anemia of inflammation

  • Most common anemia in hospitalized US patients. Ferritin ↑, TIBC ↓.
  • IL-6 → hepcidin ↑ → degrades ferroportin → iron locked in macrophages/enterocytes. Plus ↓EPO, ↓erythroid proliferation, ↑hemophagocytosis.
  • Treat the underlying disease. Oral iron does not work; IV iron bypasses the locked door.

Anemia II · LO 5 · 12 · 32 · 36 · 85 · 88

Intravascular vs extravascular

  • Shared: short lifespan · ↑ unconjugated bilirubin · compensatory retic ↑.
  • Intravascular triad: pink plasma · dark urine · absent haptoglobin. Schistocytes. Kidney at risk — free heme is nephrotoxic.
  • Extravascular: spherocytes, splenomegaly, haptoglobin only mildly low.

Match the cell

  • Ball → spherocytosis · Bite → G6PD · Bull's-eye → thalassemia · Crescent → sickle · Fragment → microangiopathic · Howell-Jolly → no spleen.

The four inherited defects

  • HS — AD, cytoskeleton (ANK1/SPTB/SLC4A1). MCHC ↑, osmotic fragility ↑, Coombs NEGATIVE. Tx: folate, transfusion, splenectomy.
  • G6PD — X-linked; enzyme unstable, so old cells fail. Bite/blister cells, Heinz bodies (supravital stain). Triggers: infection (commonest), sulfa/antimalarials/nitrofurantoin, fava beans. Assay falsely normal during a crisis — repeat at 2–3 months. A− class III (mild) vs Mediterranean class II (severe).
  • Thalassemia — 4 alpha genes (chr 16), 2 beta (chr 11). The unpaired partner is the poison. HbH = β₄ (1 alpha gene); Hb Barts = γ₄ (0 alpha) = hydrops. Beta major presents at 3–6 months when the globin switch happens. Electrophoresis: ↑HbF, ↑HbA₂. Tx: transfusion + chelation.
  • Sickle — glutamic acid → valine, 6th codon, HBB. Deoxy-HbS polymerizes. Disease = HbS 90–100%, HbA absent; trait = 40–45% HbS + HbA.

Sickle — crises & management

  • Aplastic → retic LOW (parvovirus B19). Sequestration → retic high + huge spleen. Vaso-occlusive → Hgb near baseline; dactylitis in infants.
  • Worse with ↑HbS%, ↑concentration (dehydration), acidosis.
  • Hydroxyurea ↑HbF — boxed: myelosuppression + carcinogenic. Penicillin prophylaxis + pneumococcal/meningococcal/Hib. Folic acid. Chronic transfusion to HbS < 30% for stroke prevention. Casgevy (CRISPR, BCL11A) / Lyfgenia (lentiviral beta gene), age ≥12.
  • Fever in a sickle child = emergency (autosplenectomy).

Asplenia

  • Encapsulated organisms: S. pneumoniae, N. meningitidis, H. influenzae b.
  • Prevent: vaccinate (before surgery if planned) · daily penicillin in children · any fever is an emergency.
Transfusion · LO 20 · 38 · 54 · 71

Products & thresholds

  • RBC 1–6 °C, 42 d · Platelets room temp, agitated, 5–7 d · Plasma frozen 1 yr · Cryo = fibrinogen.
  • TRICC: transfuse < 7 g/dL, one unit then reassess. Liberal arm did worse.
  • Platelets: 10k hypoproliferative · 20k lumbar puncture · 25k preterm neonate. Not for ITP/dengue (consumptive).
  • 30% factor activity is enough to clot — ocean vs teacup. INR 1.1 needs nothing.
  • Warfarin reversal: 4F-PCC + vitamin K, not plasma.
  • Citrate chelates calcium → hypocalcemia in massive transfusion.

Immunohematology

  • ABO antibodies are naturally occurring IgM from infant gut flora → fix complement → intravascular. Alloantibodies are IgG from transfusion/pregnancy → extravascular.
  • O = universal RBC donor; AB = universal RBC recipient. Inverts for plasma.
  • 15% RhD negative; O-negative ≈ 6%. Save D-negative for females of childbearing potential.
  • Alloimmunization 1–3% general, 20–60% in sickle cell disease.
  • Direct Coombs = on the cell (diagnosis). Indirect = in the plasma (the screen).
  • Screen = ready. Cross = reserved.

Reactions

  • Steps: stop → keep line open → clerical check → treat → send to blood bank.
  • Febrile non-hemolytic (cytokines; prevent by leukoreduction) · Septic (platelets, room temp) · Acute hemolytic ABO (back/flank pain, pink plasma, DAT+, “never event”, from wrong blood in tube) · Allergic / anaphylaxis (IgA deficiency, washed products).
  • TRALI: hypotensive, normal JVP, donor anti-HLA (14–20% of parous women), <6 h, diuresis no help. TACO: hypertensive, raised JVP, diuresis helps.
  • Transfusion GVHD: pancytopenia 1–6 wk, usually fatal. Prevent by irradiation — also for related donors.
  • Most common infectious risk = bacterially contaminated platelet.
Pharmacology · LO 13 · 14 · 19 · 34 · 55 · 62 · 65 · 68 · 69 · 77

Anticoagulants

  • UFH = long rope (IIa + Xa) · LMWH = short (mostly Xa) · fondaparinux = pentasaccharide (pure Xa, no HIT, no reversal).
  • Protamine: full → partial → none.
  • HIT: platelets fall 5–10 d, >50% drop; danger is thrombosis. Stop all heparin; give argatroban/bivalirudin/fondaparinux. Never warfarin alone (protein C → limb gangrene).
  • Warfarin: VKORC1 → factors II, VII, IX, X + protein C & S (“1972”). Factor VII shortest half-life → INR moves before the patient is anticoagulated → bridge ≥5 days. Teratogenic.
  • ↑INR: amiodarone, metronidazole, TMP/SMX, fluconazole, cipro, acute alcohol binge, gut-flora-killing antibiotics. ↓INR: rifampin, carbamazepine, phenytoin, St John's wort, chronic alcohol, leafy greens. Pharmacogenomics: CYP2C9 + VKORC1.
  • Xabans: Apixaban all-round safest · Rivaroxaban requires a meal · Edoxaban avoid if CrCl >95.
  • Reversal: protamine · vitamin K + 4F-PCC · idarucizumab (dabigatran) · 4F-PCC (xabans) · nothing (fondaparinux).

Antiplatelets & fibrinolytics

  • White clot (arterial) = antiplatelet. Red clot (venous) = anticoagulant.
  • Aspirin irreversibly acetylates COX-1 for the platelet's 7–10 day life; low dose is the antiplatelet dose.
  • Clopidogrel = prodrug, CYP2C19 (avoid omeprazole; use pantoprazole) · prasugrel contraindicated after stroke/TIA · ticagrelor reversible, dyspnea, aspirin ≤100 mg · cangrelor IV, minutes.
  • GP IIb/IIIa (eptifibatide, tirofiban): thrombocytopenia + worst bleeding. Vorapaxar: absolute contraindication with prior stroke/TIA/ICH. Cilostazol: boxed — contraindicated in heart failure.
  • -plase = plasminogen activator. Tenecteplase = single bolus, now approved for stroke. Catastrophic bleed → cryoprecipitate + tranexamic acid.

Anemia drugs

  • Oral iron: Fe²⁺ only, acidic duodenum; avoid enteric-coated/SR; alternate-day dosing; retic bump 7–10 d; continue 3–6 months past normalization.
  • IV iron: premedicate with methylprednisolone, NOT diphenhydramine. Ferric carboxymaltose → hypophosphatemia (FGF23) + heart failure indication.
  • Iron toxicity: acute → deferoxamine. Chronic overload → deferasirox (renal/hepatic/GI boxed), deferiprone (agranulocytosis), deferoxamine (8–12 h infusions, ocular/auditory).
  • ESAs: stimulate erythroid progenitor division/differentiation + retic release. IV or SC (SC needs 15–30% less). Approved: CKD + chemotherapy anemia. 4 boxed warnings, all at Hgb > 11 g/dL; cut the dose if it rises >1 g/dL in 2 weeks. HTN in up to ⅓. Replete iron first.
  • Leucovorin bypasses DHFR — the rescue for methotrexate. Luspatercept unblocks late maturation in thalassemia.
  • Complement inhibitors (PNH) → boxed meningococcal warning, vaccinate first.
Sepsis & HIV · LO 16 · 28 · 30 · 31 · 33 · 73 · 79

Sepsis

  • Dysregulated host response. Cultures negative in 30–50%. 1.7 M cases, ~350k deaths, $62 B/yr.
  • SOFA ≥ 2 ≈ 10% mortality. qSOFA: RR ≥22, SBP ≤100, altered mentation. NEWS2 = 6 params (adds O₂ sat); MEWS = 5, more sensitive. SIRS criteria are bidirectional.
  • Source order: respiratory > urinary > abdominal. 20% polymicrobial. Abdominal → B. fragilis.
  • G(−) → LPS · G(+) → lipoteichoic acid + peptidoglycan · fungal → β-glucans/mannans/chitin · viral → DAMPs.
  • Inflammation → endothelial dysfunction + ischemia → sequential organ failure → necrosis, necroptosis, pyroptosis, autophagy.
  • Procalcitonin = the one cause-specific marker (bacterial). Bundle: bactericidal antibiotic, IV fluids, lactate, assess perfusion, vasopressors + source control.
  • Mortality 10–52%; post-sepsis syndrome in 50% of survivors; 90% survival if young/healthy.

HIV

  • pol → RT (high error rate), integrase, protease · gag → p24 (easiest to detect), p17, p6/7/9 · env → gp120 (binds CD4/CCR5/CXCR4), gp41 (fusion). gag/pol cleaved by viral protease; env by host protease.
  • Cycle → drug: attachment/fusion (fostemsavir gp120, maraviroc CCR5, enfuvirtide gp41, ibalizumab post-attachment) · RT (NRTI competitive/chain-terminating; NNRTI allosteric — one mutation kills the class; lenacapavir capsid) · integration (INSTI) · maturation (PI — resistance uncommon). Cobicistat is not an antiretroviral.
  • Testing: Ag/Ab combo with p24 → antibody differentiation → NAT (positive = acute). Eclipse period day 0–10 detects nothing. Western blot obsolete.
  • CD4 stages; viral load transmits. AIDS = CD4 <200 (or <14%) OR an AIDS-defining OI. OI risk greatest <50. ALC <1,000 predicts CD4 <200.
  • Thresholds: <200 PJP/Cryptococcus/Candida · <100 toxoplasmosis (ring-enhancing) · <50 CMV, Cryptococcus, MAC, CNS lymphoma.
  • PJP: cholesterol not ergosterol → TMP/SMX. Steroids if PaO₂ <70 or A–a ≥35. Start ART within 2 weeks.
  • IRIS: 10–20%, median 48 days, improving CD4/viral load with clinical worsening. Do not delay ART.
  • Prophylaxis: TMP/SMX <200 (PJP) and <100 (toxo). Live vaccines only if CD4 ≥ 200.
  • Boosters (ritonavir, cobicistat) inhibit CYP3A4 → OTC nasal steroid → iatrogenic Cushing.
  • U = U, CDC < 200 copies/mL. PARTNER trial: 74,000 episodes, zero transmissions. PrEP ~99% sexual; PEP within 72 hours.
